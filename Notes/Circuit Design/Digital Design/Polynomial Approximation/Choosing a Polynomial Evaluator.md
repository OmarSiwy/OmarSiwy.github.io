# Choosing a Polynomial Evaluator

Every circuit in this folder turns the same one knob — how much of the answer comes out of a $2^{\alpha}$-entry ROM and how much out of $d$ multipliers — so the choice is decided by which of the two resources you have spare, and then, at equal cost, by depth.

> [!warning] Chapter 18 reports no synthesis numbers at all
> Unlike Chap. 17, this chapter gives no LUT, slice, DSP, BRAM or delay figure for any of its architectures, and no cycle counts — every architecture here is presented combinationally. The columns below are therefore **table bits, operand widths, multiplier counts and logic depth in multiply-add delays**, either quoted from the printed page or computed from the book's own Eqs. (18.3)–(18.31) and Algorithms 18.1–18.2. Rows marked *computed* are derived in `4.3) Polynomial Approximation` and in the sibling note, not measured; every `—` below is a quantity the source genuinely does not give.

Three quantities a synthesis report would give and this table cannot: **LUT/slice counts, DSP-block counts, and delay in nanoseconds**. There are no cycle counts either — every architecture in Ch. 18 is drawn combinationally, so "depth" is multiply-add delays, not pipeline stages. Two rows carry `—`-grade gaps on accuracy specifically: the [[Parallel Polynomial Evaluator]] has no closed-form $\bar\delta_{\text{eval}}$ anywhere in the chapter (p. 561), and [[HOTBM]]'s index-truncation error is deferred to [DD05] (p. 560), so neither can be sized from this book alone.

**Memory is the constraint, and it is settled by the degree, not by the segmentation.** The coefficient table is the only exponential resource in the operator; everything else is linear in $d$ and polynomial in the widths. Since $\alpha\approx w_R/(d+1)$, the marginal exponent saved by one more degree is $\frac{w_R}{(d+1)(d+2)}$ — quadratically diminishing — while each degree costs one more multiplier and one more stage of depth forever (p. 542). The sweep above is that asymmetry in numbers: the first multiplier is worth a factor $21$ on the table, the second only $3.1$, the third $2.7$, and the multiplier area meanwhile climbs monotonically. **Take $d=2$ or $d=3$ with [[Uniform Piecewise Segmentation]] as the default**, which is where every published evaluator sits (p. 560), and treat $\alpha$ as the output rather than the input: pick the memory you are willing to spend, and let it fix $d$. The knob is coarse enough that trial and error settles it in seconds. If even $d=2$ will not fit, the escape is not a cleverer evaluator but a different family — at $w_X\lesssim16$ multipartite is multiplier-free with an airtight error analysis, and on the book's own 16-bit $\sin$ example it needs $7808$ table bits automatically or $6912$ tuned (Ch. 17, pp. 520–521) against a degree-1 evaluator's $7936$. That tie is structural: **multipartite *is* a degree-1 polynomial evaluator whose product $C_1(A)\cdot Y$ has been replaced by tables of its partial products**, so both are stuck in the $2^{w/2}$ regime, and degree 2 at $1408$ bits is what breaks out of it. See [[Multipartite Methods]] and [[Table-Based Function Evaluation]] for the other side of that comparison.

**Latency is the constraint, and the prologue usually costs more than the evaluator.** [[Horner Evaluation]] is $\Theta(d)$ deep, and its rivals attack that from two ends. At the evaluator, [[Parallel Polynomial Evaluator]] buys $O(\log d)$ for roughly double the multipliers — but at the $d\le3$ hardware actually builds, that is $2$ delays against $3$ for twice the arithmetic, and [[Estrin's Scheme]] reaches the same depth for only $\lceil\log_2(d+1)\rceil-1$ extra operators, all of them squarers. The book is blunt that Estrin "is useful only for large degrees, which probably explains why it has not been used in hardware" (p. 562), and the reason is the memory law above: hardware never gets to the degree where the tree pays. Estrin also forfeits the single bit heap, which is the parallel form's real argument — each subtree value is a multiplier *operand*, and a multiplier needs a resolved number, not a heap. At the front of the datapath the numbers are larger and less often counted: [[Logarithmic Segmentation]] puts an LZC and a barrel shifter ahead of the table read where uniform segmentation has the address at the input pins; [[Hierarchical Segmentation]] puts **two dependent memory reads** plus a shift there; [[Dichotomy-Based Segmentation]] puts $\lceil\log_2 N\rceil$ comparator delays plus a subtraction there. Below a few dozen segments that search is a significant fraction of a whole Horner chain. **If latency binds, spend on the table and keep the reduction a wire split** — a larger $\alpha$ with uniform segmentation and, only then, a parallel evaluator. And if the design is pipelined to one result per cycle, depth amortises into stages and this entire paragraph stops applying: only area and registers matter, and Horner wins both.

**Accuracy is the constraint in exactly two situations, and they pull opposite ways.** The first is a function that misbehaves at an endpoint — a discontinuity, an infinite derivative, or a derivative varying by orders of magnitude across $I$ — where uniform segmentation sets $d$ from the hardest subinterval and wastes it on every other. Then [[Logarithmic Segmentation]] earns its LZC, its shifter and its unreduced $w_Y$; if the evaluator's multipliers are the budget, that unreduced $w_Y$ eats the saving and the answer is LU [[Hierarchical Segmentation]], whose tabulated $\alpha_2(A_1)$ is the knob logarithmic segmentation structurally lacks. When the generator must swallow arbitrary user functions — most inverse cumulative distributions have no usable identity — [[Dichotomy-Based Segmentation]] is optimal by construction, since every other scheme is a feasible point of the same balancing problem. Before reaching for any of it, **try the function's own algebra**: $\sqrt{2^{2p}x}=2^{p}\sqrt{x}$ collapses the square root to two segments (p. 553), and [[Addition-Theorem Coefficient Generation]] collapses four sine tables into one for three constant multipliers. The second situation is the opposite one — the target is correct rounding rather than faithful — and there [[ILP-Optimized Polynomial Evaluator]] is the only member of the family that delivers it, at $w_X\le24$, $\alpha=7$ and degree $\le2$, because it enumerates every input value and its constraint count is $2\cdot2^{w_X-\alpha}$ per subinterval. Note that its scaling runs *against* the degree knob: a higher $d$ means a smaller $\alpha$ means a larger $w_X-\alpha$ in the exponent. Two circuits are here mainly to be ruled out. [[Function-Value Interpolation]] is a $1.06\times$ table saving at $d=1$ bought with a subtractor, an incrementer, a swap mux and an extra adder delay, and beyond $d=1$ its table grows *faster* than the one it was replacing — take it only when the samples are not yours to precompute (a calibration LUT, a shared sine ROM). [[SquareRich Evaluation]] trades a multiplier for a cheaper squarer, but after a uniform reduction $C_2\ll C_1$ forces $m=C_1/2C_2\gg x$, so the squarer runs on a much wider word than the multiplier it replaced — the trade turns against it exactly where $\alpha$ is large, which is the regime the memory law pushes you into.

**FPGA and ASIC disagree on three of these rows.** On an FPGA the resource classes map cleanly onto the structure — the wide, shallow coefficient ROM into block RAM (or, for $\alpha\le6$, into $W$ LUTs and no BRAM at all), the $d$ multipliers of decreasing width into DSP blocks with the deep narrow stages staying in soft logic, every addition into one compressor tree — and that changes three verdicts. [[HOTBM]] gets much better, because a $2^{\alpha-t+w_k}$-word table with $\alpha-t+w_k\le6$ is *one LUT per output bit*, whereas on ASIC those ROMs are synthesised logic and keeping the multiplier wins. [[Addition-Theorem Coefficient Generation]] gets one-sided, because its second read port is the block RAM's idle B port and its constant multipliers are CSD adder networks in spare LUTs, while on ASIC dual-porting an SRAM is roughly a doubling of bit-cell area. [[Estrin's Scheme]] gets *worse*: Horner's chain is exactly the shape DSP slices are cascaded for, so the dedicated accumulator route between adjacent blocks carries the partial sum with no general routing, while every edge of Estrin's tree is general interconnect — the asymptotics still favour the tree, but the crossover degree moves up, the same reversal the [[Adders]] asymptotics undergo once a carry chain is hardened. Pulling the other way, [[Dichotomy-Based Segmentation]]'s flat comparator bank is affordable on ASIC and expensive on FPGA, where an $\alpha$-bit comparison costs about as much as an addition, so the $\lceil\log_2 N\rceil$-deep dichotomic form is the better mapping there. And if the interface is floating-point, take [[Floating-Point Polynomial Function Evaluator]] before designing any segmentation at all: the exponent field is already $\lfloor\log_2|X|\rfloor$, so the logarithmic first level of an LU hierarchy is free, and unlike its fixed-point twin it keeps $w_Y=w_F-\alpha$ unconditionally.

**Related Notes**

- [[Polynomial Coefficient Table]] – depends-on: the only exponential resource in the operator, and therefore the thing every row above is really trading against.
- [[Uniform Piecewise Segmentation]] – compares: the default reduction, free in hardware, and the baseline the other three are measured against.
- [[Logarithmic Segmentation]] – compares: the one reduction that survives a singularity at $0$, for an LZC, a shifter and an unreduced $w_Y$.
- [[Hierarchical Segmentation]] – extends: restores the missing $\alpha$ knob to logarithmic segmentation, at two dependent lookups of latency.
- [[Dichotomy-Based Segmentation]] – compares: optimal partition by construction, paid for with a comparator search in front of the evaluator.
- [[Function-Value Interpolation]] – counter-example: the coefficient supply that scales backwards with degree, worth having only when the samples are written at run time.
- [[Addition-Theorem Coefficient Generation]] – extends: what a known function's algebra buys over the generic table — four tables into one, for three constant multipliers.
- [[Horner Evaluation]] – compares: minimal multiplier count and 26% of their area removed by per-stage truncation, at $O(d)$ depth.
- [[Parallel Polynomial Evaluator]] – compares: $O(\log d)$ depth and one bit heap, for roughly double the multipliers and no published error model.
- [[HOTBM]] – extends: the table-based realisation of a parallel term, and the one that maps best onto LUT fabric.
- [[Estrin's Scheme]] – compares: the cheapest point on the latency curve, at degrees hardware never reaches.
- [[SquareRich Evaluation]] – counter-example: a multiplier traded for a squarer, defeated by the very range reduction the rest of the family depends on.
- [[ILP-Optimized Polynomial Evaluator]] – extends: the only route to correct rather than faithful rounding, bounded to 24 bits and degree 2.
- [[Floating-Point Polynomial Function Evaluator]] – extends: wraps any row above in exponent handling and gets the logarithmic segmentation for nothing.
- [[Remez Minimax]] – depends-on: the two scaling laws, $\bar\delta_{\text{approx}}\propto h^{d+1}$ and $\approx3$ bits per degree, from which every $\alpha$ in this note follows.
- [[Multipartite Methods]] – counter-example: a degree-1 evaluator with the product replaced by tables; the answer below about 16 bits.
