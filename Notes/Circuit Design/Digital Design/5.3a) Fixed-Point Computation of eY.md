**Specification.** Input: the reduced argument $Y$ in `sfix`$(-1,\,-w_F-g)$ with $g\ge3$, produced by the range reduction of [[5.3) Floating-Point Exponential]]. Output: $M$, a fixed-point approximation to $e^Y\in[0.65,1.65]$ — MSB at position 0, so `ufix`$(0,\,-w_F-g)$. Accuracy: $\bar\delta_{\mathrm{arch}} = |M-e^Y|$ under $1-2u$ where $u=2^{-w_F-g}$, which is what closes the parent operator's 1-ulp faithfulness budget. Optimized: **table bits and DSP-block count**, since the arithmetic here is trivially pipelinable and latency is rarely the constraint.

Write $\ell = -w_F-g$ throughout. This note is §22.5 of de Dinechin & Kumm, split out of [[5.3) Floating-Point Exponential]]; the error terms it produces are consumed by that note's [[5.3) Floating-Point Exponential#Overall error analysis]].

**For very small formats, tabulate $e^Y$ directly.** BFloat has $w_F=7$; with $g=3$, $Y$ is 10 bits and the table is $2^{10}\times 11$ bits — more than four times smaller than the $2^{12}\times15$-bit table of the whole-operator direct tabulation (p. 655). Whether it *wins* is unclear, though: add the range-reduction hardware in front of it and direct tabulation may still be cheaper, and direct tabulation is **correctly rounded** while this is not.

## Using large tables and square multipliers

A **second** range reduction, this time multiplicative-free, splitting $Y$ by bit position (p. 655):

$$Y = A + Z \qquad (22.19)$$

$A$ = the $k$ MSBs of $Y$ (positions $-1$ down to $-k$); $Z$ = the rest (positions $-k-1$ down to $\ell$). By (22.1),

$$e^Y = e^{A+Z} = e^A\cdot e^Z \qquad (22.20)$$

$e^A$ is a $2^k$-entry table. $Z$ is small:

$$0\le Z < 2^{-k} \qquad (22.21)$$

so $e^Z$ comes from Taylor (22.3), whose first three coefficients $1,1,\tfrac12$ are **powers of two, hence free shifts** (p. 656):

$$e^Z\approx 1 + Z + \frac{Z^2}{2}+\cdots \qquad (22.22)$$

Now two rewritings that carry the whole architecture. Define

$$f(Z) = e^Z-1 \qquad (22.23) \qquad\qquad h(Z) = e^Z - 1 - Z \qquad (22.24)$$

Taylor with $0\le Z<2^{-k}$ bounds them:

$$0\le f(Z) < 2^{-k+1} \qquad (22.25) \qquad\qquad 0\le h(Z) < 2^{-2k} \qquad (22.26)$$

so the MSB of $f$ is at position $-k$ and the MSB of $h$ at $-2k-1$.

**First rewriting.** $e^Z = 1+f(Z)$ has at least $k-1$ zeros between its leading one and the next nonzero bit. Multiplying by that string of zeros is wasted work, so distribute:

$$e^A\times e^Z = e^A\times(1+f(Z)) = e^A + e^A\times f(Z) \qquad (22.27)$$

by distributivity of $\times$ over $+$. Both addends align at LSB $\ell$, and **the multiplier shrinks by $k$ bits on the $e^Z$ side.**

**Second rewriting.** By (22.24),

$$f(Z) = Z + h(Z) \qquad (22.28)$$

so the only thing that needs a function evaluator is $h$ — and by (22.26) $h$ is **$2k$ bits smaller** than $e^Z$. All the transcendental difficulty has been squeezed into a $2k$-bits-narrower box; $Z$ itself is added back for free.

*After Fig. 22.6, p. 657. $\ell=-w_F-g$; $g'$ = guard bits internal to the $e^Y$ operator ($g'=0$ recovers the single-global-$g$ style); $t$ = truncation of the $e^A$ table feeding the multiplier; $p$ = LSB of $Z_{\mathrm{trunc}}$.*

Both truncation parameters exist for the same reason. $T_{\mathrm{trunc}}$ exists because a **truncated multiplier** wants its two inputs to be roughly the same size ([[2.4.3) Truncated Multipliers]], [[2.4) Multipliers]]), and $F$ is $k$ bits shorter than $T$ — so shorten $T$ to match, producing a near-**square** multiplier. $Z_{\mathrm{trunc}}$ exists because $H$'s MSB is at $-2k-1$ and its LSB at $\ell-g'$, so $H$ is only $-2k-\ell+g'$ bits wide and does not need all of $Z$ to be computed.

**Error analysis** (pp. 658–659), telescoping from output back to input with $u=2^{\ell-g'}$:

$$\delta_{\mathrm{arch}} = \delta_{\text{final round}} + \delta_{\mathrm{mult}} + \delta_{\mathrm{trunc}T} + \delta_H + \delta_{\mathrm{trunc}Z} + 0 + \delta_T \qquad (22.29)$$

| Term | Source | Bound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\delta_{\text{final round}}$ | dropping the $g'$ guard bits | $0$ if $g'=0$; else $2^{\ell-1}$ — **free** if the rounding bit $2^{\ell-1}$ is pre-added to every $e^A$ table entry |
| $\delta_{\mathrm{mult}}$ | the truncated multiplier | $1u$ (faithful) |
| $\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}T}$ | $(T_{\mathrm{trunc}}-T)(Z+H)$ | $2^{\ell+t-g'}\times 1.25\cdot 2^{-k}$; balancing against $u$ gives $t=k\Rightarrow 1.25u$, or $t=k-1\Rightarrow 0.625u$ |
| $\delta_H$ | the $h(Z)$ evaluator, amplified by $T$ | $1.65u/2$ if $h$ is tabulated with correct rounding; $1.65u$ if $h$ comes from a faithful generic approximator |
| $\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}Z}$ | $T(h(Z_{\mathrm{trunc}})-h(Z))$ | Taylor on $h$: $\approx e\,h'(Z) = eZ+\cdots$ with $0\le e<2^p$ and $|Z|<2^{-k}$, so $2\cdot 2^{p-k}$; $p=\ell-g'+k-1\Rightarrow 1u$ |
| $0$ | $T(1+Z+h(Z))-Te^Z$ | **exactly zero** — $h$ is *defined* as $e^Z-Z-1$, so the decomposition is algebraically exact and its approximation error is already counted in $\delta_H$ |
| $\delta_T$ | tabulation of $e^A$, amplified by $e^Z$ | $\tfrac{u}{2}\times1.25 = 0.625u$ with round-to-nearest |

Note the $1.65$ appearing as an amplification factor: it is the upper bound on $T\approx e^A$. When $e^Y<1$ — which is exactly the branch where the outer analysis had no slack — we also have $e^A\le e^Y<1$ by monotonicity of $e^x$ and $A\le Y$, so the bound tightens to $T<1$ where it matters most.

Summing: $\bar\delta_{\mathrm{arch}}$ lands **between $3u$ and $6u$** depending on the choices. Two concrete settlements (p. 659):

- **$g'=0$, small precision, $h$ tabulated correctly rounded**: $p=\ell+k-1$, $t=k$ gives $0+1+1.25+0.825+1+0.625 = 4.7u$.
- **$g'=0$, larger precision, $h$ faithful**: needs $t=k-1$ to compensate, giving $0+1+0.625+1.65+1+0.625 = 4.9u$.

Both sit just under $5u$, so with the $3u$ contributed by the floating-point range reduction, **$g=4$**. Holding $g$ at its minimal 3 instead requires $\delta_{\mathrm{mult}}+\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}T}+\delta_H+\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}Z}+\delta_T < 4u$, achievable with $g'=3$. That is the trade in one sentence: **more work in the floating-point range reduction, or more work inside $e^Y$.**

## A first-order architecture

For small precisions, drop $h$ entirely: $f(Z)\approx Z$ (p. 659). The approximation error is the whole neglected tail:

$$\delta_{\mathrm{approx},1}(Z) = 1+Z-e^Z = -\frac{Z^2}{2}-\frac{Z^3}{6}-\cdots \qquad (22.30)$$

Its magnitude is slightly above the leading term $Z^2/2 < 2^{-2k-1}$, giving

$$\bar\delta_{\mathrm{approx},1} = 2^{-2k-1}\left(1+2^{-k}\right) \qquad (22.31)$$

where $1+2^{-k}$ is a gross overestimate of the tail sum. This is worth doing when $2^{-2k-1}\approx 2^{\ell}$, i.e.

$$k\approx\frac{\ell+1}{2},\qquad\text{and with } g=3,\quad k\ge\frac{w_F+4}{2}$$

The architecture is Fig. 22.7: $e^A$ table $\to$ $T_{\mathrm{trunc}}$, one multiplier $T_{\mathrm{trunc}}\times Z$, one adder, truncate. No $h$ box, no $Z_{\mathrm{trunc}}$, and $g=3$ suffices.

- **binary16** ($w_F=10$): $Y$ is $w_F+3=13$ bits, $\ell=-13$, $k=7$, $Z$ is 6 bits, $e^A$ truncated to LSB $\ell+k-1=-7$. **Multiplier: $6\times8$ bits.** Trivial.
- **IEEE single** ($w_F=23$): $k=14$, multiplier $13\times12$ — but the $e^A$ table now has $2^{14}$ entries. **Use the higher-order architecture instead**; the $h$ box is cheaper than the $2^7\times$ table growth.

## Polynomial approximation for large precisions

For large $w_F$, replace the $h$ box with the generic piecewise-polynomial evaluator ([[4.3) Polynomial Approximation]], [[4.3.1) Remez Minimax]], [[4.3.3) Horner Evaluation]]) used as a black box on

$$h:[0,1]\to[0,1],\qquad h(x) = e^{2^{-k}x} - 2^{-k}x - 1$$

with input and output precisions read off the diagram above and a degree $d$ chosen for faithfulness (p. 660). Now **two** parameters set the whole trade-off: $k$ fixes the $e^A$ table, $d$ fixes the tables-versus-multipliers split inside the polynomial evaluator. For double precision a sensible pair is $k=9$, $d=2$, needing $2^9=512$ intervals in the piecewise approximation — which shows the method scales well to large precisions. Since it is again tables plus multipliers, it maps cleanly onto heterogeneous FPGAs. FloPoCo's `FPExp` picks defaults and exposes both in expert mode.

## FPGA-specific remarks

Embedded memories implement the $e^A$ table; DSP blocks implement the multipliers of both architectures **and the surrounding adders** ([[1.3.2) DSP Blocks]], [[1.3.3) Block Memory]]). Three places where the coarse granularity of hard blocks is *free accuracy* rather than waste (p. 661):

- **$t$ can be reduced for free** as long as $T_{\mathrm{trunc}}$ still fits one DSP input — reducing $\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}T}$ at zero cost.
- **The $e^A$ table output can be widened for free** up to the memory's maximum output width.
- **If the multiplier fits a single DSP block**, the product is *exact*, and can then be rounded to nearest — killing $\delta_{\mathrm{mult}}$. The half-ulp addition this needs merely widens the final adder by one bit.

**Case study, IEEE single precision** ($w_F=23$), with $g'=0$ and $g=4$, so $\ell=-27$, choosing $k=10$, $p=\ell+k-1=-18$, $t=k=10$, with $h(Z)$ tabulated with correct rounding:

- Both multiplier inputs are **18 bits** — supported by every common DSP block. On asymmetric DSPs (Xilinx $25\times18$) the truncation of $T_{\mathrm{trunc}}$ can be *relaxed*, improving accuracy for free.
- The $e^A$ table is $2^{10}\times28$ bits and the $h(Z)$ table is $2^8\times6$ bits. **Both fit in a single 36 Kbit block RAM** used in $2^{10}\times36$ dual-port mode.

One BRAM, one DSP, plus the range-reduction logic, for a faithful single-precision exponential.

## Iterative computation of $e^Y$ using small tables and rectangular multipliers

A different family entirely (pp. 662–663), built on precomputed pairs of reals $(E_i^\dagger, L_i^\dagger)$ with $E_i^\dagger = e^{L_i^\dagger}$. From seeds $E_0=e^{L_0}$, iterate

$$\forall i>0\quad\begin{cases} L_{i+1} = L_i + L_i^\dagger\\ E_{i+1} = E_i\times E_i^\dagger\end{cases} \qquad (22.32)$$

**Invariant: $E_i = e^{L_i}$**, since $E_{i+1}=E_i^\dagger E_i = e^{L_i^\dagger}e^{L_i}=e^{L_i^\dagger+L_i}=e^{L_{i+1}}$ by (22.1). One addition and one multiplication per step, and the $E_i^\dagger$ are chosen to fit in few bits so the multiplication stays cheap. The same recurrence computes logarithms by running it in the other direction — drive $E_i\to1$ instead of $L_i\to0$.

For the exponential of $Y\in[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12]$: seed $L_0=Y$ and drive $L_i\to 0$ until $e^{L_n}-1$ is cheap by table or Taylor. Each iteration takes the $\alpha_i$ **leading** bits of $L_i$ as a subword $A_i$ addressing two tables:

- $X_i^\dagger = E_i^\dagger - 1$, an approximation to $e^{L_i}-1$ built from $A_i$ alone, rounded to $\alpha_i$ bits;
- $L_i^\dagger = \ln(E_i^\dagger)$, rounded to LSB $\ell$.

By construction $L_i^\dagger$ is close to $L_i$, so

$$L_{i+1} = L_i - L_i^\dagger \qquad (22.33)$$

**cancels the $\alpha_i-1$ most significant bits of $L_i$**. Since $L_i$ and $L_i^\dagger$ share the same LSB $\ell$, the number fed to the next iteration is $\alpha_i-1$ bits shorter: the datapath narrows monotonically.

Reconstruction runs (22.32) with *decreasing* $i$, but with a change of variable — because $E_i\to1$, storing $E_i$ wastes the leading one. Store $X^\dagger = E^\dagger-1$ and compute $X_i = E_i-1$. Expanding $E_i = E_{i+1}E_i^\dagger$ as $(1+X_i)=(1+X_{i+1})(1+X_i^\dagger)$ and cancelling the 1:

$$X_i = X_{i+1}\times X_i^\dagger + X_{i+1} + X_i^\dagger \qquad (22.34)$$

with $e^{L_0}\approx X_0+1$ at the end. $X_i^\dagger$ is only $\alpha_i$ bits wide, so each step needs a **rectangular** multiplier, and the *cumulated* size of all of them is only slightly larger than one single full multiplier ([[2.4) Multipliers]]).

*The word split at the top is Fig. 22.8, p. 663; the surrounding dataflow is my own construction from (22.32)–(22.34), pp. 662–663. Every $L_i$ and $L_i^\dagger$ shares the LSB $\ell$, so the subtraction is width-aligned and $L_{i+1}$ is $\alpha_i-1$ bits narrower than $L_i$; $X_i^\dagger$ stays $\alpha_i$ bits wide at every step, which is what keeps each multiplier rectangular.*

**Where it wins and where it loses.** On LUT-only FPGAs this is an excellent match: set $\alpha_i$ to the LUT input width and the tables cost one LUT level each. Larger $\alpha_i$ means bigger tables and fewer iterations — a clean knob. On **modern** DSP-and-BRAM FPGAs it is a poor match in both directions: sized for LUTs, the rectangular multipliers underuse the DSP blocks; sized to fill the DSPs, the $2^{\alpha_i}$ tables explode. Prefer the previous sections there.

On ASIC the bottleneck is different: the additions on the $L_i$ datapath (22.33) are in the critical path, so implementations go to **high-radix redundant arithmetic** ([[1.1.2) Redundant Number Systems]]) to break the carry chains [Erc73; PEB03; PEB04].

**Picking.** Tabulate $e^Y$ outright below about 8 fraction bits — one small memory beats every architecture here, and it is the only one that is correctly rounded. Take the **first-order** architecture where $k\approx(\ell+1)/2$ still keeps $2^k$ under a few hundred entries, i.e. binary16 and neighbours; past that the $e^A$ table grows as $2^{w_F/2}$ and kills it. Take the **higher-order** architecture from single precision upward, where the $2k$-bit shrinkage of the $h$ box pays for the second table — on FPGA it lands on exactly one BRAM and one DSP. Swap $h$ for a **piecewise polynomial** at double and beyond, where $k$ and $d$ give you two independent knobs instead of one. Take the **iterative** architecture only on LUT-only fabrics, or on ASIC with high-radix redundant adders; on a DSP-and-BRAM FPGA it underuses both.
