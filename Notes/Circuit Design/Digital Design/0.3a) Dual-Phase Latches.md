**Specification.** Input: a combinational pipeline of $N$ stages with per-stage delays $d_1\dots d_N$ on an $n$-bit datapath. Output: bit-identical to the same pipeline clocked by edge-triggered flops — this is a *clocking discipline*, not an arithmetic transform, so the arithmetic error is exactly $0$ ulp and none of the rounding machinery in [[1.2) Error Accuracy]] applies. What is being optimised is **cycle time** (throughput), by removing the per-stage $\max$ from the timing constraint. The costs paid are min-delay margin, clock power, area and design-flow friction, and every claim below is judged against that trade.

## The two-phase scheme

Two non-overlapping clocks $\phi_1,\phi_2$ of period $T$, each high for $W$, separated by a dead gap $t_{\mathrm{nov}}$ on both edges:

$$\phi_1 \text{ high on } [0,\,W],\qquad \phi_2 \text{ high on } [T/2,\,T/2+W],\qquad W=\tfrac{T}{2}-t_{\mathrm{nov}}$$

Latches are placed on a half-period grid and alternate phase: L1, L2, L1, L2… Latch $i$ opens at $t_i=(i-1)T/2$ and closes at $t_i+W$. Because it is level-sensitive, its output during the open window is a *wire*, not a register:

$$Q_i(t)=\begin{cases} D_i(t-t_{dq}) & t\in[t_i,\,t_i+W]\quad\text{(transparent)}\\[2pt] Q_i(t_i+W) & \text{otherwise}\quad\text{(opaque)}\end{cases}$$

That transparency window is the whole point and it is *not* an accident of the cell — a master–slave flop is exactly two of these latches in series clocked on opposite phases, so the half-cycle of transparency has always existed inside every flop. Edge-triggered design simply throws it away by placing the master and slave at the same physical point, where the closed slave immediately re-blocks anything the open master let through. Two-phase design keeps the pair but *separates* it in space, putting logic between L1 and L2. See [[0.3) Introduction to Flip-Flops & Latches]] for the cell-level construction.

The two-phase discipline is a colouring rule: every signal is $\phi_1$-derived or $\phi_2$-derived, no latch may have input and output of the same colour, and no combinational cone may mix colours. Pedroso et al. verify exactly these three rules as a graph two-colouring on the placed netlist, and note the check collapses to a single condition — for every latch-to-latch edge $(u,v)$ in the latch graph, $\mathrm{colour}(u)\ne\mathrm{colour}(v)$.

## Time borrowing / slack passing

Let $A_i$ be the arrival time at latch $i$'s D pin and $D_i=d_i+t_{dq}$ fold the latch delay into the segment. Level-sensitivity gives the recurrence

$$A_{i+1}=\max\big(t_i,\;A_i\big)+D_{i+1}$$

The $\max$ is the entire mechanism: **early data waits for the opening edge; late data walks straight through.** Setup at latch $i$ requires the data stable $t_{su}$ before the *closing* edge, derated by skew $t_{sk}$:

$$A_i\;\le\;t_i+\underbrace{W-t_{su}-t_{sk}}_{\textstyle B}$$

so the borrow $b_i=\max(0,A_i-t_i)$ is capped at $B=\tfrac{T}{2}-t_{\mathrm{nov}}-t_{su}-t_{sk}$.

Unrolling the recurrence — the max is taken over the last latch that was *not* borrowing — gives $A_i=\max_{j\le i}\big(t_{j-1}+\sum_{k=j}^{i}D_k\big)$. Substituting the grid $t_i=(i-1)T/2$ and cancelling collapses the whole system to one family of inequalities, with $L=i-j+1$ the length of the run:

$$\boxed{\;\sum_{k=j}^{i}D_k\;\le\;L\cdot\frac{T}{2}+B\;}\qquad\text{for every }1\le j\le i\le N$$

Divide by $L$:

$$\overline{D}_{[j,i]}\;\le\;\frac{T}{2}+\frac{B}{L}$$

> [!important] This is the whole argument, and it is weaker than the folklore
> The constraint is on the **running average over every window**, not the maximum. One slow stage is fine; $L$ consecutive slow stages are fine as long as their *total* excess over the nominal grid stays within a single $B$. Borrowing accumulates along a run and is repaid by later fast stages — but the accumulated debt is bounded by $B$, not by $L\cdot B$. "Only the average matters" is true asymptotically ($B/L\to0$) and false for a short pipeline with a large local hump.

Contrast the flop constraint of [[0.3) Introduction to Flip-Flops & Latches]]:

$$\max_i\big(d_i+t_{cq}+t_{su}+t_{sk}\big)\le T$$

which is the $L=1$, $B=0$ special case. A flop resynchronises the data to the clock edge every stage, so slack cannot cross a boundary and the single worst stage sets $T$ for the whole chip. Note also what the boxed form does *not* contain: for large $L$ the only per-hop overhead left is $t_{dq}$. **Setup, skew and non-overlap are paid once for the run, not once per stage** — the second, less-advertised reason latch pipelines are fast at high stage counts.

Feedback changes the character of the problem. Around a cycle in the latch graph spanning $m$ half-periods, the same inequality applies with the run wrapping onto itself, so the loop's total delay must fit $m\cdot T/2$ — a *fixed-point* condition rather than a topological one. That is the source of every tool problem in the next-but-one section.

## The costs

**Race-through.** Data launched when latch $i$ opens at $t_i$ must not reach latch $i+1$ while it is still open from its *previous* transparency window, which ended at $t_i-t_{\mathrm{nov}}$:

$$\delta_{dq}+\delta_{i+1}\;\ge\;t_h+t_{sk}-t_{\mathrm{nov}}$$

with $\delta$ the *minimum* delays. Two readings, both important. First, $T$ does not appear — you cannot fix a latch hold violation by slowing the clock, exactly as with flops. Second, $t_{\mathrm{nov}}$ is a free parameter that buys hold margin directly, and can make the requirement *negative*, i.e. free. This is genuinely better than flops, and Pedroso et al. make the point sharply: many Google–SkyWater shuttle designs failed permanently on GPIO scan-chain hold, which two-phase clocking fixes by widening the $\phi_1/\phi_2$ gap at no frequency cost. The gap is visible as the slack between the launching edge and the hold window it has to clear:

**But the gap comes out of the borrow budget.** $B=\tfrac{T}{2}-t_{\mathrm{nov}}-t_{su}-t_{sk}$. Every picosecond of non-overlap you buy for hold safety is a picosecond you cannot borrow. The two headline benefits are in direct competition and the designer sets the split.

**Clock distribution.** Two balanced trees instead of one, with a *phase* relationship to hold as well as a skew budget — and the trees see different latch loads, so their insertion delays differ. The open-source flow needs `balance_levels`, `repair_clock_nets` and obstruction-aware buffering in TritonCTS to cope. When one phase is clock-gated the asymmetry is severe: the RISCV32I clock-gated variant showed **2.63 ns skew against 0.18 ns** for the ungated recirculation-mux variant, from a single AND gate in the $\phi_2$ clock path.

**Area and power.** Real numbers on an RTL-to-GDS flow (SkyWater 130 nm, TT/25 °C/1.8 V, [Pedroso et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05374) Table 1): RISCV32I total power +36.6 % for the clock-gated variant (29.1 mW vs 21.3 mW baseline) and **+142.7 %** for the recirculation-mux variant (51.7 mW); sequential cells +7.3 % and +299.1 % respectively; combinational cells more than doubled (3502 → 7613/8184) because the retiming that redistributes the latch pairs also replicates logic. JPEG's recirculation-mux variant **failed to route at all** from congestion at 17 325 latches. The theoretical "a latch is half a flop" area argument does not survive contact with the flow.

**Loss of a clean cycle boundary.** With borrowing, no single time instant separates cycle $n$ from cycle $n+1$; a signal's "cycle" depends on how much its predecessors borrowed. Waveform debug, cycle-accurate model correlation, assertion timing and silicon bring-up all get harder, and there is no tooling that gives it back.

## EDA tool support — the real answer

Short version: **level-sensitive STA is solved in the textbook sense and merely adequate in the engineering sense.** Every signoff tool models it; nothing about the flow is pleasant.

**What genuinely works.** The mathematics has been settled since [Sakallah, Mudge & Olukotun's `checkTc`/`minTc`](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/123186.123312) (ICCAD 1990) posed multi-phase latch timing as a min–max linear program, general over arbitrary phase counts and covering short paths as well as long ones. Borrowing is on by default in PrimeTime and is capped by `set_max_time_borrow`; the semantics are identical across tools and the SDC command is standard. [OpenSTA's manual](https://github.com/parallaxsw/OpenSTA/blob/master/doc/OpenSTA.pdf) defines it as "the maximum amount of time that latches can borrow… the time that a data input to a transparent latch arrives after the latch opens", accepting clocks, instances or pins, and `all_registers -level_sensitive` selects the latches to constrain. Default max borrow is $W-t_{su}$ from the library. Cadence Tempus and AMD Vivado expose the same command with the same meaning.

**Where it hurts: convergence.** Latch loops make the timing graph cyclic, so arrival times cannot be propagated in topological order and must be iterated to a fixed point. The DATE 2025 analysis by [Shi, Guo, Lin et al.](https://yibolin.com/publications/papers/TIMING_DATE2025_Shi.pdf) is blunt about the state of the art: *"As of today, leading-edge STA engines including PrimeTime and OpenSTA still use a vanilla iteration-till-converge approach to handle latch loops… its worst theoretical complexity can also be up to $O(n^2)$"* in the pin count. Worse, neither tool looks ahead for *divergent* loops — a loop whose delay cannot fit its cycle budget simply iterates, arrival times growing without bound, until a violation happens to fall out. On their extreme latch-loop-chain cases they measure **hundreds of times** the runtime of an SCC-decomposed timer, and 12.31× / 7.3× over PrimeTime / OpenSTA on average across industrial designs. This is the single most concrete reason latch closure iterations are slow.

**Where it hurts: pessimism.** The older escape from cyclic graphs is cycle-cutting — designate loop-breaker arcs, delete them, analyse the resulting DAG. PrimeTime exposes this (loop-breaker latches, latch loop groups) and it is fast, but as Shi et al. note it "prevents timing propagation in the selected broken timing arcs, which may turn out to be critical", so it is fast and *may be inaccurate*. A second pessimism source is the borrowing policy itself: OpenSTA borrows minimally, "only as much time as needed to give the input path a setup slack of zero", so a borrowing endpoint reports exactly zero slack regardless of how much margin actually remains — [OpenSTA issue #243](https://github.com/The-OpenROAD-Project/OpenSTA/issues/243) documents downstream flows treating that zero as a violation. Reported slack on a latch endpoint is not a margin metric.

**OCV/AOCV.** No vendor claims latch borrowing and on-chip-variation derating interact badly, and no vendor publishes that they interact *well* either. The structural concern is real and follows from the boxed constraint: a borrowed path's setup check spans several segments and several clock-tree branches, so the common-path-pessimism-removal that makes flop OCV tractable has a longer, less-common launch/capture clock path to work with, and independent derates apply over a longer run. Statistical treatments exist ([Hurst & Brayton, IWLS 2006](https://people.eecs.berkeley.edu/~alanmi/publications/2006/iwls06_aaron.pdf) show latch designs *win* under variation, because delays balance anywhere inside the window and more variation samples meet timing), but signoff-grade AOCV for latch loops is not something I found publicly documented. Treat this as an open question, not a solved one.

**Implementation, not just analysis.** Analysis being correct does not make optimisation correct. OpenROAD's resizer setup-repair mis-handles borrowing endpoints ([issue #1962](https://github.com/The-OpenROAD-Project/OpenROAD/issues/1962)), where a maintainer states plainly: *"Modeling borrowing across multiple latches is something Liberty is not up to."* Commercial tools are better but not free of this — Zhang et al. report that "many commercial tools have limited support for retiming latches" and work around it by mapping latch retiming onto flop retiming, retiming back-to-back flops and only converting to latches afterwards. Pedroso et al. take the same route for the same reason. Hold fixing is the other weak spot: backend engines are built to close hold against an edge, not against a tunable phase gap.

**DFT and scan.** This is the part that is genuinely well-solved, and it is solved because IBM solved it in 1977. [Eichelberger & Williams' LSSD](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/800262.809169) (14th DAC, 1977) makes the L1/L2 pair *itself* the scan cell: separate system and scan clocks, non-overlapping scan clocks A and B held low in system mode, giving race-free and hazard-free operation in both modes. A two-phase latch pipeline is closer to LSSD than a flop design is, and LSSD ATPG has forty years of tool support. The cost is cell area and clock routing — one study reports a 54 % power increase for an LSSD standard-cell design over non-scannable — and, in practice, that mainstream muxed-D scan tooling is what your library and ATPG vendor actually tuned.

**Formal equivalence.** Combinational LEC needs matching state points, and latch conversion plus retiming destroys the correspondence. The workable discipline is to *constrain the transform so the correspondence survives*: Zhang et al. impose the rule that every original flop position must still carry a latch, explicitly "designed to make logical equivalence checking between the latch and FF-designs easier" — the fixed latches map to the original flops and the inserted ones are treated as transparent. Retiming-aware LEC in Conformal/Formality handles the rest. Pedroso et al. run Yosys `equiv` before and after retiming. So: solvable, but it constrains the optimisation rather than the other way round, and the design rule has to be in place before synthesis, not after.

**Clock-gating checks.** Gating a latch clock is where the flow bites hardest in practice. It is the mechanism that makes the clock-gated variant cheap (−29.2 % power and −50 % latch count over recirculation muxes on average), and it is also what produced the 2.63 ns skew above, because the gate sits in one phase's tree only. Every clock-gating check now has to be evaluated against a phase with a non-overlap requirement rather than a single edge.

## FPGA mapping

Two-phase latch pipelining is close to a non-starter on commercial FPGAs, for fabric reasons rather than tooling reasons. Every LUT already has a free flop beside it, so the "a latch is half a flop" area argument evaporates; the global clock network is single-phase and generating a skew- and duty-controlled second phase burns a PLL output and a global buffer; and clock already accounts for 20–39 % of FPGA dynamic power, so a second tree is expensive before it does anything useful.

The tooling exists and is deliberately discouraged. AMD/Xilinx documents `set_max_time_borrow` in [UG835](https://docs.amd.com/r/en-US/ug835-vivado-tcl-commands/set_max_time_borrow) and [UG949](https://docs.amd.com/r/2021.2-English/ug949-vivado-design-methodology/Max-Time-Borrow) — with the guidance that latches "are not recommended in general as they are difficult to test and validate in hardware" and that the command "should be used by expert users". Intel/Altera's Quartus timing analyser documents time borrowing with latches with the same `set_max_time_borrow` semantics.

The academic upside is real but unrealised: Teng's UofT MASc thesis measures that latch-based optimisation on FPGA benchmarks could cut clock period by **32 % on average** versus flop baselines, with iterative improvement alone worth ~9 %. Nobody ships this, because no vendor toolchain will place, route and sign off two-phase latch logic without hand-holding.

| | Edge-triggered flop | Pulsed latch | Full two-phase latch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Timing constraint | $\max_i d_i \le T-t_{oh}$ | $\sum_{[j,i]} D \le LT + B_p$ | $\sum_{[j,i]} D \le L\tfrac{T}{2} + B$ |
| Cycle time / hop (example) | 390 ps | 270 ps | 210 ps |
| Time borrow | none | $t_p-t_{su}-t_{sk}$ (~50 ps) | $\tfrac{T}{2}-t_{\mathrm{nov}}-t_{su}-t_{sk}$ (~140 ps) |
| Hold risk | baseline; unfixable by slowing $T$ | **worst** — $\delta\ge t_p+t_h+t_{sk}$, grows with pulse width | tunable to zero via $t_{\mathrm{nov}}$, at the cost of $B$ |
| Area | baseline | ~25 % smaller per cell; pulser amortised over a latch cluster | +7.3 % seq. cells (gated) to +299 % (recirc mux) |
| Clock power | baseline | −40 % clock-pin energy per cell; pulser adds back | +36.6 % total (gated) to +142.7 % (recirc mux) |
| Tool / DFT friction | none | low — flop-like interface, standard scan | high — latch loops, borrow convergence, LEC constraints, dual CTS |

**Pick edge-triggered flops** unless you have a specific measured reason not to; the flow is free and the debug story is clean. **Pick pulsed latches** when the design is flop-shaped, you need 20–30 % of cycle time back, and you can afford aggressive hold padding — this is where nearly every high-frequency team actually lands. **Pick full two-phase** only when you own the library, the CTS and the STA methodology, the pipeline is deep and genuinely unbalanced, and hold robustness (not just frequency) is a first-class goal.

## Worked example

Four unbalanced stages, $d = 220 / 180 / 300 / 200$ ps. Total logic 900 ps, mean 225 ps, max 300 ps. Overheads: flop $t_{cq}=45$, $t_{su}=30$; latch $t_{dq}=20$, $t_{su}=25$; skew $t_{sk}=15$; non-overlap $t_{\mathrm{nov}}=30$ ps; pulse width $t_p=90$ ps. Effective segment delays $D_k = d_k+t_{dq}$:

$$D = (240,\;200,\;320,\;220)\ \text{ps},\qquad \textstyle\sum D = 980\ \text{ps}$$

**Flops.** $T\ \ge\ \max_i d_i + t_{cq}+t_{su}+t_{sk} = 300+45+30+15 = \mathbf{390\ ps}$. The 300 ps stage sets it; the 180 ps stage wastes 120 ps every cycle, forever.

**Pulsed latches** (full-cycle grid, one latch per boundary, borrow $B_p=t_p-t_{su}-t_{sk}=90-25-15=50$ ps). Constraint $\sum_{[j,i]}D\le LT+B_p$, i.e. $T\ge(\sum-50)/L$:

| $L$ | runs → required $T$ |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | $190,\;150,\;\mathbf{270},\;170$ |
| 2 | $195,\;235,\;245$ |
| 3 | $236.7,\;230$ |
| 4 | $232.5$ |

$T=\mathbf{270\ ps}$, set by the single 320 ps segment relieved by its 50 ps borrow. Note the shape: **a pulsed latch does not remove the max, it discounts it.**

**Full two-phase** (half-period grid, $B=\tfrac{T}{2}-70$). Constraint $\sum_{[j,i]}D\le L\tfrac{T}{2}+\tfrac{T}{2}-70$, i.e. $T\ge 2\big(\sum+70\big)/(L+1)$:

| $L$ | runs → required $T$ |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | $310,\;270,\;390,\;290$ |
| 2 | $340,\;393.3,\;406.7$ |
| 3 | $415,\;405$ |
| 4 | $\mathbf{420}$ |

$T=\mathbf{420\ ps}$ over **two** stages per period, so $T/2 = \mathbf{210\ ps}$ per hop. The binding constraint has moved from $L=1$ to $L=4$: **the whole-pipeline average now sets the clock, not the worst stage** — which is the entire claim, appearing here as a change in which row of the table wins.

Check the borrow ledger at $T=420$: $B = 210-70 = 140$ ps. Nominal grid positions $0,210,420,630$; actual arrivals $240,\;440,\;760,\;980$; borrows $240{-}210=30$, $440{-}420=20$, $760{-}630=130$, $980{-}840=140$ ps. The last equals $B$ exactly — the pipeline closes with zero margin, as the $L=4$ equality predicted. Data physically exits at 980 ps against a nominal 2-cycle (840 ps) pipeline latency; the 140 ps difference is debt carried in the final transparency window.

**Result.** 390 → 270 → 210 ps per hop, i.e. **1.44× from pulsed latches and 1.86× from full two-phase**, on a pipeline whose max/mean imbalance is only 1.33×. Two caveats, both real: the raw periods 390/270/420 ps are *not* comparable, because the two-phase version packs two stages into one period — the honest figure of merit is time per sequencing hop, since pipeline depth is a design variable. And 1.86× is the ceiling with perfect $t_{\mathrm{nov}}$; buying hold margin by widening the gap moves $B$ down picosecond for picosecond and moves the $L=4$ row up with it.

## Industrial record

**IBM shipped it, and shipped it hardest.** LSSD made the L1/L2 latch pair the standard state element across IBM mainframe and later POWER designs from 1977 onward, with two-phase clocking and level-sensitivity chosen for race-free test as much as for speed. POWER6 (ISSCC/JSSC 2007, 4.7 GHz) is the clearest documented case: state elements outside arrays are scannable master–slave pairs on two opposite-phase, slightly skewed clocks C1 and C2 driving L1 and L2. A "delay C1" mode allows cycle stealing during the C2-rise/C1-fall overlap — shifting cycle boundaries *in hardware* to tune frequency after fabrication — and a pulsed mode (C1 held high, C2 pulsed) allows more cycle stealing still, at the cost of extra hold padding. Three selectable pulse widths (2.9 / 4.2 / 5.2 FO4) were built in so the part could be tuned in the lab, with padding done for the narrowest.

**Intel used the latch, not the discipline.** Itanium 2 (Naffziger et al., JSSC 2002) used pulsed latches with a nominal pulse of about one-sixth of the cycle — 125 ps at 1.2 GHz — with intentional clock skewing and time borrowing as first-class frequency techniques, backed by fuse-based deskew to 24 ps across the die and scan-based control to 7 ps. That is a single-phase, flop-interfaced design that captures the borrowing benefit locally. I found no public evidence of Intel adopting a global two-phase non-overlapping discipline in a modern core; treat claims to that effect as folklore.

**Where the industry landed.** On flops for the bulk, pulsed latches for the frequency-critical or clock-power-critical blocks, and full two-phase almost nowhere outside IBM. The reason is not that the timing theory is unsound — it has been sound since 1990 — but that the *ecosystem* is single-phase: Pedroso et al. state it flatly, that by the time logic synthesis, automated physical design and modern STA became standard practice, "the entire EDA ecosystem shifted to single-phase, edge-triggered flip-flop designs", and their 2026 flow is presented as the *first* fully automated open-source two-phase RTL-to-GDS path. Their own results are the honest verdict: it recovers timing closure on a RISCV32I that failed at 6.4 ns with flops (−0.515 ns setup slack), borrowing up to 2.88 ns — and it costs +36.6 % power in the best variant and +142.7 % in the worst.

The pulsed latch is the pragmatic settlement: flop-like interface for STA, scan and LEC; borrow window $t_p-t_{su}$ instead of $W-t_{su}$; measured ~25 % area and ~40 % clock-pin energy per cell against a flop, and 7.3 % average total-power reduction (4.3–11.2 % range, 45 nm) even after paying for pulsers and hold buffers. It captures most of the benefit at a small fraction of the flow cost, which is exactly why it won.

> [!note] The honest bottom line on the tooling question
> STA on level-sensitive latches is *correct* — the model is fifty years old and every signoff tool implements it. What is not solved is everything around it: borrow convergence is a fixed-point iteration with $O(n^2)$ worst case and no divergence lookahead in either PrimeTime or OpenSTA; loop-breaking trades that runtime for silent pessimism; reported slack at a borrowing endpoint is not a margin; optimisation engines and Liberty models lag the analysis engines; and LEC only works if you constrain the transform up front. None of these is a wall. All of them are a reason a schedule-driven team picks flops.
