# Choosing a Router Architecture

Every circuit in this folder serves one specification — deliver $L$ flits of $w$ bits bit-exact, in order, losslessly — so none of them is chosen for accuracy; the choice is decided by deadlock freedom first, then by buffer area, then by the clock period the allocator will tolerate.

> [!warning] No sourced silicon numbers, and therefore no printed pages
> This topic has no book source. Every figure below is **structural** — bit counts, crosspoint counts, flop counts, logic depth, cycle counts — derived in the long-form note and in the sibling notes, and it carries a `§` reference rather than a page. The LUT figures that do appear are arity arguments (a 6-LUT is natively a $4{:}1$ mux), not synthesis results. Absolute delay, slice and DSP totals, and power must come from a real synthesis run, never from this table.

Unless a row says otherwise, every number is for the canonical router of §Router microarchitecture: $p=5$, $V=4$, $B=6$, $w=128$; every latency is for the worked example's $H=5$, $L=4$, $t_r=1$, $t_{crt}=2$.

Every entry in the **Printed page** column is `—`, and that is a property of the topic, not an omission: the long-form note cites Dally & Towles, Dally & Seitz, Glass & Ni, Peh & Dally, Karol et al., Goossens et al. and Weste & Harris at chapter and paper level only, so there is no printed page behind any number here. The same `—` covers what a synthesis report would give and this note cannot: **slice counts, absolute delay in nanoseconds, and DSP-block counts** — a router contains no multiplier, so the DSP column would be zero for all 22 rows and is not worth a column. Cycle counts and bit counts *are* present because they are definitional: $T_0$, $t_{crt}$, $\eta$, $pVBw$ and $p^2w$ all follow from the derivations rather than from measurement. Where a delay cell reads `—` the source genuinely says nothing on that axis; [[Turn Model Routing]] and [[Dateline Virtual Channel Allocation]] in particular are constraints on the routing *relation* rather than new datapath, so they inherit the [[Dimension-Order Routing Unit]]'s depth and add only the productive-set logic.

**Deadlock freedom is a correctness constraint, not a trade-off, so it picks the routing before anything else gets a vote.** A cyclic channel dependency graph stalls forever with no error signal, so the first question is which construction makes $\mathcal D$ acyclic, and the answer is fixed by topology. On a mesh, [[Dimension-Order Routing Unit]] gets it for free — the lexicographic label $(\dim,\text{coord})$ strictly increases on every dependency edge, so acyclicity costs zero flops, two comparators and two LUT levels, and there is no cheaper correct answer at any $k$. Take it unless you can name the traffic pattern that hurts you: XY's failure is not deadlock but variance, since $|R|=1$ means a hot link has no alternative and the mean hop count $\tfrac23\sqrt N$ is achieved by exactly one path per pair. When you can name that pattern, [[Turn Model Routing]] is the upgrade with the best ratio in the folder — every cycle in $\mathcal D$ must use all four turns of one abstract cycle, so **two** prohibitions suffice where XY imposes four, and the two you get back are full adaptivity within a half-plane for a productive-set encoder and a selection function. On a torus none of this is enough, because the wraparound closes a cycle inside a single dimension with no turn at all; [[Dateline Virtual Channel Allocation]] is then mandatory rather than optional, and the honest way to read its $2\times$ buffer cost is against the $2\times$ throughput the wraparound bought in the first place ($\lambda_{\max}=4/\sqrt N$ against $2/\sqrt N$) — the wraparound and the dateline are one decision, not two.

**Buffers are the area, so flow control is the area decision and everything else is rounding.** At $p{=}5,V{=}4,B{=}6,w{=}128$ the [[Virtual Channel Buffer]]s are $15{,}360$ bits against the [[Crossbar Switch]]'s $3{,}200$ crosspoints and the allocators' handful of gates, and the flow-control scheme sets the multiplier on that dominant term. [[Store-and-Forward Flow Control]] and [[Virtual Cut-Through Flow Control]] both demand $B\ge L$, which ties the router's area to the *protocol's* packet length — a parameter the network designer usually does not own — and store-and-forward additionally re-serializes at every hop, $25$ cycles against $8$ for the worked example's five hops. That $3\times$ is why store-and-forward survives only where the control simplicity is the point, typically a configuration or debug ring nobody measures. [[Wormhole Flow Control]] breaks the $B\ge L$ coupling entirely and is the default for that reason alone, but it substitutes a subtler floor: $B\ge t_{crt}$, the credit round trip. A 3-stage router with one-cycle links each way and one cycle of credit logic gives $t_{crt}=5$, so [[Credit-Based Flow Control]] with $B=4$ caps every link at $4/5$ of its bandwidth *with no contention anywhere* — shrinking buffers to save area and silently throttling the network is the single most common NoC sizing mistake, and $\eta=\min(1,B/t_{crt})$ is the line to check before any other. The second buffer decision is $V$, and it is really three unrelated decisions sharing a parameter: $V=2$ per dimension if the dateline needs it, $V\approx4$ if head-of-line blocking does (the $2-\sqrt2\approx0.586$ input-queued bound climbs steeply to $V=4$ and flattens after), one VC per traffic class if QoS does — take the maximum, do not add them.

**Once the area is settled the clock period is decided in the allocator, which is the cheapest block in the router and the slowest.** [[Separable Input-First Allocator]] at $\Theta(\log V + \log p)$ is the default because at $p{=}5,V{=}4$ its arbiters are $5\times4{:}1$ and $5\times5{:}1$ — every one within a 6-LUT's fan-in, so the asymptotics never even engage and the whole allocator is about three LUT levels. Its cost is a non-maximal matching: crossbar ports idle while requests wait. [[Wavefront Allocator]] fixes exactly that, finding a maximal matching in one pass, and it is the right choice on an ASIC where its cell is a few transistors in a regular tiled mesh; on FPGA the $24$ diagonal steps are $24$ LUT-plus-routing hops and general routing dominates, so the wave loses for a reason that has nothing to do with logic. Inside either allocator, [[Fixed-Priority Arbiter]] is the floor — $g = r\wedge(-r)$ reassociated into a prefix-OR at depth $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil+2$, one LUT per lane below $n=6$ — and it is never usable alone because its starvation is unbounded; [[Round-Robin Arbiter]] is the same circuit twice plus a pointer, and since it sits on the critical path every cycle it is, in practice, the block that sets $f_{\max}$. If the period still will not close, stop optimising gates and delete stages instead: [[Lookahead Routing]] removes route computation from the critical path for one port field per flit, [[Speculative Switch Allocation]] turns $t_{VA}+t_{SA}$ into $\max(t_{VA},t_{SA})+t_{sel}$ for a small misspeculation rate, and together they take the canonical four stages to two. Both are close to free and both shorten $t_{crt}$, which relaxes the buffer floor of the previous paragraph — the rare optimisation that pays twice.

**The last two decisions are made outside the router: what the clock does at the tile boundary, and whether anyone needs a guarantee.** If one grid closes across the die, take none of the link FIFOs — they exist only to buy back what a global clock costs, which is $\Theta(N)$ in wire and buffer power and stops being payable past a few dozen tiles. One frequency with an arbitrary but *constant* phase per tile is the common case, and [[Mesochronous Link FIFO]] is then strictly the better answer: 2–4 flits, a strobe trained once at reset, and **zero** synchronizer cycles, because a constant phase carries no metastability risk in principle. Reach for [[Dual-Clock Asynchronous FIFO]] only when the frequencies are genuinely unrelated, and price the 2–3 destination cycles per crossing into $t_{crt}$ in both directions before deciding — the Gray pointer is not the cost, the synchronizer latency is, and it lands straight on the buffer floor. [[Bundled-Data Handshake Link]] removes the receiver clock from the link altogether and gives data-dependent rather than quantized latency, which is attractive exactly once and then has to be signed off with a matched delay line that static timing analysis cannot check — on an FPGA, where routing delay is neither predictable nor under your control, that assumption is close to unverifiable and the structure should be treated as ASIC-only. On the guarantee axis the split is clean and it is about the *kind* of bound, not its tightness: [[TDM Slot Table Scheduler]] gives a hard $(S-1)+H$ with exact $\tfrac{n}{S}w$ bandwidth and is the only option a real-time class can be specified against, at the price of idle reserved slots and an offline table; [[Priority Aging Arbiter]] and [[Deficit Round Robin Scheduler]] give statistical bounds instead — aging bounds starvation in cycles under fixed-priority classes, DRR splits the best-effort remainder in proportion to $Q_i$ to within one flit for one adder and one comparator per VC. The usual production answer uses both: reserved slots for the guaranteed class, best-effort VCs scavenging every idle slot the table wastes.
