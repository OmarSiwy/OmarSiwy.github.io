# Choosing an Exponential Architecture

Fourteen circuits, but not fourteen alternatives: four of them are whole operators that genuinely compete, five are the fixed decomposition of one of those four, and five are drop-in replacements for a single box inside it. Knowing which of the three groups a row belongs to answers most of the question before any number is read.

> [!warning] Chapter 22 reports no synthesis figures for any exponential architecture
> There are **no LUT counts, no slice counts, no DSP-block totals, no absolute delays and no $f_{\max}$ figures anywhere in pp. 641–667** — not for one architecture, and therefore not for any comparison between two. The book says so itself on p. 663: *"a detailed comparison on the same technology of this iterative architecture and a polynomial-based one is currently missing."* Every cell below is a **sizing** figure — table dimensions in words $\times$ bits, multiplier operand widths, adder widths, error bounds in ulps — because those are what the source actually gives. Where a column has nothing to report the cell is `—`. Do not fill one in from memory.

Numbers marked **†** are not printed in the book: they are instantiations of the book's own formulas at a named format, derived in the sibling note that owns the row, and they are flagged so that a reader chasing a citation does not go looking for them on the page.

The blanks are load-bearing. Every row's **Latency** cell is a structural depth rather than a cycle count or a nanosecond figure, because §22.3 and §22.5 draw combinational datapaths and never state a pipeline depth; the only genuine cycle counts in the whole chapter are the "one memory read" of the two tabulated rows and the $n\approx w_F/(\alpha-1)$ of the iterative family, and both are definitional. There is no **LUT / slice / DSP-count** column at all — not because the rows would be empty but because a column of fourteen `—` cells is worse than saying it once. The only two hard-resource figures the book commits to anywhere are in the single-precision case study on p. 661: **one 36 Kbit BRAM and one $18\times18$ DSP block**, and they belong to [[Higher-Order e^Y Evaluator]] alone.

**The fraction width decides, and it decides by doubling.** Every table in the chapter is $2^{\text{something}+w_F}$ or $2^{\text{something}+w_F/2}$ deep, and no multiplier grows faster than $w_F^2$, so the whole ordering is a memory-versus-multiplier race that memory loses at a predictable rate: one fraction bit doubles the table and adds a bit to each multiplier operand. Below about eight fraction bits memory has not yet started losing, and [[Direct Tabulation Exponential]] is the answer — $60$ Kbit at BFloat, no arithmetic at all, and the only architecture in the table that is **correctly rounded**. Take that seriously: correct rounding is not a tie-break here, it is a property the other thirteen rows cannot buy at any price, because each of them rounds a product or truncates an approximation somewhere. From binary16 upward the doubling has taken over and the range reduction of [[FPExp Operator]] is compulsory, at which point the question narrows to which box fills the $e^Y$ slot. [[Tabulated e^Y]] fills it with a table $5.45\times$ smaller than the direct one at BFloat and $114$ Kbit by binary16, which is the same wall one format later. [[First-Order e^Y Evaluator]] survives binary16 on a $2^7$ table and a $6\times8$ multiplier and then dies precisely, at $k\ge(w_F+4)/2$: single precision asks for $2^{14}$ entries. [[Higher-Order e^Y Evaluator]] is what takes over, because subtracting $Z+1$ from $e^Z$ makes the remaining function $2k$ bits narrower and that shrinkage is what pays for the second table. [[Polynomial h(Z) Evaluator]] is the double-precision answer for a reason that is not about size at all: it is the only row with two knobs, $k$ for the $e^A$ table and $d$ for the polynomial, so when one of them runs out of room the other still moves. [[Split-Table Exponential]] is the one row that never wins anywhere — it is legal, it does shrink the table eightfold at BFloat, and the book records it and drops it on p. 646, because by the precision at which you need the saving, additive range reduction reconstructs by concatenation where this reconstructs by a $w_F\times w_F$ multiplication.

**On an FPGA the granularity of the hard blocks, not the bit count, sets the winner.** A $5.45\times$ shrink in table bits is worth exactly one BRAM tile if it crosses a tile boundary and worth nothing if it does not, which is why [[Tabulated e^Y]] loses most of its apparent advantage the moment it is mapped: $2^{10}\times11$ occupies one 36 Kbit block and strands 69% of it. The same coarseness runs the other way and becomes free accuracy — widen the $e^A$ output to the memory's maximum, relax the $T_{\mathrm{trunc}}$ truncation until it just fills a DSP input, and if the product fits a single DSP it is *exact* and can be rounded to nearest, deleting $\delta_{\mathrm{mult}}$ outright (p. 661). That is the whole reason the single-precision higher-order configuration is stated as $k=10$, $\ell=-27$, $p=-18$, $t=10$: it is tuned to land on one BRAM and one DSP rather than on a minimal bit count. [[Iterative Table-Based Exponential]] is the row this argument disqualifies. Size its $\alpha_i$ to the LUT input width and each table is one LUT level deep and the rectangular multipliers underuse every DSP on the die; size it to fill the DSPs and the $2^{\alpha_i}$ tables explode. It belongs on a LUT-only fabric, or on ASIC — where the $L_i$ additions of the reduction are the critical path and implementations go to high-radix redundant arithmetic to break the carry chains. ASIC flips two other rows as well: without tile quantisation a $5.45\times$ shrink really is $5.45\times$, so [[Tabulated e^Y]] looks considerably better, while a ROM macro's per-bit cost pulls [[Direct Tabulation Exponential]]'s crossover several fraction bits earlier than it sits on fabric.

**The accuracy is spent upstream of the exponential, so do not shop for it downstream.** An absolute error $\delta_Y$ on the reduced argument becomes a *relative* error of the same size on $e^X$, which means the range reduction must be accurate to $2^{-w_F-g}$ **absolutely, however large $X$ was** — while the inner evaluator only ever approximates a value bounded in $[0.65,1.65]$, where absolute and relative accuracy coincide and a truncated table plus one small multiplier suffices. That asymmetry is why the five FPExp block rows carry the tight numbers and the five $e^Y$ rows carry loose ones. It also explains the two places where the budget is already spent before any architecture is chosen: [[Exponential Normalize-Round-Pack]]'s final rounding is $2^{-w_F-1}$, **half** of the $2^{-w_F}$ promise, on its own and irreducibly; and the $M<1$ branch of the analysis fills the remaining half exactly at $g=3$, with nothing to spare. Every guard bit is therefore a decision with a cost across the entire datapath, which is what makes the $4.7u$/$4.9u$ of [[Higher-Order e^Y Evaluator]] consequential — it forces $g=4$ unless you give the evaluator its own $g'=3$ instead. In the other direction, [[Tentative Exponent Unit]] is the one block permitted to be sloppy: its whole budget is $0.22$, half of which is spent truncating $X_{\mathrm{fix}}$ to LSB $-4$ to shrink the multiplier, and being wrong by one in $E$ costs nothing because the packer corrects it anyway. And if the argument is a computed sum, none of this is the first question: sum in fixed point and take [[Fixed-Point-In Exponential]], which is smaller *and* more accurate than converting to float — its guard bits are literally zero, and the shifter you skip is the conversion you were about to pay for.

**Four of the rows are not choices and should never be shopped for.** [[Exponential Special-Case Unit]], [[Exponential Fixed-Point Converter]], [[Tentative Exponent Unit]], [[Reduced Argument Subtractor]] and [[Exponential Normalize-Round-Pack]] are the decomposition of [[FPExp Operator]], not competitors to it; picking FPExp instantiates all five and the only freedom left is the $e^Y$ box. What they do carry is a coupling worth knowing before it bites. The special-case unit is allowed to be coarse only because the packer's $w_E+w_F+1$-bit adder is dimensioned to catch what it misses — for IEEEfloat(8,23) that is every input between $88.73$ and $127$ — so shrinking the packer's headroom bit silently breaks the front end. The reduced-argument subtractor's KCM skips its own high bits only because the tentative exponent unit stayed inside $0.22$; blow that budget and the subtractor does not degrade, it returns the wrong representative, off by exactly one. And the fixed-point converter needs no subnormal path at all, which is unique among floating-point front ends, purely because the special-case unit has already flushed every subnormal into the return-$1.0$ region.

**Related Notes**

- [[FPExp Operator]] – compares: the default whole operator, and the one every block row below belongs to.
- [[Direct Tabulation Exponential]] – compares: the only correctly rounded row, and the only one that needs no arithmetic.
- [[Split-Table Exponential]] – counter-example: legal, eightfold smaller at BFloat, and beaten at every precision by additive range reduction.
- [[Fixed-Point-In Exponential]] – extends: the same datapath entered one block downstream, smaller and more accurate when the argument is a sum.
- [[Exponential Special-Case Unit]] – depends-on: two constant comparisons on the exponent field, deliberately too coarse.
- [[Exponential Fixed-Point Converter]] – depends-on: one unidirectional shifter, and the reason input subnormals cost nothing.

- [[Reduced Argument Subtractor]] – depends-on: where the operator's real accuracy is won, by a KCM that skips its own MSBs.
- [[Exponential Normalize-Round-Pack]] – depends-on: one mux, one adder, and half the faithfulness budget.
- [[Tabulated e^Y]] – compares: the $e^Y$ slot filled by a single ROM, and where tile quantisation eats the saving.
- [[First-Order e^Y Evaluator]] – compares: binary16's answer, with the exact precision at which it stops.
- [[Higher-Order e^Y Evaluator]] – compares: single precision on one BRAM and one DSP, at the cost of $g=4$.
- [[Polynomial h(Z) Evaluator]] – extends: the same architecture with a second independent knob, which is what reaches double.
