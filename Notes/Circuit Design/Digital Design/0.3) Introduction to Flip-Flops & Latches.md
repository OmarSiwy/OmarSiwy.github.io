**Specification.** Input: one data bit $D$ plus a clock; output: one bit $Q$ holding the value $D$ carried across a clock boundary. Storage is *exact* — $0$ ulp, no quantization, nothing to round (contrast [[1.2) Error Accuracy]], where every operator loses something). The promise is conditional: it holds only while the timing inequalities of [the timing constraints below](#single-cycle-timing-constraints) are satisfied at every PVT corner. Violate them and the error is not a small perturbation, it is an arbitrary bit — or no bit at all, for an unbounded time. What is being optimized is **maximum clock frequency for a given area and clock power**, subject to a hold-safety constraint that is not negotiable.

That last clause is the whole note. Everything else follows from the fact that one of the two constraints can be bought with frequency and the other cannot.

## Cross-coupled inverters and bistability

Every storage element in this note is the same circuit wearing different clothes: two inverters in a loop.

Let $V_2=f(V_1)$ be the DC transfer curve of one inverter. A stable operating point requires $V_1=f(V_2)=f(f(V_1))$, so the states are the intersections of $f$ with $f^{-1}$. A monotone decreasing $f$ with high gain crosses its own inverse **three** times: near $0$, near $V_{DD}$, and once at the symmetric point $V_M=f(V_M)$.

Linearize about an intersection. Perturb $v_1$ by $\delta$; the loop returns $\delta \cdot A^2$ where $A=\left.\frac{df}{dV}\right|$ is the (negative) small-signal gain of one stage. By the **loop-gain criterion**, the point is stable iff $|A^2|<1$:

- at the rail intersections both transistors are far from saturation, $|A|\ll 1$, the perturbation dies — **stable**;
- at $V_M$ both devices are in saturation, $|A|\gg 1$ by design (that is what makes an inverter restore logic levels), so $|A^2|\gg 1$ — **unstable**.

Two stable states, one unstable one wedged between them. The unstable point is not a curiosity to be dismissed: it is exactly where [metastability](#metastability) lives, and the same gain $|A|\gg1$ that makes the circuit a good memory is what makes the escape from $V_M$ take unbounded time.

> [!note] Bistability is free, writeability is what costs
> The loop above stores forever and costs two inverters. Every extra transistor in a latch exists to *overpower* that loop for a controlled instant. The entire design problem of a latch is: how do you break a positive-feedback loop without breaking it permanently?

## SR latch

Replace the inverters with NOR gates and feed one input of each from outside:

$$Q = \overline{R + \bar{Q}}, \qquad \bar{Q} = \overline{S + Q}$$

With $S=R=0$ the NORs degenerate to inverters — by the **identity $x+0=x$** — and the loop holds. $S=1$ forces $\bar Q=0$, which by the same identity releases the other gate to hold $Q=1$. Solving the loop equation with $R=0$ gives the characteristic equation

$$Q^{+} = S + \bar{R}\,Q$$

by **Shannon expansion** of the loop about $S$ and $R$ (see [[0.1) Boolean Algebra]]).

$S=R=1$ drives $Q=\bar Q=0$, violating the complementarity the name $\bar Q$ asserts, and on the simultaneous release $S,R:1\!\to\!0$ the winner is decided by which gate happens to be faster — a race with no defined outcome. The NAND version ($\bar S,\bar R$ active-low) has the same defect at $\bar S=\bar R=0$.

The SR latch survives in exactly two places: as the storage core inside other latches, and as a switch debouncer, where the mechanical contact physically cannot assert both inputs at once so the forbidden state is unreachable by construction.

## Level-sensitive D latch and transparency

Tie $R=\bar S$ and the forbidden state becomes unreachable — one input, no invalid code. Gate it with a clock:

$$Q^{+} = clk\cdot D + \overline{clk}\cdot Q$$

which is a **2:1 multiplexer whose select is the clock and whose feedback leg is the hold path**. This is the whole latch. In CMOS it is two transmission gates and two inverters (roughly 8–12 transistors); on an FPGA it is one LUT with its output fed back, which is why an accidental latch is so cheap to infer and so expensive to debug.

The behaviour splits into two phases:

- **$clk=1$ — transparent.** $Q$ tracks $D$ through a plain combinational delay $t_{dq}$. The latch is a wire.
- **$clk=0$ — opaque.** The feedback leg closes and $Q$ holds.

Transparency is the latch's defining property and it cuts both ways. It means data can *keep moving* after the edge — the basis of time borrowing in [[0.3a) Dual-Phase Latches]] — and it means the latch's hold requirement extends across the whole transparent phase, because for that entire window the input is connected to the output.

A latch alone cannot build a feedback loop: with $clk=1$ the loop `latch → logic → latch` is a combinational cycle that will oscillate or settle to a fixed point of the logic, neither of which is a state machine. You need a phase where *nothing* is transparent.

## Master–slave edge-triggered D flip-flop

Cascade two latches on opposite clock phases, as drawn above. The master is transparent while $clk=0$ and samples $D$; the slave is opaque and holds the previous value. At the rising edge the master closes and the slave opens, propagating the value the master captured — and only that value, because the master is now opaque.

The invariant that makes it work: **the two latches are never transparent simultaneously**, so there is no combinational path from $D$ to $Q$ at any instant. Data advances exactly one stage per clock edge, which is precisely the $z^{-1}$ that [[0.4) Synchronous Sequential Logic]] assumes.

The cost is real: two storage nodes, two clocked stages (typically 20–26 transistors static CMOS), $4$ clocked transistors of load on the clock net per bit, and a genuine $t_{cq}$ that a transparent latch does not pay. In exchange the timing model collapses to two inequalities a tool can check in $O(\text{paths})$ without solving loop equations — which is why every mainstream synthesis and STA flow assumes flops.

The overlap invariant is not automatic. If $clk$ and $\overline{clk}$ are generated by a local inverter, there is a window of width $t_{inv}$ where both phases are high and the flop is briefly a transparent latch. This is *internal race-through*, and it is the physical origin of the flop's hold time: $t_{hold}$ is the designer's promise that this window is closed before your data can sneak through it.

## JK and T flip-flops, and why they are historical

Add feedback around the SR characteristic equation to make the forbidden state useful:

$$Q^{+}_{JK} = J\bar{Q} + \bar{K}Q, \qquad Q^{+}_{T} = T \oplus Q$$

JK's four-way behaviour (hold / set / reset / toggle) let a designer implement a next-state function with fewer gates when gates were discrete packages, because the excitation table has **don't-cares in half its entries** — $Q:0\!\to\!1$ needs $J=1$, $K=\times$. Under Karnaugh minimization those don't-cares are free literals, and in a 1970s design where every gate was a pin on a DIP that mattered.

It stopped mattering. Both reduce to a D flop with combinational logic on its input:

$$D = J\bar{Q}+\bar{K}Q, \qquad D = T\oplus Q$$

so in a standard-cell or LUT library they cost strictly more than the D flop plus the mux/XOR the synthesizer would have produced anyway, while the extra inputs add clock-adjacent load and a second data pin to characterize. Modern libraries carry D flops with asynchronous reset, synchronous reset, and clock enable; JK cells are usually absent entirely. T behaviour still shows up *as a pattern* — an XOR in front of a D flop is the standard toggle cell in [[2.8) Counters]] — just not as a library element.

> [!note] Read them, do not write them
> JK and T remain worth knowing because excitation tables in older references and exam problems are stated in them, and because a toggle *structure* is still the right way to build a counter bit. Instantiating a JK cell in new RTL is a code smell.

## Setup, hold, clock-to-Q, and the aperture window

Three characterized numbers describe a flop at its clock boundary:

| Parameter | Definition | Bounds the |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $t_{setup}$ | $D$ must be stable this long **before** the edge | end of the aperture's leading side |
| $t_{hold}$ | $D$ must remain stable this long **after** the edge | trailing side |
| $t_{cq}$ | edge $\to$ $Q$ valid, characterized as $t_{cq,max}$ and $t_{cq,min}$ | departure of new data |

Together $[-t_{setup},\,+t_{hold}\,]$ around the edge is the **aperture window**: a keep-out zone during which $D$ must not move. Its width $T_A = t_{setup}+t_{hold}$ is a property of the cell; its *position* relative to the edge is not fixed by physics, and $t_{hold}$ is frequently negative in modern libraries (the internal clock path to the second stage is slow enough that data may change slightly before the nominal edge).

The physical meaning: $t_{setup}$ is the time the master latch's internal loop needs to regenerate a full-swing value before it is cut off, and $t_{hold}$ is the time until the transparency window of the master is genuinely closed. Neither is a hard cliff — push $D$ closer to the edge and $t_{cq}$ degrades smoothly, then sharply. Libraries define $t_{setup}$ at the point where $t_{cq}$ has degraded by 10 %, which is why setup and $t_{cq}$ trade against each other and why a single "setup time" is a linearization of a curve.

Take a launch flop $L$ and a capture flop $C$ separated by combinational logic.

Define the skew with a **sign**: $t_{skew} = t_{arrival}(C) - t_{arrival}(L)$. Positive skew means the capture clock is late.

**Setup (max-delay).** The launch edge at $t=0$ produces valid data at $C$'s $D$ pin at $t_{cq,max}+t_{logic,max}$. The capture edge occurs at $T_{clk}+t_{skew}$. Data must be stable $t_{setup}$ before it:

$$t_{cq,max}+t_{logic,max}+t_{setup} \;\le\; T_{clk}+t_{skew}$$

$$\boxed{\;T_{clk}\;\ge\;t_{cq}+t_{logic,max}+t_{setup}-t_{skew}\;}$$

**Hold (min-delay).** The *same* edge $n$ launches fast data that arrives at $C$ at $t_{cq,min}+t_{logic,min}$. Edge $n$ reaches $C$ at $t_{skew}$, and $C$'s aperture stays open until $t_{skew}+t_{hold}$. New data must not arrive before then:

$$t_{cq,min}+t_{logic,min} \;\ge\; t_{skew}+t_{hold}$$

$$\boxed{\;t_{cq,min}+t_{logic,min}\;\ge\;t_{hold}+t_{skew}\;}$$

Note what dropped out of the second inequality: **$T_{clk}$ never appeared**, because both sides refer to the same clock edge. That is not an algebraic accident, it is the structure of the problem.

In practice the sign of $t_{skew}$ is unknown until place-and-route, so both are written with the uncertainty budget $T_{skew}=|t_{skew}|_{max}$ taken adversely, plus cycle-to-cycle **jitter** $t_j$ on the period:

$$T_{clk}\;\ge\;t_{cq}+t_{logic,max}+t_{setup}+T_{skew}+t_{j}$$
$$t_{cq,min}+t_{logic,min}\;\ge\;t_{hold}+T_{skew}$$

Jitter appears only in the setup equation. The hold check compares two paths from one edge, so a displacement of that edge is **common-mode and cancels** — the same reason $T_{clk}$ vanished.

> [!important] You cannot slow your way out of a hold violation
> $T_{clk}$ appears in the setup inequality and **not** in the hold inequality. A setup failure is a performance bug: drop the frequency and the part works, ship it slower, fix it next spin. A hold failure is a *functional* bug — the chip is broken at every frequency, at DC, forever, and no board-level change can rescue it.
>
> Everything else in sequential design is downstream of this asymmetry:
> - Timing sign-off runs hold at the **fast** corner (low temp, high $V_{DD}$, fast process) where $t_{cq,min}+t_{logic,min}$ is smallest, and setup at the slow corner. Two corners, opposite directions, both mandatory.
> - Hold is fixed by *adding* delay — buffer insertion on short paths — which costs area and leakage but never costs frequency. Tools do this automatically and generously, because the alternative is a dead die.
> - Positive skew *buys* setup margin and *spends* hold margin, one-for-one. Useful skew scheduling is real, and it is playing with the only lever that can turn a working chip into a paperweight.
> - This is why deliberately transparent styles ([[0.3a) Dual-Phase Latches]]) are dangerous: transparency lengthens the window during which fast data can race through.

## Metastability

If $D$ changes inside the aperture window, the master latch is cut off partway through regenerating, leaving the cross-coupled loop near the unstable point $V_M$ derived above.

Small-signal about $V_M$, with each node loaded by $C$ and driven by transconductance $g_m$ into output resistance $R$:

$$C\frac{dv_1}{dt}=-g_mv_2-\frac{v_1}{R},\qquad C\frac{dv_2}{dt}=-g_mv_1-\frac{v_2}{R}$$

Subtract, and define the differential mode $v_d=v_1-v_2$ (the **differential/common-mode decomposition**; the common mode decays and is uninteresting):

$$C\frac{dv_d}{dt}=\left(g_m-\frac{1}{R}\right)v_d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v_d(t)=v_d(0)\,e^{t/\tau},\qquad \tau=\frac{C}{g_m-1/R}\approx\frac{C}{g_m}$$

Positive feedback gives **exponential divergence with time constant $\tau$**, not decay. Resolving to a full swing $\Delta V$ takes

$$t_{res}=\tau\ln\frac{\Delta V}{|v_d(0)|}$$

which is *unbounded as $v_d(0)\to0$*. There is no $t_{res}$ that always suffices. If the input transition time is uniformly distributed over the clock period — a fair model for a genuinely asynchronous source — then $v_d(0)$ is uniform near zero and

$$P(\text{unresolved after } t) = e^{-t/\tau}$$

Failures need a coincidence (arrival inside the aperture, characterized as an effective width $T_0$) *and* a slow resolution. Rate $=T_0 f_c f_d\,e^{-t_r/\tau}$, hence

$$\boxed{\;\mathrm{MTBF}=\frac{e^{\,t_r/\tau}}{T_0\,f_c\,f_d}\;}$$

with $f_c$ the clock frequency, $f_d$ the rate of asynchronous data events, and $t_r$ the resolution time you allow. Note the shape: **linear in everything except $t_r$, exponential in $t_r$.** Halving $f_d$ doubles MTBF. Adding one clock period of resolution time multiplies it by $e^{T_{clk}/\tau}$.

That exponential is the entire justification for the **synchronizer chain**: $N$ flops in series on the destination clock, nothing combinational between them, giving $t_r \approx N\,T_{clk}-t_{setup}-t_{cq}$.

Rules that follow directly, and are not negotiable:
1. **Never fan out an unsynchronized signal** to two places. Each destination resolves independently and may resolve *differently* — one FSM sees the bit, another does not.
2. **Never put logic between synchronizer stages.** Logic consumes $t_r$, the one term in the exponent.
3. **Synchronize control, not data.** A multi-bit bus through parallel synchronizers gives bits from different cycles. Use a handshake, a gray-coded pointer, or an asynchronous FIFO.

## Clock skew and jitter

**Skew** is a static, spatial difference in arrival time between clock pins: unequal tree depth, unequal load, on-die $V_{DD}$ and temperature gradients. It is repeatable, so it can be measured, budgeted, and even scheduled deliberately. Balanced structures — H-trees, spines with matched taps — exist to bound $T_{skew}$; the entire cost of a clock tree (often 20–40 % of dynamic power) is paid to make this number small.

**Jitter** is a dynamic, temporal variation of the same edge cycle to cycle: PLL noise, supply ripple, thermal noise in the tree buffers. It is not repeatable and cannot be scheduled — only budgeted.

| | skew | jitter |
| --- | --- | --- |
| nature | spatial, static | temporal, random |
| affects setup | yes, signed | yes, always adverse |
| affects hold | yes, and it is the dangerous one | largely cancels (common edge) |
| fixable after tapeout | no | partially, via supply/PLL cleanup |
| can be exploited | yes, useful skew | no |

## Gated clocks and enable flops

A register that must hold its value has two implementations:

**Enable flop** — a 2:1 mux in the D path, $D' = en\cdot D + \overline{en}\cdot Q$. Purely combinational, timing-safe, and it adds a mux delay to $t_{logic,max}$ on *every* cycle including the ones that matter. The clock still toggles, so the flop still burns its clock power.

**Gated clock** — $clk_g = clk \cdot en$, using an **integrated clock-gating cell**: a latch that samples $en$ on the low phase, feeding an AND. The latch is not optional. Gating with a bare AND lets a glitch on $en$ during the high phase produce a spurious edge, and a spurious clock edge is a functional failure of exactly the kind that survives every simulation and appears once in silicon.

Clock gating is the single largest dynamic-power lever in a synchronous design, because the clock net is the highest-activity node on the chip ($\alpha=1$ by definition) and gating removes both the tree power and the flop's internal clock power. The cost: $en$ acquires a setup constraint against the gating cell, the gated branch becomes a separate (skewed) clock domain that STA must model, and test insertion needs a bypass so scan can shift.

> [!note] Write the enable, let the tool choose
> RTL should say `if (en) q <= d;` and let synthesis pick between the mux and the gating cell under a power constraint. Hand-instantiated `assign clk_g = clk & en;` is the classic way to ship a chip that fails at one corner.

## FPGA mapping

The trade-offs invert almost completely on an FPGA.

- **Flops are free; latches are not.** Every LUT output in a slice has a dedicated flop next to it. An unused flop is wasted silicon, so registering aggressively costs nothing. A latch, by contrast, must be built out of a LUT with combinational feedback, which consumes a LUT *and* creates a path STA must special-case. Vendor tools warn on inferred latches for good reason — they usually come from an incomplete `if` or `case` in [[0.2) Combinational Logic]], not from intent.
- **Hold violations are essentially impossible on the datapath.** The flop-to-flop connection inside a slice has a fixed, characterized delay, and the global clock tree's skew is small and known; the router will not produce a path shorter than $t_{hold}$. Hold is an ASIC problem. It returns on the FPGA only at I/O boundaries and across clock-domain crossings.
- **Enables are free, gated clocks are expensive.** Every slice flop has a dedicated `CE` pin, so the enable mux costs nothing — no LUT, no added logic delay. Meanwhile a gated clock must be routed on a global buffer resource, of which there are a few dozen on the whole device. Use `BUFGCE` if you must gate; prefer `CE`.
- **Clock trees are pre-built.** Global buffers give tightly bounded skew across the die for free, and there is no tree to design. Jitter is dominated by the on-chip PLL/MMCM.
- No pulsed-latch equivalent exists; there is no way to generate and distribute a narrow, controlled pulse in the fabric.

See [[1.3) FPGAs]] and [[1.3.1) Lookup-Tables]] for the underlying slice structure.

## Trade-offs

> [!warning] Numbers below are canonical order-of-magnitude figures, not characterized data
> This note has no book source, so the transistor and load counts are the typical static-CMOS values every textbook quotes rather than measurements from a specific library. Real values are PDK-dependent and vary by 2× across cell families (static vs. dynamic, single- vs. dual-rail, low-$V_t$ vs. high-$V_t$). Use them for reasoning about ratios, never for a budget — characterize the actual library.

| | transparent latch | master–slave flop | pulsed latch |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| storage nodes / bit | 1 | 2 | 1 |
| transistors / bit (typ.) | 8–12 | 20–26 | 10–14 + shared pulse gen |
| clocked transistors / bit | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| clock power | low | highest | low |
| $D\to Q$ cost | $t_{dq}$ if data arrives while transparent; $t_{cq}$ otherwise | always $t_{cq}$ | small $t_{cq}$ |
| slack passing between stages | yes, up to half a phase | **none** | yes, up to $t_{pw}$ |
| hold requirement | severe — the whole transparent phase | mild, $t_{hold}$ only | moderate, $\propto t_{pw}$ |
| hold constraint | $t_{cq,min}+t_{logic,min}\ge t_{hold}+T_{skew}+T_{phase}$ | $\ge t_{hold}+T_{skew}$ | $\ge t_{hold}+t_{pw}+T_{skew}$ |
| STA model | loop analysis, borrowing propagates | two independent inequalities | needs pulse-width modelling |
| tool / library support | poor | universal | vendor-specific |
| FPGA | discouraged, costs a LUT | free, one per LUT | unavailable |

Pick the **master–slave flop** by default and for anything you intend to hand to a synthesis flow — the timing model is checkable, the libraries are characterized, and the hold margin is the largest of the three. Pick the **pulsed latch** when clock power and $t_{cq}$ dominate and you own the pulse generator and the sign-off methodology, i.e. inside a hand-crafted high-performance core. Pick **latches** only when you specifically want to pass slack between unbalanced pipeline stages, and only with the analysis in [[0.3a) Dual-Phase Latches]] behind you.

## Worked example

An 8-bit register-to-register path: `FF_L[8] → 8-bit ripple-carry adder → FF_C[8]`, with the following assumed library numbers (illustrative, stated as assumptions, not characterization data).

$$t_{cq,max}=120\ \mathrm{ps},\quad t_{cq,min}=60\ \mathrm{ps},\quad t_{setup}=80\ \mathrm{ps},\quad t_{hold}=50\ \mathrm{ps},\quad T_{skew}=40\ \mathrm{ps},\quad t_j=25\ \mathrm{ps}$$

The adder's critical path is the carry chain through all 8 bits; its shortest path is bit 0's sum, which does not wait on any carry. Take $t_{logic,max}=8\times90=720$ ps and $t_{logic,min}=45$ ps.

**Setup.**
$$T_{clk}\ \ge\ 120+720+80+40+25 = 985\ \mathrm{ps}\ \Longrightarrow\ f_{max}=1.02\ \mathrm{GHz}$$

**Hold.**
$$t_{cq,min}+t_{logic,min}=60+45=105\ \mathrm{ps}\quad\text{vs.}\quad t_{hold}+T_{skew}=50+40=90\ \mathrm{ps}$$

$105\ge 90$: passes, with $15$ ps of margin. Now suppose the router places $C$ on a clock branch $70$ ps late instead of $40$:

$$105 \ \ge\ 50+70=120 \quad\text{\textbf{false}}\quad\Longrightarrow\ \text{violation of } 15\ \mathrm{ps}$$

Slowing the clock to $500$ MHz changes the left side by nothing and the right side by nothing — the inequality has no $T_{clk}$ in it. The only fix is $+15$ ps (plus margin) of buffering on the bit-0 sum path, which relaxes setup on that path too since bit 0 was not critical. Cost: one buffer, zero frequency.

**Metastability.** Feed the same $D$ from an asynchronous source at $f_c=1$ GHz, $f_d=100$ MHz, with a deliberately poor flop, $\tau=100$ ps, $T_0=20$ ps. Resolution time with $N$ stages is $t_r=N\,T_{clk}-t_{setup}-t_{cq}=N\cdot1000-80-120$ ps, and $T_0f_cf_d = 20\times10^{-12}\cdot10^{9}\cdot10^{8}=2\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$:

| $N$ | $t_r$ | $e^{t_r/\tau}$ | MTBF |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.80 ns | $e^{8}\approx3.0\times10^{3}$ | $1.5\times10^{6}$ s $\approx$ **17 days** |
| 2 | 1.80 ns | $e^{18}\approx6.6\times10^{7}$ | $3.3\times10^{10}$ s $\approx$ **1000 years** |
| 3 | 2.80 ns | $e^{28}\approx1.4\times10^{12}$ | $7.2\times10^{14}$ s $\approx$ **23 Myr** |

One extra flop — a few square microns — moves the failure rate by five orders of magnitude. Nothing else in digital design has that leverage, and it is entirely because $t_r$ sits in an exponent while everything else sits in a product.

**Error in ulp:** $0$, in every passing row above. Sequential elements do not approximate. The failure mode is categorical, not numerical, and that is why the constraints are inequalities rather than error bounds.

---

The flop's guarantee — data crosses at the edge, never before, never after — is bought by throwing away every picosecond of slack that a fast stage did not use. Stage $k$ finishing $200$ ps early cannot lend that time to stage $k+1$; the edge is hard, and $T_{clk}$ is set by the single worst stage. In a pipeline that cannot be balanced by construction — a multiplier array feeding a trivial mux, say — that is a large amount of purchased silicon left idle.

Making the boundary *soft*, so slack flows forward from an easy stage into a hard one, requires giving up the flop's hard edge and returning to transparency, with all the hold-margin danger the callout above describes. That is the subject of [[0.3a) Dual-Phase Latches]].
