**Specification.** Inputs $X$ in $\mathrm{fix}(m_X,\ell_X)$ on $w_X=m_X-\ell_X+1$ bits and $Y$ in $\mathrm{fix}(m_Y,\ell_Y)$ on $w_Y$ bits, either signedness. Output $P$ in $\mathrm{fix}(m_P,\ell_P)$, **specified independently of the inputs** — this is the whole game. The exact product needs $\ell_P=\ell_X+\ell_Y$, $m_P=m_X+m_Y+1$, so $w_P=w_X+w_Y$; anything narrower is a *truncated* multiplier and buys area with error. Accuracy promised here: **last-bit accurate**, error bounded by $u=2^{\ell_P}$ (faithful), not $u/2$ (round-to-nearest) — see [[1.2) Error Accuracy]]. Optimized for **area** (LUT/DSP count) at $\mathcal{O}(\log w)$ latency; the accuracy relaxation is what pays for the area.

> [!important] Why last-bit and not nearest
> A multiplier last-bit accurate to $\ell_P=k$ is in general much cheaper to build than one rounded to nearest at $\ell_P=k+1$, so FloPoCo provides last-bit accurate multipliers by default (p. 209). Every truncation result below rests on this budget.

## A functional point of view

### Integer multipliers

For unsigned $X,Y$, $(2^{w_X}-1)(2^{w_Y}-1)<2^{w_X+w_Y}$, so the product always fits on $w_X+w_Y$ bits. The schoolbook method computes $w_X\cdot w_Y$ partial products arranged in a parallelogram; since multiplication is commutative, expanding $X$ or expanding $Y$ gives the *same* dot diagram (Fig. 8.1, p. 205). Sheared upright, the parallelogram is a **rectangle of width $w_X$ and height $w_Y$** (Fig. 8.2) — that rectangle is the object every FPGA tiling result below manipulates. Multipliers with $w_X\ne w_Y$ are called *rectangular*; the AMD DSP blocks are $18\times27$ (p. 204). Signed $\times$ signed is asymmetric: the largest product is $(-2^{w_X-1})(-2^{w_Y-1})=2^{w_X+w_Y-2}$, and *only that one input combination* uses the top bit. Half the $w_X+w_Y$-bit range is unreachable, because $(-X)\times Y$ and $X\times(-Y)$ always collide (p. 206).

### Fixed-point numbers

A fixed-point number is an integer scaled by $2^{\ell}$ (see [[1.1) Number Formats]]), so a fixed-point multiplier *is* an integer multiplier whose output is scaled by $2^{\ell_X+\ell_Y}$. Hence (p. 207) the smallest exact format is $w_P=w_X+w_Y$, with $\ell_P=\ell_X+\ell_Y$ and $m_P=m_X+m_Y+1$; asking for $\ell_P>\ell_X+\ell_Y$ *is* the request for truncation or rounding.

**The MSB issue.** For two signed $w$-bit numbers in $[-1,1)$, i.e. $\mathrm{sfix}(0,\ell)$ with $\ell=-w+1$, the exact product lands in $\mathrm{sfix}(1,2\ell)$ — a sign bit of weight $2^1$ that only $(-1)\times(-1)=+1$ ever needs. Computing just right means legislating that value away in the specification (signal in $(-1,1)$, filter coefficients bounded away from $-2^{m_Y}$, a deliberately scaled $(1-2^{-\ell_R})\sin(\pi x)$), so the product fits $\mathrm{sfix}(0,2\ell)$. The saving is not in the multiplier but in every operator downstream, which now consumes one bit less (p. 209).

> [!warning] $m_P<m_X+m_Y+1$ is a red flag
> A multiplier whose MSB is smaller than the exact one can **silently overflow** — an overflow whose result cannot be detected as such, usually catastrophic. The book knows of essentially one legitimate case: $Y=X-E\times\log 2$ in range reduction, where $E$ was constructed so the leading bits of the product provably cancel (p. 212). Anything else should raise suspicion.

### Error analysis involving multipliers

Take both inputs already inexact, $X=X_0+\delta_X$ and $Y=Y_0+\delta_Y$, and let $\delta_\mathrm{mult}$ be the multiplier's own error. Expanding the product by **distributivity**:

$$P=(X_0+\delta_X)(Y_0+\delta_Y)+\delta_\mathrm{mult}=X_0Y_0+\underbrace{\delta_XY_0+\delta_YX_0+\delta_X\delta_Y+\delta_\mathrm{mult}}_{=\;\delta_P}$$

By the **triangle inequality**, with $\overline{X_0},\overline{Y_0}$ the maximum absolute values from the format (Table 8.1, p. 207):

$$\overline{\delta}_P=\overline{Y_0}\cdot\overline{\delta}_X+\overline{X_0}\cdot\overline{\delta}_Y+\overline{\delta}_X\overline{\delta}_Y+\overline{\delta}_\mathrm{mult}$$

The second-order term is negligible; the other three are not. Crucially $\overline{Y_0}\overline{\delta}_X$ and $\overline{X_0}\overline{\delta}_Y$ **survive even with an exact multiplier** — an exact multiplier amplifies its inputs' errors and buys nothing. If inputs are relatively accurate, $\overline{\delta}_X\approx2^{\ell_X}$, then $\overline{Y_0}\overline{\delta}_X\approx2^{m_Y+\ell_X}$ and $\overline{X_0}\overline{\delta}_Y\approx2^{m_X+\ell_Y}$. Balancing these two, and balancing both against $\overline{\delta}_\mathrm{mult}=2^{\ell_P}$:

$$\ell_P\;\approx\;m_Y+\ell_X\;\approx\;m_X+\ell_Y \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad w_X\approx w_Y \tag{8.10, p.210}$$

### Multipliers for computing just right

- **Exact multipliers, incl. rectangular** (pp. 211–212), are useful — they are the FPGA building block, and they are what cryptography and RNS arithmetic need.
- **Truncated square multipliers are the sweet spot.** $w_X\approx w_Y$ falls straight out of (8.10) whenever neither input is exact.
- **Truncated rectangular multipliers are rare.** They only make sense when one input is *exact* ($\delta_X=0$), which drops the $w_X\approx w_Y$ constraint and leaves $\ell_P\approx m_X+\ell_Y$. The canonical case is $E\times\log 2$ in a logarithm: $E$ is an exact integer exponent, $\log 2$ a wide constant. A truncated rectangular multiplier where *neither* input is exact means hardware is being wasted — truncate the inputs before multiplying instead.

## Overview of multiplier construction

Three stages, as drawn above: generate partial products, compress, add. Because a fixed-point multiplier is an integer multiplier plus an implicit scale, the rest of this note works with integers and the single parameter $w$ (a second parameter reappears only for truncated multipliers). On FPGAs the same flow holds, but partial product generation may use *heterogeneous* resources — LUTs, carry chains, and DSP blocks. Choosing among them is a **geometric tiling problem** over the $w_X\times w_Y$ rectangle, solvable by integer linear programming (Sect. 8.4). Truncation starts from a smaller subset of partial products (Sect. 8.5); Karatsuba trades multiplications for additions at the cost of delay (Sect. 8.6).

## Partial product generation and sign management

Unsigned radix-2 is the double sum

$$P=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{w_X-1}2^ix_i\right)Y=\sum_{i=0}^{w_X-1}\sum_{j=0}^{w_Y-1}2^{i+j}x_iy_j$$

one AND gate per bit, $w_X w_Y$ bits total. Radix 2 has the pleasant property that a digit-by-digit product is *one* digit; any radix above 2 produces two (p. 214).

### Signed multiplication in radix-2

Substituting the two's-complement value of $X$ and applying **distributivity of $\times$ over $+$**:

$$X\times Y=\left(-2^{w-1}x_{w-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{w-2}2^ix_i\right)Y=-2^{w-1}x_{w-1}Y+\sum_{i=0}^{w-2}2^ix_iY$$

Two changes to the array (p. 215):

1. every row $2^ix_iY$ must be **sign-extended** to $w_P$, because $Y$ is itself signed;
2. the top row $x_{w-1}Y$ must be **subtracted**, i.e. replaced by its two's complement — by the identity $-z=\bar z+1$, negate its bits and inject a $1$ at that line's LSB.

Sign extensions are long runs of a repeated bit, and the **sign-extension reduction** of [[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]] replaces each run by a constant vector plus one complemented bit. Adding up all the resulting constant columns (including the $+1$ from negating $x_{w-1}Y$) collapses them to a handful of constant bits (Fig. 8.7, p. 216). **The whole cost of signedness in a radix-2 multiplier is a few constant bits in the array.**

### Radix-4 Booth encoding

Split $X$ into 2-bit chunks, $X=\sum_i 4^iX_i$ with $X_i\in\{0,1,2,3\}$. This halves the number of rows — and hence the compressor tree — but $X_i=3$ needs $3Y=2Y+Y$, an addition on the critical path that gives back most of the win (p. 216).

Fix it by moving to the **redundant** radix-4 digit set $Z_i\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}$ (see [[1.1) Number Formats#Redundant Number Systems]]), for which every $Z_iY$ is a shift, a negation, or both. The recoding is not a lookup table; it is one algebraic identity applied per digit.

Start from the geometric-series identity, which is just $2^{k+1}-2^{k}=2^{k}$ telescoped over a run of ones:

$$\sum_{i=a}^{b}2^i=2^{b+1}-2^{a}$$

so a run of ones from weight $a$ to $b$ **is** a subtract-add pair: add $2^{b+1}$, subtract $2^a$. Per-digit, that identity is the single rewrite $x=2x-x$ applied to every *odd-weight* bit of $X$:

$$
X=\dots+x_52^5+x_42^4+x_32^3+x_22^2+x_12^1+x_0
=\dots+(2x_5-x_5)2^5+x_42^4+(2x_3-x_3)2^3+x_22^2+(2x_1-x_1)2^1+x_0
$$

Now **factor $2^{2j}$ out of each group of three consecutive bits** — distributivity again, this time pulling the common power out:

$$X=\dots+2x_52^5+2^4\underbrace{(-2x_5+x_4+x_3)}_{Z_2}+2^2\underbrace{(-2x_3+x_2+x_1)}_{Z_1}+\underbrace{(-2x_1+x_0)}_{Z_0}$$

Pad with $x_{-1}=0$ on the right and zeros on the left so that every digit depends on exactly three consecutive bits (the padding is optimized away by synthesis):

$$X=\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}Z_j2^{2j},\qquad Z_j=-2x_{2j+1}+x_{2j}+x_{2j-1},\qquad m=\left\lfloor\frac{w}{2}\right\rfloor+1 \tag{8.22, p.218}$$

The form $-2x_{2j+1}+x_{2j}+x_{2j-1}$ *is* the proof that $Z_j\in\{-2,\dots,2\}$ — the maximum of $x_{2j}+x_{2j-1}$ is $2$, the minimum of $-2x_{2j+1}$ is $-2$, and the value $3$ is unreachable. All $Z_j$ are computed from the $x_i$ **fully in parallel, in constant time**. Table 8.2 (p. 218) is a consequence, not a definition.

*Check with $X=123_{10}=0\,\underline{01}\,\underline{11}\,\underline{10}\,\underline{11}\!\cdot\!0$:* $Z_0=-1,Z_1=-1,Z_2=0,Z_3=2$, and $-1+4(-1)+16(0)+64(2)=123$ ✓ (p. 218).

**Realization.** Recoding and the multiplication $Z_jY$ fuse into three control signals (p. 219):

$$\mathrm{cpl}_j=x_{2j+1},\qquad \mathrm{one}_j=x_{2j}\oplus x_{2j-1},\qquad \mathrm{two}_j=\overline{x_{2j+1}}x_{2j}x_{2j-1}\vee x_{2j+1}\overline{x_{2j}}\,\overline{x_{2j-1}}$$

A mux selects $Y$ or $2Y$ (a wire shift), an XOR bank inverts it when $\mathrm{cpl}_j=1$, and $\mathrm{cpl}_j$ itself is added at the LSB position of $4^jZ_jY$ to complete the $-z=\bar z+1$ negation. The two AND gates per bit of a plain multiplier become an OR and an XOR — **Booth's real overhead is $m\times w_Y$ gates plus the recoder, in exchange for halving the array.**

Sign management: every $Z_jY$ can be negative, so rows need sign extension *even for unsigned* multiplication (p. 219); the same reduction as before absorbs it. For signed $X$ with $w$ even, the top three bits $x_{w-1}x_{w-2}x_{w-3}$ are already Booth-encoded because $x_{w-1}$ carries negative weight — no padding, no $x=2x-x$ on it, and $m=w/2$, one digit *less* than unsigned. For $w$ odd, sign-extend $X$ by one bit to reduce to the even case (p. 221).

### Higher radix

Radix-8 needs $\{-4,\dots,4\}$, which still contains the factor 3; radix-16 needs $\{-8,\dots,8\}$, containing 3, 5 and 7. Each requires a real addition, and a carry-save adder cannot serve because it returns each partial product line as *two* lines — negating the point of a higher radix. That is the main reason radices above 4 are not used (p. 222). Bewick's radix-8 variant keeps the $Z_jY$ in high-radix carry-save form built from small RCAs: the bit-heap overhead is one carry bit per $k$ sum bits, and the delay overhead is one $k$-bit RCA.

## Multiplier construction for FPGAs

DSP blocks are the best answer whenever they match the required precision. Five situations force an application-specific multiplier (pp. 222–223): the multiplier is much **smaller** than the DSP (waste); much **larger** (must combine several DSPs and logic); **too few** DSPs available; DSP **routing delay** dominates for small multipliers; or **not all output bits are used**, in which case build a truncated multiplier.

### One DSP for multiple multiplications

Compute $P=A\times C$ and $Q=B\times C$ — one shared operand, as in 8-bit ML inference — with a single large $R=X\times Y$. Pack:

$$X=A+2^kB,\qquad Y=C\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R=A\times C+2^k\,B\times C$$

Since $0\le P<2^{w_P}$, the two products do not interfere as soon as $k\ge w_P$. Unsigned: $A+2^kB$ is *just a concatenation* (no adder), and

$$P=R[w_P-1\ldots0],\qquad Q=R[w_P+w_Q\ldots w_P]$$

Two 8-bit multiplications fit one $18\times27$ DSP with $k=w_P=16$ (Fig. 8.11, p. 224). Signed is trickier: $A$ must be sign-extended, so $A+2^kB$ needs a real addition — use the DSP's **pre-adder**, $R=(X_0+X_1)\times Y$ with $X_1=A$ sign-extended and $X_2=2^kB$ (p. 224). Now $Q$ overlaps the sign extension of $P$, a $w_Q$-bit vector equal to $0$ or $2^{w_Q}-1$ depending on $p_{w_P-1}$:

$$Q'=Q+p_{w_P-1}(2^{w_Q}-1)\;\Longrightarrow\;Q=Q'-p_{w_P-1}(2^{w_Q}-1)=Q'+p_{w_P-1}$$

because $-(2^{w_Q}-1)$ in two's complement on $w_Q$ bits is $1$. The correction is a single bit, and the sign of $P$ is precomputable as $p_{w_P-1}=b_{w_A-1}\oplus b_{w_B-1}$ (pp. 225–226). Feed it to the post-adder, or fold it into $X_1$'s sign extension.

### Multiplier construction as a tiling problem

View the multiplier as the $w_X\times w_Y$ **rectangle** of partial products. A *tile* is any $m\times n$ sub-rectangle; its content is the weighted sum of the partial products it covers, computable by a smaller $m\times n$ multiplier. A multiplier is built by **tiling the rectangle so every position is covered exactly once** — coverage is mandatory, overlap is forbidden (p. 227). Tiles may extend *outside* the rectangle: that is zero-padding the sub-multiplier's inputs. A tile's inputs are read off geometrically, as the projections of its extent onto the $x$ and $y$ axes; its output weight is $2^{x+y}$ where $(x,y)$ is its **lower-right corner**, so the sub-products are shifted by $x+y$ and dropped into the compressor tree (Fig. 8.14, p. 227). For signed multipliers, only the tiles touching an argument's MSB are signed in that dimension — the rest are unsigned $\times$ unsigned.

**Efficiency metric** (p. 228): $E_s=\mathrm{area}_s/\mathrm{cost}_s$ where area is the tile's bit count. The LUT cost includes the compression the tile's output causes:

$$\mathrm{cost}^\mathrm{LUT}_s=\#\mathrm{LUT}^\mathrm{mult}_s+\#\mathrm{LUT}^\mathrm{comp}\cdot w_s,\qquad \#\mathrm{LUT}^\mathrm{comp}=0.55\ \text{LUT/bit on AMD}$$

| Tile (AMD, Table 8.3, p. 229) | area | $\mathrm{cost}^\mathrm{LUT}$ | $\mathrm{cost}^\mathrm{DSP}$ | $E^\mathrm{LUT}$ | $E^\mathrm{DSP}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $1\times1$ | 1 | 1.55 | 0 | 0.65 | – |
| $1\times2$ | 2 | 2.1 | 0 | 0.95 | – |
| $3\times3$ | 9 | 9.3 | 0 | 0.97 | – |
| $2\times3$ | 6 | 5.75 | 0 | 1.04 | – |
| $2\times k$ (carry chain) | $2k$ | $1.55k+2.1$ | 0 | $\tfrac{2k}{1.55k+2.1}$ | – |
| $24\times17$ (DSP) | 408 | 22.55 | 1 | 18.13 | 408 |
| super-tile, 2 DSP (a–d) | 816 | 23.1 | 2 | 35.32 | 408 |
| super-tile, 2 DSP (e–l) | 816 | 31.9 | 2 | 25.58 | 408 |

A $3\times3$ tile is one LUT6 per output bit ($E\approx0.97$); a $2\times3$ exploits LUT6-as-two-LUT5 and beats it at $1.04$; the $1\times1$ exists only to plug gaps and guarantee a solution always exists. The **$2\times k$ carry-chain tile** — a slice-mapped Baugh–Wooley row, two bit-products per LUT summed directly by the fast carry chain — reaches $E=1.05$ already at $k=6$ and tends to $1.29$ (p. 231). Super-tiles chain two DSPs through the post-adder, but only for relative shifts of $0$ or $17$ bits, the only value the dedicated routing allows (p. 232); chaining more DSPs keeps raising latency, so the book stops at two.

### Solving the tiling problem

Given a set $\mathcal{S}$ of sub-multipliers with costs $\mathrm{cost}_s$ and a shape $M_{x,y}\in\{0,1\}$ (not necessarily rectangular — truncated multipliers are not), find the minimum-cost tiling covering every $M_{x,y}=1$ exactly once. Tiling a multiplier with square blocks generalizes a known **NP-complete** problem (p. 234), so heuristics dominated until an exact ILP appeared:

$$
\min\sum_{s}\sum_{x}\sum_{y}\mathrm{cost}_s\,d^s_{x,y}
\quad\text{s.t.}\quad
\sum_{s}\sum_{x'\le x}\sum_{y'\le y}m^s_{x-x',y-y'}\,d^s_{x',y'}=1
$$

with $d^s_{x,y}$ true when tile $s$ is placed at $(x,y)$ and $m^s$ its shape mask. The constraint reads $=1$: $0$ means an uncovered position, $>1$ an overlap — both illegal. It is a binary ILP; it scales to $64\times64$. Add $\sum\mathrm{cost}^\mathrm{DSP}_s d^s_{x,y}\le\#\mathrm{DSP}$ to cap DSP usage and sweep the LUT/DSP trade-off directly (p. 235). Beyond $64\times64$, use the beam-search heuristic that also handles Karatsuba tiles.

## Truncated multipliers

If $\ell_P>\ell_X+\ell_Y$, some output bits are unused. Two levels of aggression:

1. **Correctly rounded**: build the exact multiplier, add a constant $1$ at position $\ell_P-1$ into the array, truncate the compressed result at $\ell_P$. Round-to-nearest-ties-up for the price of *one constant bit* — almost the same area and delay as exact.
2. **Truncated**: relax the specification to *faithful* ([[1.2) Error Accuracy#Faithful Rounding]]) and then **do not compute the low partial products at all** — saving resources, delay, and energy.

### Plain and Booth truncated multipliers

A truncated multiplier is an instance of the truncated bit heap of [[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits#Truncating a Bit Heap for Last-Bit Accuracy]]: keep $g=\ell_P-\ell_\mathrm{ext}$ guard columns below $\ell_P$, drop everything below $\ell_\mathrm{ext}$, and compensate with a constant. The number of guard bits needed for faithfulness:

| guard bits $g$ | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | 9 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| plain, $-\ell_P=w$ | 3–5 | 6–10 | 11–19 | 20–36 | 37–69 | 70–134 | 135–263 | 264–520 |
| Booth, $-\ell_P=w$ | 3–6 | 7–15 | 16–32 | 33–65 | 66–130 | 131–259 | 260–516 | 517–1029 |

*Table 8.4, p. 239.* Closed form for the plain case: $g$ guard bits suffice for $w<2^g+g$. The table is valid for signed and unsigned alike — signedness only touches the *leading* bits, which truncation never sees. **Booth recoding buys one extra truncated column**, for the simple reason that halving the number of rows halves $\Delta^{minor}$, the weighted sum of the truncated bits (p. 238).

### Optimal tiling for truncated multipliers on FPGAs

Truncating by guard bits gives the triangular tiling area of Fig. 8.22 (p. 240) — feed that shape as $M_{x,y}$ to the ILP and it is solved. But that is not optimal (p. 239): it never truncates the rightmost column, and a tile that spills *outside* the required area contributes bits that partially compensate the error of removing bits *inside* it. Better to let the ILP choose the uncovered set. Relax the coverage constraint to $=b_{x,y}$ with $b_{x,y}\in\{0,1\}$ (still no overlaps). An uncovered position contributes a negative error, so

$$\overline{\delta}_{x,y}=(1-b_{x,y})2^{x+y},\qquad \delta_\mathrm{tiling}=\sum_{x}\sum_{y}\overline{\delta}_{x,y}$$

Add a compensation constant $C$ to the heap to make the error two-sided, and require $|C-\delta_\mathrm{tiling}|\le\overline{\delta}_{\tilde P}=2^{\ell_P-1}$. Split into the two linear cases $\delta_\mathrm{tiling}=0$ and $\delta_\mathrm{tiling}=\overline{\delta}_\mathrm{tiling}$:

$$\text{C2: } C<\overline{\delta}_{\tilde P}\qquad\qquad \text{C3: } \sum_x\sum_y(1-b_{x,y})2^{x+y}-C<\overline{\delta}_{\tilde P}$$

and count $C$'s nonzero bits in the objective ($C=\sum2^ic_i$, $c_i\in\{0,1\}$, $\ell_\mathrm{ext}\le i\le\ell-2$), each costing $\#\mathrm{LUT}^\mathrm{comp}$ (pp. 241–242). **The book's worked instance** — $32\times32$ truncated to 32 output bits, Fig. 8.24, p. 243: $C=2\,013\,265\,920$ (four constant ones in the heap), $\delta_\mathrm{tiling}=4\,152\,360\,961$, so $|C-\delta_\mathrm{tiling}|=1\,954\,545\,664$, just under $\overline{\delta}_{\tilde P}=2^{31}=2\,147\,483\,648$. Shortening any $2\times k$ tile further would add at least $2^{27}+2^{28}=402\,653\,184$ and break the bound. The refined model reaches roughly the same outer shape as the guard-bit version but covers/uncovers positions inside it so that efficient $2\times k$ tiles replace the wasteful $1\times1$s.

## The Karatsuba method

The first sub-quadratic multiplication method: it trades sub-multiplications for additions, which are cheaper.

### Two-part splitting

Split both operands in two halves of $w$ bits, $X=2^wX_1+X_0$, $Y=2^wY_1+Y_0$:

$$X\times Y=\underbrace{X_1Y_1}_{M4}2^{2w}+(\underbrace{X_1Y_0}_{M3}+\underbrace{X_0Y_1}_{M2})2^{w}+\underbrace{X_0Y_0}_{M1}$$

Four $w\times w$ multiplications. The identity is: expand $(X_0+X_1)(Y_0+Y_1)$ by **distributivity**,

$$(X_0+X_1)(Y_0+Y_1)=X_0Y_0+X_0Y_1+X_1Y_0+X_1Y_1$$

and note the two *unwanted* terms are exactly $M1$ and $M4$, which are already being computed for the other columns. Subtract them:

$$X_1Y_0+X_0Y_1=(X_0+X_1)(Y_0+Y_1)-X_0Y_0-X_1Y_1 \tag{8.55, p.244}$$

The middle column is **recovered by subtraction rather than by multiplication** — that is the whole trick, and it is why the diagonal products must be computed anyway for the method to be free. Three multiplications instead of four, paid for with two **pre-additions** $X_0+X_1$, $Y_0+Y_1$ and two **post-additions** (the two subtractions). The new product is $(w+1)\times(w+1)$, slightly wider than the $w\times w$ it replaces. Recursively, $T(2w)=3T(w)+\Theta(w)$ gives $T(w)=\Theta(w^{\log_23})=\Theta(w^{1.585})$ instead of $\Theta(w^2)$.

> [!note] Karatsuba improves area, degrades timing
> Post-additions merge into the final bit-array compression at small cost — they are just more terms in the heap of [[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]], and in carry-save form they are constant-time. **Pre-additions are the problem**: they need carry propagation, in front of the multiplier, on the critical path (p. 245).

### Subtractive formula

The alternative rewriting

$$X_1Y_0+X_0Y_1=(X_0-X_1)(Y_1-Y_0)+M1+M4$$

uses pre-*subtractions*, the same hardware cost as pre-additions. Both add one bit; the difference is that here the extra bit is a **sign bit**. On FPGAs whose DSP multipliers are natively signed — where unsigned use wastes the sign bit by forcing it to 0 — the subtractive form is preferred, because it consumes a bit that would otherwise be thrown away (p. 245).

### Tile representation

In the tiling picture, the four sub-products of the 2-part splitting are four tiles. The pair replaced by one multiplier is drawn as two **linked empty squares**; the two reused diagonal products as **black squares** (Fig. 8.26, p. 245). This makes Karatsuba just another (non-connected, multi-part) tile the ILP can place, and it is how the generalizations below are derived graphically instead of algebraically.

### Square K-part splitting

Split into $K$ chunks of $w$ bits: $X\times Y=\sum_{i,j}2^{(i+j)w}X_iY_j$. Any pair of terms with the same weight collapses:

$$X_iY_j+X_jY_i=(X_i+X_j)(Y_i+Y_j)-X_iY_i-X_jY_j$$

the generalization of the two-part identity, with the novelty that the diagonal products $X_iY_i$ can be **reused more than once**. For $K=3$ the rewriting applies three times, cutting $3\times3=9$ sub-multipliers to **6**. In general $K$-part splitting saves the triangle of $K(K-1)/2$ multiplications — $K=4$: 16 → 10 (Fig. 8.27, p. 248). Counting graphically: number of tiles = linked pairs + black squares.

### Recursive Karatsuba

Apply the splitting to the sub-multipliers themselves. A $4w\times4w$ product becomes three $(2w+1)\times(2w+1)$ products, each in turn three $(w+1)\times(w+1)$ products: **9 small multipliers, versus 10 for the flat 4-part splitting**. The price is two pre-additions **in sequence** on the critical path (p. 248). Recursion is the standard choice in software (GMP) where multiplications cost time; in hardware it is a straight latency-vs-multiplier-count trade.

### Generalized formula

The Karatsuba pattern can be *shifted anywhere* on the rectangle, like any other tile. Any two tiles sharing the same weight — $i+j=k+\ell=s$, so the product contains $2^{sw}(X_iY_j+X_kY_\ell)$ — can be paired:

$$X_iY_j+X_kY_\ell=(X_i+X_k)(Y_j+Y_\ell)-X_iY_\ell-X_kY_j$$

(or the subtractive $=(X_i-X_k)(Y_j-Y_\ell)+X_iY_\ell+X_kY_j$), assuming $X_iY_\ell$ and $X_kY_j$ are already computed. For square multipliers this is weaker than $K$-part splitting, which exploits diagonal reuse; its value is that it applies to **rectangular and truncated** multipliers (p. 249).

### Karatsuba with rectangular sub-multipliers

DSP blocks are rectangular ($17\times24$ on AMD DSP48E1); using them as $17\times17$ squares to run classic Karatsuba wastes a third of each block. Split $X$ into $w_X$-bit chunks and $Y$ into $w_Y$-bit ones; the weight of $X_iY_j$ is $2^{w_Xi+w_Yj}$, so a pairing needs

$$w_Xi+w_Yj=w_Xk+w_Y\ell\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad w_XN-w_YM=0,\qquad N,M\in\mathbb{N}$$

whose smallest solution is $M=w_X/w$, $N=w_Y/w$ with $w=\gcd(w_X,w_Y)$. The first common weight is $2^{wNM}$, so the smallest multiplier that can benefit is $wM(N+1)\times wN(M+1)$ — **large $w$ is what makes the pattern small**, which is the opposite of intuition (p. 250).

- $17\times24$: $\gcd=1$, so $M=17$, $N=24$ and the smallest usable multiplier is $450\times450$ — saving one DSP out of 408. Useless.
- $16\times24$ (under-using the DSP by one bit): $\gcd=8$, $M=2$, $N=3$, smallest pattern $64\times72$. **This is the configuration to use.**
- $18\times24$ (one row done in logic): $w=6$, $M=3$, $N=4$, pattern $90\times96$.
- $15\times25$: $w=5$, $M=3$, $N=5$, pattern $90\times100$.

Unlike the square case the rectangular patterns leave **gaps**, filled by further shifted copies of the same pattern (Fig. 8.30, p. 253). Operation counts for comparable sizes (Table 8.6, p. 254): classic square Karatsuba needs $6/6/6$ (mult/pre-add/post-add) at $51\times51$, $10/12/22$ at $68\times68$, $21/30/51$ at $102\times102$, $28/42/70$ at $119\times119$. Rectangular Karatsuba: $6/0/6$ at $48\times48$ (versus 6 for plain tiling — no gain), $11/2/13$ at $64\times72$ (versus 12), $18/5/30$ at $96\times96$ (versus 24), $27/7/43$ at $112\times120$ (versus 35). It wins on multipliers everywhere except $68\times68$, and wins big on adder count because the larger tiles produce fewer sub-products. Pre-adders are further **shared**: only 5 of 12 pre-additions differ at $96\times96$, 7 of 16 at $112\times120$ — impossible for traditional Karatsuba, where every pre-addition is distinct (p. 255).

## Trade-offs

| Variant | Partial products | DSP | LUT | Latency | Accuracy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Plain array, radix-2 | $w_X w_Y$ bits, $w_X$ rows | 0 | $E^\mathrm{LUT}\le1.04$ for $\le3\times3$ tiles; $1.05$ at $2\times6$, $\to1.29$ for $2\times k$ (p. 229, 231) | $\mathcal{O}(\log w)$ with a compressor tree; $\mathcal{O}(w)$ if array-fused (p. 214) | exact |
| Booth radix-4 | $\lfloor w/2\rfloor+1$ rows ($w/2$ if signed, $w$ even) — array almost halved (p. 219) | 0 | plain multiplier's 2 ANDs/bit become 1 OR + 1 XOR, plus $\mathrm{cpl/one/two}$ recoders $\times m$ (p. 219) | $\mathcal{O}(\log w)$; recoding is constant time (p. 218) | exact |
| DSP-tiled | tiles, not bits: $24\times17$ covers area 408 | 1/tile; $24\times24$ in 1 DSP, $53\times53$ in 8 (Figs. 8.19–8.20) | 22.55 per DSP tile; $E^\mathrm{LUT}$ 18.13, up to 35.32 for a 2-DSP super-tile (p. 229) | +1 DSP pipeline stage per super-tile chain link (p. 232) | exact |
| Truncated | drop everything below $\ell_\mathrm{ext}=\ell_P-g$; $g=3$ at $w=8$, $g=5$ at $w=32$, $g=4$ Booth at $w=32$ (p. 239) | fewer tiles for the same $w$ | + $\#\mathrm{LUT}^\mathrm{comp}$ per nonzero bit of $C$ (4 bits for $32\times32\to32$, p. 243) | shorter — smaller heap, shorter carry chains | **faithful**, error $<1\,$ulp$\,=2^{\ell_P}$ |
| Karatsuba | 3 instead of 4 tiles per pair; $K$-part saves $K(K-1)/2$ | $E^\mathrm{DSP}$ 385.3 / 433.5 / 462.4 (square 2/3/4-part), 512 / 576 / 614.4 (rect. 2/3/4-part) vs 408 plain (p. 254) | $E^\mathrm{LUT}$ drops to 9.7–12.2 from 18.13 — extra adders (p. 254) | **worse**: pre-additions carry-propagate ahead of the multiplier; recursion stacks them in sequence (pp. 245, 248) | exact |

**Crossovers, from the book's own numbers.** With square $17\times17$ tiles, 2-part Karatsuba has $E^\mathrm{DSP}=385.3<408$ — it is *worse than not doing Karatsuba at all*. Classic Karatsuba only starts paying at **3-part splitting** ($E^\mathrm{DSP}=433.5$), i.e. from $51\times51$ upward. With rectangular $16\times24$ tiles it pays from the **2-part splitting already** ($512>408$), whose smallest instance is $64\times72$ — where it saves 1 DSP out of 12. Try to pair native $17\times24$ tiles instead and the smallest beneficiary is $450\times450$, saving 1 DSP out of 408: not worth building. Pick a plain radix-2 array for small $w$ or when LUT tiles beat everything ($2\times k$ carry-chain tiles are the densest logic option). Pick Booth radix-4 in ASIC flows where halving the compressor tree dominates and there is no DSP to tile onto — on FPGAs the LUT tiling usually wins instead. Pick DSP tiling whenever DSPs exist and $w>17$; cap `maxDSP` and let the ILP move the balance. Add truncation whenever the specification does not need the low output bits — it is nearly free and it shrinks everything downstream. Reach for Karatsuba only above $\sim64\times72$ with rectangular tiles, when DSPs are the scarce resource and you can afford the pre-adder delay.

## Worked example: $8\times8$ signed, three ways

$X=11011011_2=-37$, $Y=00110101_2=+53$, exact $P=-1961=1111100001010111_2$ on 16 bits.

**Radix-2 with sign extension.** By (8.16), $X\times Y=-2^7x_7Y+\sum_{i=0}^{6}2^ix_iY$ with $x=x_7\ldots x_0=1,1,0,1,1,0,1,1$:

```text
  i=0  0000000000110101   +53      (x0=1)
  i=1  0000000001101010   +106     (x1=1)
  i=2  ----------------     0      (x2=0)
  i=3  0000000110101000   +424     (x3=1)
  i=4  0000001101010000   +848     (x4=1)
  i=5  ----------------     0      (x5=0)
  i=6  0000110101000000   +3392    (x6=1)
       ----------------
       0001001011010111   +4823    (positive rows)
  i=7  1110010110000000   -6784  = ~(2^7 Y) + 1     (x7=1, subtracted)
       ================
       1111100001010111   -1961   ✓
```

Eight rows, one of them negated via $-z=\bar z+1$; the four leading zeros of $Y$ replicated up each row are the sign extensions that the reduction of [[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]] collapses into constant bits.

**Radix-4 Booth.** $w=8$ is even and $X$ is signed, so $m=w/2=4$ digits. With $x_{-1}=0$ and $Z_j=-2x_{2j+1}+x_{2j}+x_{2j-1}$:

| $j$ | $x_{2j+1}x_{2j}x_{2j-1}$ | $Z_j$ | $Z_jY\cdot4^j$ | on 16 bits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | $110$ | $-1$ | $-53$ | `1111111111001011` |
| 1 | $101$ | $-1$ | $-212$ | `1111111100101100` |
| 2 | $011$ | $+2$ | $+1696$ | `0000011010100000` |
| 3 | $110$ | $-1$ | $-3392$ | `1111001011000000` |

Check the recoding: $-1+4(-1)+16(2)+64(-1)=-1-4+32-64=-37=X$ ✓. Check the product: $-53-212+1696-3392=-1961$ ✓. **Four rows instead of eight** — the array is halved. Every row is a shift of $Y$ ($Z=\pm1$) or of $2Y$ ($Z=\pm2$), never $3Y$; the three negative rows are XOR-inverted with $\mathrm{cpl}_j=1$ and their $\mathrm{cpl}_j$ bit is dropped into the heap at position $2j$ to finish the negation.

**Truncated variant.** Same bit patterns as unsigned ($A=219$, $B=53$, $A\times B=11607$), output truncated to 8 bits: $\ell_P=8$, $u=2^8=256$. Table 8.4 with $w=8$ (range 6–10) gives $g=3$, so $\ell_\mathrm{ext}=\ell_P-g=5$ and columns of weight $2^0\ldots2^4$ are **not computed at all** — 15 of the 64 AND gates, 23% of the array, plus their compression.

*After Figs. 8.21–8.22, pp. 238–240.* Worst-case dropped weight $\overline{\delta}_\mathrm{tiling}=1\!\cdot\!1+2\!\cdot\!2+3\!\cdot\!4+4\!\cdot\!8+5\!\cdot\!16=129$, and the budget is $\overline{\delta}_{\tilde P}=2^{\ell_P-1}=128$ — so $C=0$ *fails* by one. Take $C=64$ (a single constant bit, and $\ell_\mathrm{ext}\le6\le\ell_P-2$ as required by C4): $\max|C-\delta_\mathrm{tiling}|=\max(64,\,129-64)=65\le128$ ✓. For these operands the actually-dropped weight is $\delta_\mathrm{tiling}=\sum_{i+j\le4}x_iy_j2^{i+j}=1+2+4+16+32=55$, so the heap holds $11607-55=11552$; add $C=64$ and the round bit $2^{\ell_P-1}=128$, giving $11744$; truncate at $\ell_P$: $\lfloor11744/256\rfloor=45$.

$$\text{delivered }45\times256=11520,\qquad \text{exact }11607,\qquad \text{error}=\frac{87}{256}=0.34\ \text{ulp}$$

Worst case: $|C-\delta_\mathrm{tiling}|\le65<128$ from the dropped bits, plus $\le128$ from the final rounding, total $<256=1\,$ulp — **faithful**, per [[1.2) Error Accuracy#Faithful Rounding]]. At $w=8$ the saving is 23% of a tiny array; the technique only really pays at $w\ge32$, where the dropped triangle is a large fraction of the rectangle (Fig. 8.21, p. 238).
