**Specification.** Input: an *operator name* plus a list of `name=value` parameters, split into a **functional specification** (input format, output format, operator-specific parameters — what mathematical function, at what accuracy) and a **performance specification** (target FPGA, target frequency, operator-specific controls). Output: a `flopoco.vhdl` file of synthesizable VHDL, optionally a self-checking test bench and a `test.input` vector file. Accuracy promised: whatever the operator's own specification says — usually **last-bit accurate (faithful, error $<1$ ulp)**, sometimes correctly rounded; the test bench is generated from that specification, not from the VHDL. Optimized: whatever the performance parameters ask for — area, or a stated target frequency, with the tool inserting pipeline registers to hit it (pp. 761–764).

That two-way split *is* the tool. Every other generator in the field bakes one of the two halves into the source; FloPoCo keeps both on the command line, which is what makes "computing just right" ([[1.2) Error Accuracy]]) mechanically expressible.

## Derivation: what "frequency-directed" actually computes

Let $\Delta t(x)$ be the delay of a combinatorial operator on input $x$, and $\overline{\Delta t}=\max_x \Delta t(x)$ its **critical path delay**. Sandwiched between input and output registers, the operator accepts a new input every $\overline{\Delta t}$, so it runs at

$$f_{\text{comb}}=\frac{1}{\overline{\Delta t}}.$$

Split the circuit graph into $N$ *pipeline stages*, each of critical path $\approx\overline{\Delta t}/N$, with registers between stages. Ignoring register delay, the frequency becomes $N/\overline{\Delta t}$ — multiplied by $N$, at the cost of the extra registers (p. 768). In practice register setup/propagation delay is not ignorable and caps $N$ usefully (the classic 6–8 FO4 optimum, [Hri+02; SC02], p. 768).

The user does not state $N$. The user states $f$, and the tool solves for $N$. That is the inversion: in a normal RTL or HLS flow you write registers (or a schedule) and then *measure* the frequency you got; here the frequency is an input and the register placement is the output.

**Circuit time (p. 774).** Combinatorially, the timing of a signal $s$ is the accumulated delay along the longest simple path from the earliest input to $s$. Pipelined, it becomes a pair

$$t(s)=(c,\tau),$$

- $c\in\mathbb{N}$: number of registers on the longest path from an input to $s$;
- $\tau\in\mathbb{R}$: critical path delay in seconds since the last register (or the earliest input).

ordered **lexicographically**:

$$(c_1,\tau_1)>(c_2,\tau_2)\iff c_1>c_2\ \text{ or }\ (c_1=c_2\ \text{and}\ \tau_1>\tau_2).$$

The scheduler's job is exactly: assign a lexicographic time to every signal. For a signal $s$ produced from predecessors $p_i$ with a designer-declared delay $\delta$, the natural rule is $t(s)=\max_i t(p_i)\oplus\delta$, where $\oplus$ adds $\delta$ to $\tau$ and carries into $c$ whenever $\tau$ would exceed the cycle budget $1/f$ — that carry *is* the register insertion. The maximum is taken in the lexicographic order, which is why a signal arriving one cycle later dominates a signal that is merely slower within the same cycle. Drawn on one chain of LUT levels, with the delays of the worked example below, that carry is the entire mechanism:

> [!important] Two software–hardware correspondences hold the whole tool together
> The **`BitHeap` class is the software realization of [[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]]** — a weighted bit is literally a (signal name, position, arrival time) record, a column is a list sorted by arrival time, and `startCompression()` runs the compressor-tree optimization of Sect. 7.3 and emits the hardware (pp. 775–777). The **`Target` class hierarchy is the software realization of [[1.3) FPGAs]]** — `lutInputs()` exposes the LUT-$\alpha$ of the chip, `adderDelay(n)` exposes its carry chain (p. 771). Every architectural decision the book derives from "a LUT has $\alpha$ inputs" or "a bit heap column can be compressed" reaches the generated VHDL through one of those two objects, and nowhere else.

## History of the FloPoCo project

Started modestly in 2008 [DKP09] as a generator for a few parametric **flo**ating **po**int **co**res — hence the name. It grew into a general research framework for hardware arithmetic cores: LNS arithmetic, random number generators, elementary functions, specialized operators (constant multiplication and division), FPGA-specific optimization techniques, posit arithmetic, and more recently signal-processing transforms, filters, and neural networks. It is also used as the VHDL code-generation backend for the Origami HLS tool and the Elysia neural-network code generator (pp. 761–762). Most of the research synthesized in the book was implemented there first; the project lives at `http://flopoco.org/`, with an experimental web interface if you do not want to build the C++.

The successive advertising slogans, quoted at the head of the appendix, are a compressed history of the ambition: *"When FPGAs are better at floating point than microprocessors" → "All the operators you will never see in a microprocessor" → "Circuits computing just right"*.

## FloPoCo from a user point of view

### Overview

An executable with a plain command-line interface. An operator specification is an operator name followed by parameter values:

```sh
flopoco IEEEFPAdd wE=8 wF=23
```

writes `flopoco.vhdl`, synthesizable VHDL for an IEEE-754 binary32 adder (p. 762). Some operators are a single entity; most are a hierarchy. The command above reports five subcomponents plus a top level, the **last** entity in the file:

```
|---Entity RightShifterSticky26_by_max_25_comb_uid4
|---Entity IntAdder_27_comb_uid6
|---Entity LZC_26_comb_uid8
|---Entity LeftShifter27_by_max_26_comb_uid10
|---Entity IntAdder_31_comb_uid13
Entity IEEEFPAdd_8_23_comb_uid2
```

Read that list next to the datapath in [[2.7) Floating-Point Operators]]: shifter, adder, leading-zero counter, shifter, adder. The name encodes the parameters and the pipeline state (`_comb_` = combinatorial). Several operator specifications may share one command line, and global parameters (`frequency`, `target`) apply to all of them — a whole FPU in one call, all in one VHDL file (p. 763):

```sh
flopoco frequency=200 FPAdd wE=8 wF=23 FPMult we=8 wf=23 \
        FPDiv we=8 wf=23 FPSqrt we=8 wf=23
```

Output size ranges from a few bytes to a few megabytes: an addition can be one `+` character, a table of precomputed values written out in extension cannot (p. 763).

### More on parameters

All parameters are `name=value`. Every operator is heavily parameterized, and the parameter list is self-documenting: invoke the operator with no parameters at all, e.g. `flopoco FPAdd`, and it prints them (p. 763).

The general rule (p. 764): **functional parameters are mandatory with no default; performance parameters are optional with a sensible default.** Both directions have exceptions worth knowing, because they tell you where the tool refuses to guess:

- `FPAdd` has a `sub` parameter deciding adder vs. subtracter. Functional, but defaulted, because addition is the common case.
- `FixFunctionByPiecewisePoly` makes the polynomial *degree* mandatory although it is a performance parameter — the "sensible default" is judged too application-dependent to invent.

There are many parameter combinations that do not make sense. The tool filters what it can and emits an error, "but users often have more imagination than developers can anticipate, and invalid combinations of parameter values may lead to unexpected crashes or nonworking VHDL" (p. 764).

### Do not trust FloPoCo, the test bench is included

FloPoCo can generate an infinite number of operators; the developers have obviously not tested each one. This is not sloppiness, it is *intrinsic to application-specific arithmetic* — the whole point is that the operator you want has never been built before. The answer is that **each operator ships a test bench generator that exercises exactly the operator being generated** (p. 764).

Why the test bench is more trustworthy than the circuit (p. 765):

1. Test vectors are **not** derived from the generated VHDL. They come from the operator's mathematical specification — a function composed with a well-defined rounding, per the book's definition of an operator (Sect. 1.3) — implemented independently in C++. A VHDL bug therefore has to be *mirrored* by an independent bug in the reference model to go unnoticed.
2. The reference code is small, boilerplate-heavy, and built on GMP and MPFR (correctly rounded multiple-precision floating point). Those libraries are far better specified and far more exercised than any generated datapath.

So the confidence in the test bench is genuinely higher than the confidence in the VHDL — which is the practical cash-out of [[1.2) Error Accuracy]]. An accuracy contract stated in ulp is only worth something if it is machine-checkable; here the same contract that drove the architecture ("faithful to $\ell_{\text{out}}$") is the oracle that checks it.

```sh
flopoco IntConstDiv wIn=16 d=3 TestBench
```

produces the VHDL plus a human-readable `test.input` containing **all** $2^{16}$ inputs with expected outputs, and prints the invocations for several simulators (p. 765). For large word sizes, exhaustive is out of reach:

```sh
flopoco FPAdd we=8 wf=23 TestBench n=100000
```

emits 100 000 random tests — but the sequence *begins* with every corner case the `FPAdd` developers could think of, including regression vectors from past bugs. And the random generator is **operator-specific**: two uniformly random binary32 bit strings almost never cancel in an addition, since cancellation needs exponents differing by at most 1 (Sect. 11.1). The generator is deliberately skewed so cancellations are frequent, because cancellation is the interesting path in a floating-point adder (pp. 765–766). A generic random tester would have near-zero coverage of exactly the subtractive-cancellation logic that makes FP addition hard.

The recommended simulator is the open-source `nvc`; the pass criterion is the single line `0 error(s) encountered`, and on failure `gtkwave` waveforms are already written (p. 765).

### Obscure branches and code attics

The install instructions target the `master` branch, but the repository carries more: experimental branches and forks holding rarer operators, and **code snapshots that make a published paper reproducible** (p. 766). Developers are encouraged to merge into `master` as soon as work is generally useful, and to clean out experimental code, over-parameterization kept for research, and obsolete variants — and, the authors admit, generally do neither, for lack of time.

When a change to the framework or its libraries breaks an operator whose original developer has moved on, maintainers **disable rather than delete** it: the code is "just unplugged," parked in `attic/` directories or obscure branches where it may or may not still compile. Currently disabled but worth reviving: the HOTBM function approximator (Detrey), the LNS operators (Collange), and the FPGA-specific random number generators (Thomas) (p. 766). Worth knowing before concluding a technique from the book is unimplemented — check the attic.

### Automatic pipelining, the user point of view

Almost every operator in the book is **combinatorial**: no memory, output depends only on the input. That falls straight out of the definition of an operator as a mathematical function composed with a rounding — a function has no history. The directed graph of subcomponents is therefore acyclic (p. 766). The stated exceptions: the floating-point accumulators of [[5.2) Exact Accumulators]] and the filters of [[6.1) LTI Filters]]; the book also discusses iterative dividers, CORDIC, and digit-recurrence algorithms in general, but **FloPoCo provides only the unrolled, combinatorial variants** of those (p. 767, footnote 1). If you came here from [[5.1) CORDIC]] expecting a sequential iteration, that is the gap.

The user interface to pipelining is one global parameter: `frequency`. Points that matter in practice (p. 768):

- FloPoCo adds **no input or output registers**, only the intermediate ones. The reported "pipeline depth" counts *synchronization barriers*; depth 3 means 4 stages (Fig. A.3a). The same information appears in comments before each entity declaration.
- The pipeline is **not claimed optimal**. An optimal pipeline would minimize total register cost subject to the frequency constraint [LS91]; FloPoCo does not do this.
- It does **not guarantee** the frequency is met — backend synthesis is where the circuit actually becomes a circuit, and that is the proper place for fine tuning.
- It **does** guarantee the pipeline is correctly synchronized, and `TestBench` adapts itself to pipelined operators, so you can check.

Which makes the workflow: ask for a frequency, synthesize, measure, adjust the request. To measure honestly you must register the inputs and outputs, which is what `RegisterSandwich` is for — it sandwiches the operator *preceding it on the command line*, exactly like `TestBench` tests the preceding operator (p. 769):

```sh
flopoco frequency=400 FPAdd wE=8 wF=23 RegisterSandwich
```

The `tools/` directory holds small Python scripts that read the target out of the VHDL, drive Vivado or Quartus, and print the synthesis result (p. 769).

## FloPoCo for arithmetic designers

### Operators and instances

The core is the abstract `Operator` class: **every VHDL entity produced by FloPoCo is an `Operator`**. A run builds the list of `Operator` instances — those named on the command line plus all their subcomponents, transitively — then generates VHDL for them (p. 770). The class offers conveniences (declare a signal next to where it is assigned; hide VHDL boilerplate) and three deeper services: the `Target` hardware abstraction, the operator testing framework, and automatic pipelining. It is consequently large, and the book explicitly says it is a *bad* entry point — start from a well-documented operator such as `FPAdd` and look up `Operator` methods as you hit them.

At the center is the `vhdl` stream. Writing an operator is writing VHDL into it (Listing A.1, p. 770):

```cpp
vhdl << declare("signX") << " <= newX(" << wE+wF << ");";
vhdl << declare(target->logicDelay(), "effSub") << " <= signX xor signY;";
vhdl << declare(target->adderDelay(wE+1), "eXmeY", wE) << " <= (X"
     << range(wE+wF-1,wF) << ") - (Y" << range(wE+wF-1,wF) << ");";
```

producing

```vhdl
signX <= newX(31);
effSub <= signX xor signY;
eXmeY <= (X(30 downto 23)) - (Y(30 downto 23));
```

`declare()` registers a signal's **name, word size, and the delay of the operation that produces it**, and returns the name for the code generation. That third argument is the whole timing model: the delay is target-dependent, and it is what the pipeliner later consumes.

### The Target class hierarchy

A `Target` is passed to every `Operator` constructor, and a singleton `target` is reachable from inside `Operator`. `Target` is itself abstract; real chips (Xilinx/AMD, Altera/Intel) are subclasses. Generator code calls abstract methods, so **the same generator code adapts to every target** (p. 771). Two categories:

- **Architecture-related** — what the fabric *is*. `lutInputs()` returns the number of LUT inputs. Techniques designed around the LUT read it directly: Ken Chapman's KCM constant multiplier [Cha94] in [[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]], the small-constant dividers of [[3.2) Division by Constants]].
- **Delay-related** — how long things *take*. `logicDelay()` is one LUT (the delay of the `xor` above); `adderDelay(n)` is the delay of an $n$-bit addition. `adderDelay(16)` returns different values on different FPGAs, and a slower FPGA simply gets a deeper pipeline for the same requested frequency. This used to be a simple affine function of $n$; it no longer is, because LUTs are grouped into slices or LABs with different carry propagation *within* a group and *between* groups ([[1.3) FPGAs]]). It remains fairly accurate for any $n$ up to the diameter of the FPGA (p. 771, footnote 2).

Relying on the virtual `Target` is what makes FloPoCo datapaths future-proof: a new chip needs a new subclass, not new operators. Two escape hatches: an `Operator` may emit target-specific code after an explicit test on the target name; and embedded memories and DSP blocks — with internal registers, chaining, dual porting — are too irregular for the abstraction, so they are reached through the `Table` and `IntMultiplier` operators, inside which the authors "are not ashamed to have target- or vendor-specific ad hoc implementations" (p. 772). ### Automatic pipelining, the implementation

In principle synthesis tools could do all of this — the timing information is in the netlist, and optimal register placement is a solved problem [LS91]. In practice FPGA and ASIC synthesis have limited pipeline-balancing support, and FPGA HLS tools produce pipelines much deeper than a manual approach achieves (p. 772).

FloPoCo's obstruction is specific: it **ignores the semantics of what designers write into the `vhdl` stream**. Recovering that would require a complete VHDL compiler. So the burden is shifted — the designer models the timing of their own VHDL. The good news is that this model is expressed generically (through `Target`), so the operator can then be pipelined for arbitrary frequencies and arbitrary targets.

Operator design therefore splits cleanly (p. 772):

1. **Build a functional combinatorial datapath.** Concern: correctness — it passes `TestBench`. Performance metric: the critical path delay of the combinatorial operator, measured register-to-register with `RegisterSandwich`.
2. **Add timing information**: `target->logicDelay()`, `target->adderDelay()` calls. This does **not change the function computed**, and does not touch a line of the `vhdl` stream. The worst a poor timing model can do is give an inefficient pipeline — frequency missed, or more stages than needed.

That separation is the reason the approach is safe to use: step 2 cannot break step 1.

Pipelining is then three passes [ID17] (pp. 772–774):

1. **Parse** the `vhdl` stream for signal names, build the dependency graph, label it with the designer-provided timings. From Listing A.1, one edge records that `eXmeY` depends on `X` and `Y` and is available $\delta t$ seconds after both, where $\delta t$ is `adderDelay(wE+1)` on this target.
2. **Schedule**: assign a cycle to each signal. The current heuristic is as-soon-as-possible. This gives the *minimal number of cycles* but not the minimal number of registers; Leiserson–Saxe optimal retiming [LS91] would typically cut register cost by **20–30 %** [Par22] and is not implemented (p. 774, footnote 3).
3. **Generate**: emit VHDL from the scheduled graph and the `vhdl` stream, by delaying signals and rewriting right-hand-side occurrences with their delayed version. In the final VHDL, `effSub_d3` reads as "signal `effSub`, delayed by 3 cycles."

Setting the global parameter `dependencyGraph=compact` or `full` dumps the annotated graph (Fig. A.4, p. 773), colored by pipeline stage — the fastest way to see where your timing model is wrong.

### The BitHeap framework

Bit heaps ([[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]]) get first-class support, and the concept is **orthogonal to `Operator`** (p. 775):

- one large `Operator` commonly contains several bit heaps;
- one bit heap is sometimes **shared across several `Operator` instances**. The canonical case is the sum of products by constants (SOPC, Sect. 12.5.5, p. 412 — see [[3.1) Multiplication by Constants#Multiple Constant Multiplication]] and [[6.1) LTI Filters]]): each constant multiplier throws its bits onto a *common* heap and performs no compression of its own, so the whole sum is compressed once. Such a constant multiplier is then **not a VHDL entity at all** — it is a method taking a pointer to another `Operator` and writing into that operator's `vhdl` stream.

That is the software expression of the central bit-heap argument: compression is cheapest when it is done once, globally, over every bit in flight.

**Interface** (Table A.1, p. 776). The bits added are signals of the operator being generated; the initial heap may be any shape or size.

| Method | Effect |
|---|---|
| `addBit(string sigName, int pos)` | add a single bit at position `pos` |
| `addSignal(string sigName, int shift)` | add a fixed-point signal, sign-extending if needed |
| `subtractSignal(string name, int shift)` | subtract a fixed-point signal, sign-extending if needed |
| `addConstantOneBit(int pos)` | add a constant one bit |
| `addConstant(mpz_class cst, int pos)` | add the constant $\texttt{cst}\cdot 2^{\texttt{pos}}$ |
| `startCompression()` | generate the compressor tree |

When the argument is a signal name it refers to a FloPoCo `Signal`, which carries the fixed-point format: signedness, MSB, LSB. So sign extension is **automatic** — the framework knows the format, which is exactly the bookkeeping that makes hand-written bit-heap code error-prone. Usage is: create the heap, loop generating VHDL that defines signals and `addBit`/`addSignal` them (no VHDL is emitted by those calls), then one `startCompression()` which emits a lot of VHDL (Listing A.3, p. 776).

**Data structure** (p. 776). A *weighted bit* is essentially a signal name, a bit position, and the fields used when building the compressor tree. Because the `Signal` class encapsulates timing, **each weighted bit also carries its arrival time**, in the lexicographic circuit time $(c,\tau)$ above. A column is a list of weighted bits **sorted by arrival time**, so compression algorithms can compress the earliest-arrived bits first — which is how the heap-scheduling story of [[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]] and the pipelining story become the same story. The complete structure is its MSB, its LSB, and an array of columns indexed by position. Heaps can be dumped as SVG dot diagrams (as in Fig. 7.18, p. 173) where hovering a bit shows its signal name and arrival instant, and colors distinguish arrival times. The SOPC case makes the whole object visible at once — three constant multipliers, one heap, one compression:

**Compressor tree generation** — what `startCompression()` does (p. 777):

1. select the solving algorithm (a class derived from `CompressionStrategy`, chosen by the global `compression` parameter);
2. generate the list of possible, **target-dependent** compressors (`BasicCompressor` for the shape, `Compressor` for the hardware);
3. schedule the bits into stages according to their cycle and combinatorial arrival time;
4. optimize the compressor trees, producing a `BitHeapSolution` — the compressors used per stage and per column;
5. generate the VHDL.

New compression methods are added by extending `CompressionStrategy`; new compressor shapes by extending `BasicCompressor`.

## FPGA-specific mapping

Everything target-dependent funnels through three places, and it is worth knowing which one to touch:

| Concern | Mechanism | Where |
|---|---|---|
| LUT width, architectural exploration | `target->lutInputs()` | `Target` subclass, p. 771 |
| Combinatorial delays driving the pipeline | `target->logicDelay()`, `target->adderDelay(n)` | `Target` subclass, p. 771 |
| Carry-chain-specific structures | explicit operator, e.g. `XilinxTernaryAddSub` | operator, p. 148 |
| DSP blocks, block RAM (registers, chaining, dual port) | hidden inside `Table`, `IntMultiplier` | operator, p. 772 |
| Which compressors exist on this fabric | `BasicCompressor` list, generated per target | `startCompression()` step 2, p. 777 |
| Vendor synthesis, actual $f_{\max}$ and area | `tools/` Python scripts → Vivado/Quartus | p. 769 |

Selecting a chip is `target=virtex6` on the command line (p. 148). The generated entity name records the pipeline state, so `..._comb_uid4` versus a pipelined name tells you at a glance whether the frequency request took effect.

## Worked example: reading a scheduled dependency graph

Take `flopoco FPAdd wE=8 wF=23` pipelined for **400 MHz**, the case shown in Fig. A.4 (p. 773). The cycle budget is

$$T = \frac{1}{400\times 10^{6}} = 2.5\ \text{ns}.$$

The zoom in Fig. A.4a shows the four signals of Listing A.1, each labelled *name / declared delay / $(c,\tau)$*:

| Signal | Declared delay | $(c,\tau)$ |
|---|---|---|
| `signY` | $0$ | $(1,\ 1.3\times10^{-11})$ |
| `signX` | $0$ | $(1,\ 1.3\times10^{-11})$ |
| `sXsYExnXY` | $0$ | $(1,\ 1.3\times10^{-11})$ |
| `effSub` | $3.66\times10^{-10}$ | $(1,\ 3.79\times10^{-10})$ |

Check the arithmetic against the rule $t(s)=\max_i t(p_i)\oplus\delta$. `effSub <= signX xor signY` has two predecessors, both at $(1,\,13\ \text{ps})$; the lexicographic maximum is $(1,\,13\ \text{ps})$; adding the declared `logicDelay` of $366$ ps:

$$13\ \text{ps} + 366\ \text{ps} = 379\ \text{ps},$$

and $379\ \text{ps} < 2.5$ ns, so no carry into $c$: `effSub` lands at $(1,\ 379\ \text{ps})$. Exactly the label in the figure. One LUT costs about $366/2500 \approx 15\%$ of a cycle at this frequency, so roughly six levels of pure LUT logic fit per stage before a barrier is forced.

The bare-bit signals cost nothing (`signX` is a wire out of `newX`, delay $0$), yet they sit at $\tau = 13$ ps rather than $0$ — they inherit the arrival time of their predecessor, which is what keeps the lexicographic maximum meaningful. Further down the same graph a node with the same $366$ ps LUT delay appears at $(2,\ 1.51\times10^{-10})$: its predecessors had run the cycle budget out, the $\oplus$ carried, and a register was inserted. That single carry is every pipeline register in the design.

Error contributed by any of this: **zero ulp**. Pipelining is a purely temporal transformation — same acyclic graph, same function, same rounding, same `TestBench` verdict, which is precisely why the timing model is allowed to be approximate.

> [!note] What the graph tells you when synthesis disagrees
> If the backend reports a lower $f_{\max}$ than requested, the fault is in the *timing model*, not the schedule: some `declare()` under-declared its delay, and the ASAP scheduler happily packed too much into a stage. Dump `dependencyGraph=full`, find the stage whose accumulated $\tau$ is closest to $T$, and check the `Target` call on its longest edge.

> [!warning] Not covered in the source — needs outside research
> The appendix gives no area or delay numbers for generated operators: no LUT/slice counts, no achieved $f_{\max}$, no comparison against vendor IP. The only quantitative claim in it is the 20–30 % register saving that optimal retiming would bring [Par22] (p. 774). Per-operator cost tables live in the chapters the reference table above points to; measuring your own is what `RegisterSandwich` plus the `tools/` scripts are for.
