**Specification.** Input: an unsigned integer $X$ on $w$ bits (a $\mathrm{ufix}(w-1,0)$ in the language of [[1.1) Number Formats]]). Divisor: a compile-time integer constant $D$, not a power of two — powers of two are a wire shift. Output: the *exact* Euclidean pair
$$X = D\cdot Q + R,\qquad 0\le R < D$$
so $Q$ on $w - \lfloor\log_2 D\rfloor$ bits and $R$ on $r$ bits. **Accuracy is 0 ulp — there is no error budget to spend.** What we optimize is area (LUT/table bits) at a delay target, with $w$ ranging from 8 to 128 and $D$ small (3, 5, 9, 15, 45).

That zero-ulp specification is the whole story of this note. Every general divider in [[2.5) Dividers]] is exact too, but costs $O(w^2)$; knowing $D$ at synthesis time buys an $O(w)$ architecture, and knowing that $D$ is *small* buys a table that fits in one FPGA LUT per stage.

Notation follows the book: $k$ is the radix chunk size ($X$ and $Q$ are read in radix $2^k$), $m=\lceil w/k\rceil$ the digit count, $r$ the bitwidth of $D-1$ (equivalently of $D$, since $D$ is not a power of two), and $\alpha$ the input count of the target FPGA LUT.

## Multiplying by the reciprocal

$1/D$ has an infinite (eventually periodic) binary expansion, so we cannot just multiply by it. The Recip architecture [DCC12], following the software trick [Rob05], instead finds integers $A,B,p$ with

$$\left\lfloor \frac{X}{D}\right\rfloor = \left\lfloor\frac{AX+B}{2^p}\right\rfloor \qquad\forall X\in\{0,\dots,2^w-1\} \tag{13.1}$$

and then $Q$ is one constant multiplication ([[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]]) plus a truncation. The remainder, if wanted, is $R = X - DQ$: a second constant multiplication and a subtraction.

### When is (13.1) actually true?

Take $B=0$ and $A=\lceil 2^p/D\rceil$, so that $A/2^p$ over-approximates $1/D$. Define the relative slack
$$\varepsilon \;=\; \frac{A}{2^p}-\frac{1}{D} \;=\; \frac{AD-2^p}{D\,2^p},\qquad 0\le AD-2^p\le D-1$$
the bound on the numerator being the definition of the ceiling. Now split $X$ by the division identity $X = DQ+R$, so $X/D = Q + R/D$, and substitute:
$$\frac{AX}{2^p} \;=\; X\left(\frac1D+\varepsilon\right) \;=\; Q \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{R}{D} \;+\; \varepsilon X}_{\text{must stay in }[0,1)}$$
Flooring returns $Q$ **iff** that bracket lies in $[0,1)$. The lower bound is free ($\varepsilon\ge0$). The upper bound is worst at $R=D-1$ and $X=2^w-1$:

$$\frac{D-1}{D} + \varepsilon\,(2^w-1) < 1 \iff \varepsilon\,(2^w-1) < \frac{1}{D}$$

Substituting $\varepsilon \le (D-1)/(D\,2^p)$ gives the clean sufficient condition

$$\boxed{\;(D-1)(2^w-1) < 2^p\;}\qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad p \;\ge\; w + r$$

so $A$ needs $w_A = p - \lfloor\log_2 D\rfloor \approx w$ bits: a $w\times w$ constant multiplier, hence the book's $O(w^2)$ area and $O(\log w)$ delay estimate for Recip. FloPoCo does not always pick the minimal $w_A$ of [DCC12] — it picks the $w_A$ whose constant multiplier is cheapest, which is a different optimum.

> [!important] This is a *stronger* requirement than faithful rounding
> [[1.2) Error Accuracy]] calls an approximation **faithful** when its error is below 1 ulp, so the result is one of the two neighbours of the exact value. That is not enough here. The pre-floor value already carries the fractional part $R/D$, which climbs to $(D-1)/D$ — within $1/D$ of the next integer. The truncated product may therefore only spend an error budget of $\mathbf{1/D}$ ulp, not 1 ulp, or the floor tips over to $Q+1$.
>
> Concretely, $D=3$, $w=32$. The naive choice $p=32$, $A=\lceil2^{32}/3\rceil=\texttt{0x55555556}$, gives $\varepsilon = 2/(3\cdot2^{32})$ and fails the test: $\varepsilon(2^{32}-1)\approx 0.667 \not< 1/3$. It breaks at the first $X\ge 2^{31}$ with $X\equiv2\pmod 3$, namely $X=2^{31}$:
> $$\lfloor 2^{31}/3\rfloor = 715\,827\,882,\qquad \lfloor \texttt{0x55555556}\cdot 2^{31}/2^{32}\rfloor = 715\,827\,883$$
> One too many, on a perfectly ordinary input. The condition demands $2\cdot(2^{32}-1) < 2^p$, so $p=33$ and $A=\lceil 2^{33}/3\rceil = (2^{33}+1)/3 = \texttt{0xAAAAAAAB}$ — exactly the magic constant GCC and Clang emit, and exactly why they emit a $32\times32\to64$ multiply-high followed by a shift by 33 rather than a shift by 32. The margin is one unit.

Not every processor has that multiply-high. On the AMD/Xilinx MicroBlaze soft core the compiler calls a software division routine (tens of cycles) unless invoked with `-mxl-multiply-high`, and even then constant division takes four instructions. Every hardware divider below has far shorter latency, and several deliver $R$ for free.

## The linear table-based architecture (LinArch)

Introduced in [DD12]; before that it lived only in lecture notes [Pap06] as a combinational-circuit exercise. It is paper-and-pencil long division, taken literally.

Divide $776$ by $3$ in decimal. Divide $7$ by $3$: digit $2$, remainder $1$. Bring down the next digit: divide $17$ by $3$: digit $5$, remainder $2$. Bring down: divide $26$ by $3$: digit $8$, remainder $2$. Answer $258$ remainder $2$. The observation that makes this hardware: **the body of the loop is itself a division, but of a two-digit number whose leading digit is a remainder, hence at most $D-1$.** There are only $30$ possible two-digit operands, so the loop body is a table with 30 entries — and that table divides numbers of *any* length by 3.

### Radix-$2^k$ representation

Cut the binary $X$ into $m$ chunks of $k$ bits:
$$X=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}X_i\,2^{ki},\qquad X_i\in\{0,\dots,2^k-1\} \tag{13.2}$$

### The recurrence

$$
\begin{aligned}
&R_m \leftarrow 0\\
&\textbf{for } i=m-1 \textbf{ down to } 0:\\
&\qquad Y_i \leftarrow X_i + 2^k R_{i+1} &&\text{// a concatenation, not an addition}\\
&\qquad (Q_i,R_i) \leftarrow \bigl(\lfloor Y_i/D\rfloor,\; Y_i \bmod D\bigr) &&\text{// one table read}\\
&\textbf{return } \Bigl(Q=\textstyle\sum_i Q_i2^{ki},\; R=R_0\Bigr)
\end{aligned}
\tag{Alg. 13.1}
$$

**Why it computes the right thing.** By definition of the table, $Y_i = DQ_i+R_i$. Then, by (13.2) and telescoping,
$$X=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}X_i2^{ki} = \sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\bigl(Y_i-2^kR_{i+1}\bigr)2^{ki} = \sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\bigl(DQ_i+R_i\bigr)2^{ki} - \sum_{i=1}^{m}R_i2^{ki}$$
the second sum being an index shift of the first's $2^{k}R_{i+1}2^{ki}=R_{i+1}2^{k(i+1)}$ terms. All $R_i$ for $1\le i\le m-1$ cancel, $R_m=0$ by initialisation, leaving
$$X = D\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}Q_i2^{ki} + R_0$$

**Why the remainder stays bounded — the load-bearing lemma.** $R_{i+1}$ is either $0$ (initialisation) or a remainder modulo $D$, so $0\le R_{i+1}\le D-1$; and $0\le X_i\le 2^k-1$ by (13.2). Therefore
$$0\;\le\;Y_i \;=\; X_i+2^kR_{i+1}\;\le\;(2^k-1)+2^k(D-1)\;=\;2^kD-1 \tag{Lemma 13.2}$$
Two consequences, and they are the entire architecture:

1. $Y_i < 2^kD \le 2^{k+r}$, so the table has $k+r$ address bits and holds $D\cdot 2^k$ useful entries. **It does not grow with $w$.**
2. $Q_i=\lfloor Y_i/D\rfloor \le \lfloor(2^kD-1)/D\rfloor = 2^k-1$, so each $Q_i$ is a genuine radix-$2^k$ digit and the concatenated $Q_i$ *are* the binary quotient — no carry propagation between stages, ever.

The table therefore stores $k+r$ bits per entry ($k$ for $Q_i$, $r$ for $R_i$): $(k+r)$ in, $(k+r)$ out.

### Iterative or unrolled

Fold the recurrence onto one table and a register holding $R$, clocked $m$ times (Fig. 13.2, p. 432): area $2^{r+k}(r+k)$ bits, latency $m$ cycles, one operand in flight. Unroll it into $m$ table instances chained left to right and you get **LinArch** (the figure above): area $\lceil w/k\rceil\,2^{r+k}(r+k)$, latency one combinational sweep, and — the reason the book keeps only this one — it pipelines to a throughput of one division per cycle by cutting the chain wherever the timing closes.

### Cost

$m=\lceil w/k\rceil$ stages, each of fixed size. **Area and delay are both linear in $w$**, where a general divider or multiplier is quadratic. In ASIC, table area $\propto$ bits held, and rounding the address space up to a power of two (letting synthesis absorb the $2^{r+k}-D2^k$ don't-cares) removes $D$ from the cost model entirely:

$$A_{\text{seq}} \sim 2^{r+k}(r+k),\qquad A_{\text{LinArch}} \sim \left\lceil\frac{w}{k}\right\rceil 2^{r+k}(r+k),\qquad T \sim \left\lceil\frac{w}{k}\right\rceil (r+k)$$

Table 13.2 confirms the elimination of $D$: $D=5$ and $D=7$ share $r=3,k=3$ and synthesise to *identical* area and delay (45 LUT, 9.3 ns), as do $D=9$ and $D=11$.

Larger $k$ means fewer stages but an exponentially larger table. The trade-off is settled by the target technology, which is where FPGAs get interesting.

## FPGA-specific mapping

An FPGA's basic cell is a LUT$\alpha$: $\alpha$ inputs, 1 output, $\alpha\in[4,6]$. It costs the same whether you use all $\alpha$ inputs or two of them. **So there is no point choosing $k$ with $r+k<\alpha$** — you would pay for a full LUT$\alpha$ and waste address lines. Above $\alpha$, an $(r+k)$-input, $(r+k)$-output table costs $2^{r+k-\alpha}$ LUT$\alpha$ per output bit rather than $2^{r+k}$, because the fabric's built-in multiplexers assemble small LUTs into large ones for free up to a point:

$$A_{\text{LinArch}}\;\approx\;\left\lceil\frac{w}{k}\right\rceil\,2^{\max(r+k-\alpha,\,0)}(r+k)\ \ \text{LUT}\alpha$$

Minimising over $k$: take the **smallest $k$ with $r+k\ge\alpha$**, i.e. $k=\alpha-r$ when $r<\alpha$, which collapses the formula to

$$A \approx \left\lceil\frac{w}{\alpha-r}\right\rceil\alpha \quad\text{LUT}\alpha\qquad (r<\alpha)$$

This single line reproduces every estimate in Table 13.2 exactly ($w=32$, $\alpha=6$): $D=3$ gives $r=2,k=4,\lceil32/4\rceil\cdot6=48$; $D=5$ gives $\lceil32/3\rceil\cdot6=66$; $D=9$ gives $16\cdot6=96$; and $D=17$ has $r=5$, forcing $k=1$ and $32\cdot6=192$.

Read it as a warning: the useful range is $r<\alpha$, i.e. $D<2^\alpha$. Once $r\ge\alpha$ the $2^{r+k-\alpha}$ term reappears and cost explodes — $D=45$ ($r=6=\alpha$) is stuck at $k=1$ and costs $32\cdot2\cdot7=448$ LUT6 for $w=32$. **LinArch is a small-constant technique.** The fix for large $D$ is §Composite division.

Concretely for $D=3$, $\alpha=6$: $r=2$, $k=4$, radix 16, 6 LUT6 per stage. That is $\lceil w/4\rceil\times6$ LUT6 — 36 LUT6 for a 24-bit single-precision significand, 78 for a 53-bit double.

## Parallel division

LinArch's chain of $m$ dependent lookups is a ripple carry: latency linear in $w$. The **binary tree constant division (BTCD)** family [Uğu+16, Uğu+17] is to LinArch what a parallel-prefix adder ([[2.1) Adders#Parallel Prefix Adders]]) is to an [[RCA, Ripple-Carry Adder]] — it restructures the dependency into a tree, giving **latency logarithmic in $w$ at an area proportional to $w\log w$**. Both are in FloPoCo (`arch=1` selects BTCD).

> [!warning] Not covered in the source — needs outside research
> The book devotes one paragraph to BTCD and defers the construction of the tree, its node function, and its cost model entirely to [Uğu+17]. The numbers in Table 13.4 below are all it gives.

## Remainder-only and quotient-only variants

Applications often need only half of the Euclidean pair — an address translation for $D$-way memory banking wants $\lfloor A/D\rfloor$ *and* $A\bmod D$, but a decimal conversion wants only digits, and a hash wants only the residue. Each architecture responds differently.

**Recip, quotient only.** The published Recip [DCC12] computes $Q$ alone; the tables above are for the slightly stronger operator that also emits $R=X-DQ$. The overhead is tiny, and for a structural reason: only the low $r$ bits of that subtraction are live. Every higher bit is provably zero because $R<D\le 2^r$, so the subtracter is $r$ bits wide regardless of $w$. Table 13.3 (p. 436) puts the two side by side on Kintex-7 — e.g. $D=3$, $w=32$: 139 LUT / 6.1 ns versus 138 LUT / 6.0 ns.

> [!note] A labelling wrinkle in Table 13.3
> The caption names the quotient-only architecture `Recip/R`, yet its area is consistently 1–12 LUT *larger* than the `Recip` column across every row. Either reading supports the text's claim that the remainder is nearly free; the column labels appear transposed.

**LinArch, remainder only.** Drop $Q_i$ from the table outputs and each LUT shrinks from $(k+r)$ to $r$ output bits: an area factor of $r/(r+k)$, predictable in advance. Delay is *unchanged* — the critical path is the $R_i$ chain, which you still need in full. For $D=3$, $w=32$: 32 LUT $\to$ 11 LUT, 6.0 ns $\to$ 6.5 ns.

**BTCD, remainder only.** Dropping the quotient removes the quotient-assembly logic and the tree collapses to a clean binary tree of $2r$-input, $r$-output tables. Both area *and* delay improve, and this is where BTCD stops being a luxury: for $D=5$, $w=32$, remainder-only BTCD is 29 LUT / 3.7 ns against LinArch's 30 LUT / 9.8 ns. Identical area (both build the same count of 6-input, 3-output LUTs), logarithmic delay instead of linear. **If you need only the remainder, use BTCD.**

## Composite division

The exponential $2^{r+k-\alpha}$ punishes large $D$. But if $D$ factors, divide twice.

### Algorithm

Let $D=D_aD_b$. Divide by $D_a$ first:
$$X = D_aQ_a+R_a,\qquad 0\le R_a\le D_a-1 \tag{13.5}$$
then divide the intermediate quotient by $D_b$:
$$Q_a = D_bQ_b+R_b,\qquad 0\le R_b\le D_b-1 \tag{13.6}$$
Substituting (13.6) into (13.5) and distributing $D_a$:
$$X = D_a(D_bQ_b+R_b)+R_a = \underbrace{D_aD_b}_{D}Q_b + \underbrace{D_aR_b+R_a}_{R} \tag{13.7}$$
This is only a valid Euclidean division if $R<D$, and it is, by the two digit bounds:
$$R = D_aR_b+R_a \;\le\; D_a(D_b-1)+(D_a-1) \;=\; D_aD_b-1 \;=\; D-1$$
So $Q_b$ is the true quotient and $D_aR_b+R_a$ the true remainder. If $D_a$ or $D_b$ factor further, recurse.

### When two small tables beat one large one

Use the FPGA cost $A(D,w)\approx\lceil w/(\alpha-r_D)\rceil\,\alpha$ with $r_D=\lceil\log_2 D\rceil$. The second divider sees $Q_a$, barely shorter than $w$, so approximating $w'\approx w$ the composite wins when

$$\boxed{\;\frac{1}{\alpha-r_{a}}+\frac{1}{\alpha-r_{b}} \;<\; \frac{1}{\alpha-r_{D}}\;}$$

The right-hand side is what makes the trick work: $r_D=\lceil\log_2 D_aD_b\rceil$ sits in the *denominator* of the monolithic cost, so as $r_D\to\alpha$ the monolithic cost diverges while each factor's cost stays finite. Splitting a divisor turns one table exponential in $r_a+r_b$ into two tables exponential in $r_a$ and $r_b$ separately — **a product of table sizes becomes a sum.**

Check it on $D=9=3\times3$, $\alpha=6$: $r_a=r_b=2$, $r_D=4$, and $\tfrac14+\tfrac14 = \tfrac12$ against $\tfrac{1}{2}$ — a predicted dead heat, $48+48=96$ versus $96$, precisely what Table 13.5 states. Measurement disagrees with the model in the composite's favour (65 LUT vs 87), because the smaller tables leave synthesis more don't-cares to absorb. The delay argument is not close at all: monolithic $D=9$ needs $k=2$, hence $m=16$ LUTs on the critical path, whereas each sub-divider by 3 gets $k=4$ and $m=8$, so the composite path is $8+1$ LUTs — half the depth, and 17.9 ns collapses to 7.0 ns.

Two things to read off. First, **factorisation order matters** — $3\times5$ beats $5\times3$, and $5\times3\times3$ beats $9\times5$ by 26 LUT and 8 ns, because putting the small factor first lets the wide first stage run at large $k$. Second, the predicted column is a ranking tool, not a budget: synthesis beats it by 10–30% by exploiting don't-cares, and it never inverts the ordering except for $D=15$, where the model prefers the atomic divider and measurement does not.

> [!note] One arithmetic slip in Table 13.5
> The model $\lceil w'/(\alpha - r)\rceil\alpha$ reproduces every split in the table — $66+48$ for $5\times3$, $96+60$ for $9\times5$, $66+48+48$ for $5\times3\times3$ — except the $3\times5$ row, printed as $68+46$. Those are not values the formula can produce ($46$ is not a multiple of $\alpha=6$), and their total matches $5\times3$ exactly; the intended split is $48+66$.

| LinArch iterative | $2^{r+k}(r+k)$ bits, 1 stage | $m=\lceil w/k\rceil$ cycles | — | area $O(1)$, latency $m$ cycles | small $D$ ($r<\alpha$) | free, it *is* the state register |
| LinArch unrolled | 32 LUT | 6.0 ns | 87 LUT | area and delay both linear | small $D$; blows up at $r\ge\alpha$ | free ($R_0$ is the last carry) |
| BTCD | 95 LUT (Tbl 13.4) | 4.8 ns | 218 LUT | area $w\log w$, delay $\log w$ | small $D$ | free; remainder-only mode is much cheaper (12 LUT, 3.8 ns) |
| Composite ($D=9$: $3\times3$) | — | — | 65 LUT | linear, with a smaller constant | composite $D$ only | one small multiply-add |

**Pick LinArch** when $D$ is small ($r<\alpha$, so $D<64$ on a 6-LUT FPGA), area matters more than latency, and you want the remainder anyway — it is the cheapest thing on the list at 32 LUT for $32/3$, and it pipelines to one result per cycle. **Pick BTCD** when latency dominates, and always when you need the remainder only: 12 LUT / 3.8 ns for $32\bmod 3$ against LinArch's 11 LUT / 6.5 ns, and the gap widens to 50 LUT / 5.3 ns versus 43 LUT / 27.2 ns at $w=128$. **Pick Recip** when $D$ is large, prime, or not known to be small — it is the only variant whose cost does not care about $D$'s magnitude, at $O(w^2)$ area. **Always factor $D$ first**, ordering the factors smallest-first; for composite constants it dominates every atomic option on area, and everything except Recip/BTCD on delay.

## Worked example

$X = 200 = 11001000_2$ on $w=8$ bits, $D=3$. Truth: $200 = 3\cdot66+2$, so $Q=66=01000010_2$, $R=2=10_2$.

### By reciprocal multiplication

Exactness condition: $(D-1)(2^w-1) = 2\cdot255 = 510 < 2^p$ requires $p\ge9$. Take $p=9$, $A=\lceil 512/3\rceil = 171 = 10101011_2$ (the 8-bit ancestor of `0xAAAAAAAB`), $B=0$.

$$AX = 171\times200 = 34200 = 1000010110011000_2$$
$$Q = \lfloor 34200/2^9\rfloor = 34200 \gg 9 = 66 \;=\; 01000010_2\ \checkmark$$
$$R = X - 3Q = 200 - 198 = 2 \;=\; 10_2\ \checkmark$$

The exactness argument, spelled out: $\varepsilon = (3\cdot171-512)/(3\cdot512) = 1/1536$, and the worst-case pre-floor fractional part over the whole input range is
$$\max_X\left(\frac{R}{D}+\varepsilon X\right) = \frac{2}{3}+\frac{255}{1536} = 0.6667+0.1660 = 0.8327 \;<\; 1$$
so no $X\in[0,255]$ can tip the floor. Margin: $0.167$ of an ulp of $Q$, against the $1/D=0.333$ budget.

Now the naive $p=8$: $A=\lceil256/3\rceil=86$, $\varepsilon = 2/768$, and $\tfrac23+\tfrac{255}{384}=1.331>1$ — the condition fails, and so does the circuit. The first casualty is $X=128$ ($128\equiv2\bmod3$): $\lfloor86\cdot128/256\rfloor = \lfloor 43\rfloor = 43$, but $\lfloor128/3\rfloor=42$. **One extra bit of $p$ is the difference between a divider and a bug that fires on half the input range.**

### By LinArch

Take $k=2$, so $r=2$ and the table is 4-bit in, 4-bit out, with $Y\in[0,2^kD-1]=[0,11]$ by Lemma 13.2. The whole design is these twelve rows:

| $Y$ | $Q_i$ | $R_i$ | | $Y$ | $Q_i$ | $R_i$ | | $Y$ | $Q_i$ | $R_i$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `0000` | `00` | `00` | | `0100` | `01` | `01` | | `1000` | `10` | `10` |
| `0001` | `00` | `01` | | `0101` | `01` | `10` | | `1001` | `11` | `00` |
| `0010` | `00` | `10` | | `0110` | `10` | `00` | | `1010` | `11` | `01` |
| `0011` | `01` | `00` | | `0111` | `10` | `01` | | `1011` | `11` | `10` |

$Y=1100\dots1111$ are unreachable — the four don't-care rows synthesis absorbs.

$X=200$ in radix 4 is $X_3X_2X_1X_0 = (3,0,2,0)_4$, i.e. $\underbrace{11}_{X_3}\,\underbrace{00}_{X_2}\,\underbrace{10}_{X_1}\,\underbrace{00}_{X_0}$. Sweep left to right, $R_4=0$:

| $i$ | $R_{i+1}$ | $X_i$ | $Y_i = R_{i+1}\Vert X_i$ | table $\to Q_i$ | table $\to R_i$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3 | `00` | `11` | `0011` $=3$ | `01` $=1$ | `00` $=0$ |
| 2 | `00` | `00` | `0000` $=0$ | `00` $=0$ | `00` $=0$ |
| 1 | `00` | `10` | `0010` $=2$ | `00` $=0$ | `10` $=2$ |
| 0 | `10` | `00` | `1000` $=8$ | `10` $=2$ | `10` $=2$ |

Concatenate the quotient digits MSB-first — no addition, no carry:
$$Q = \underbrace{01}_{Q_3}\underbrace{00}_{Q_2}\underbrace{00}_{Q_1}\underbrace{10}_{Q_0} = 01000010_2 = 66\ \checkmark,\qquad R = R_0 = 10_2 = 2\ \checkmark$$

Note the $Y_0 = 8$ row: the incoming remainder $R_1=2=D-1$ is the largest possible, and $Y_0 = 2\cdot4+3 = 11$ would be the largest addressable value — exactly the $2^kD-1$ of Lemma 13.2. The table is never asked for more.

**Error: 0 ulp for both routes.** Both are exact by construction; the reciprocal route reaches exactness through an error budget argument, LinArch through an invariant. That is the real distinction between them.
