# Choosing a Digit Recurrence

Every circuit in this folder is the same algorithm — hold a residual that measures how far the partial result is from satisfying a polynomial equation, pick a digit that keeps it bounded, scale by $\beta$, repeat — so the choice is never about the mathematics; it is decided by the per-iteration critical path, by whether the fabric has a hard carry chain, and by whether the last bit has to be right.

> [!warning] Chapter 19 prints no LUT, slice, delay or DSP figures — for anything
> The chapter is a derivation, not a synthesis study. It gives **no area or delay measurement for any square-root variant**, and no implementation-based comparison of digit-recurrence square root against Newton-Raphson or against polynomial approximation. The single quantified head-to-head in all 25 pages is the cube-root one on p. 589, and even that is a pair of ratios rather than absolute numbers. The FPGA claim that non-restoring beats restoring (p. 580) is reported as the authors' experimental finding *without figures*. Every cell below is either a definitional cycle count, a bit width that falls out of the format derivation, or a gate/row/mux count the source itself states — never a measurement.

The whole **LUT / slice / delay / DSP** column is `—` and that is the source's position, not an omission here: the warning above says why. Three further dashes are worth naming. [[Square Root Digit Selection Function]] has no cycle count because it is combinational logic *inside* an SRT iteration, and its table size is genuinely unknown — the book's "4 bits" counts fractional positions only, while the invariant needs about five more above the point, so $2^{t_W+t_S}$ is a lower bound on the address space and nothing in the chapter pins the rest. [[On-the-Fly Converter]] likewise has no cycle count of its own; it runs in parallel with the residual update and adds mux delay, not a stage. And the seed table's row is the one place where a sibling note carries numbers the book does not: they are arithmetic on a published primitive geometry, and they are labelled as such in both places. Cycle counts *are* given throughout, because $n$, $n{+}1$, $n/\alpha$ and $m{+}1$ are definitional; so are bit widths, because each one is a consequence of a format argument in [[Number Formats]] rather than of a synthesis run.

**When the per-iteration adder is the critical path, buy a radix — and pay for it at the selection function.** All three square-root operators run one digit per cycle; the only lever on latency is how many bits a digit carries. Radix 2 gives $n$ or $n{+}1$ cycles, which is roughly 25 for a binary32 significand and 54 for binary64, and there is no escape from that inside [[Restoring Square Root]] or [[Non-Restoring Square Root]] — their digit is a sign bit, and a sign bit requires a fully propagated carry, so the per-iteration delay is an $i{+}3$ or $i{+}4$-bit carry chain that *grows as the root is built*. [[SRT Square Root]] is the only structure here that breaks that: a redundant digit set widens the selection intervals until a truncated view of a carry-save residual is enough to pick a legal digit, and the iteration collapses to selection $\to$ mux $\to$ one 3:2 row, constant in $n$. That is the entire payoff and it is not free. It obliges a $2^{t_W+t_S}$-entry [[Square Root Digit Selection Function]], whose cost is exponential in the radix while the iteration count falls only logarithmically — which is why the published parameters stop at $\beta=4$ and why past roughly $\beta=8$ the table, not the recurrence, is what stops scaling. It also obliges an [[On-the-Fly Converter]], because negative digits make $S_{i+1}=S_i+\beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}$ a borrow-propagating subtraction and the selection function needs $S_i$ in conventional binary. Two more items are optional and orthogonal: [[Square Root Seed Table]] deletes the first $i_0$ iterations for about one iteration's worth of ROM at $i_0=3$, and stops paying immediately after because its area grows like $\beta^{i_0}$; [[Square Root Range Reduction]] is not optional at all, since $M\notin[1,4)$ breaks the free $d_0=1$ and makes $W_0=M-1$ negative on iteration zero.

**On an FPGA the ranking inverts, and the honest default is the radix-2 non-restoring form.** The ASIC intuition says restoring should win — a narrower adder, and a mux that is cheap in a standard-cell library. The book reports the opposite measurement on current devices (p. 580, without figures): [[Non-Restoring Square Root]] is both smaller and shorter-path, and it is what [[FloPoCo]] emits by default, with `method=0` needed to get the restoring version back. The mechanism is structural rather than arithmetic. Non-restoring's add/sub is a single ripple-carry on the hardened carry chain, where the carry is nearly free; restoring's mux sits between the adder output and the register input, consumes a LUT per bit, and cannot ride the carry chain at all. The same fabric argument then eats SRT's advantage from the other side: a hard carry chain makes the negative-digit subtraction cheap enough that [[On-the-Fly Converter]] is not obviously worth its two muxes and two registers, but doing the subtraction per iteration adds a second adder to the loop — and radix 4's whole claim was half as many iterations at the *same* per-iteration cost. Half as many iterations each twice as expensive is a wash. The escape is to convert once at the end and feed selection a truncated $\widetilde{S_i}$, at the price of a $\pm\rho\beta^{-t_S/2}$ uncertainty that may force a larger table; p. 587 states outright that the full study has not been done, so there is no published crossover to quote. The same inversion reaches the [[E-Method]]: its FPGA reference implementation drops Ercegovac's redundant residuals for plain two's complement, precisely because fast-carry addition is cheap, and gets its area efficiency from high radices, $\beta=8$ to $32$, that exploit large architectural LUTs instead.

**When the last bit has to be right, the recurrence wins by construction and no competitor is close.** Every structure here maintains an *exact* residual, so the raw result is a round-down with error in $[0,1)$ ulp, and round-to-nearest costs exactly one extra iteration and no extra hardware — take the $(n{+}1)$-th digit as the round bit ([[Rounding]]). Nothing multiplicative offers that. A Newton-Raphson or polynomial square root is faithful only; recovering the correctly rounded value from it means squaring the answer to test it, which is likely to consume the entire latency advantage that motivated the multiplier in the first place. The cube root is where the book actually measures this, and it is the one row in the table with numbers: under the same ASIC models, [[Digit-Recurrence Cube Root]] is twice as slow and twice as small as a multiplier-based implementation — comparable area-time, which the authors offer as a rule of thumb for digit recurrences generally — and then the tie is broken outright by rounding, because *cubing* a candidate to test it is worse still. So the decision rule is: if you need a correctly rounded algebraic function and can afford one cycle per digit, take the recurrence; if the datapath already contains a multiplier and faithful is enough, do not.

**Which recurrence you take is otherwise a question of what you are computing, and two of the four come with caveats attached.** Square root is the settled case and the table above is the whole choice. Cube root is the same derivation one degree up: the new object $S_i^2$ is built by a parallel digit recurrence, so no multiplier appears, at a per-iteration cost of four 4:2 rows, one 3:2 row and four muxes, with the range reduction now modulo 3 rather than modulo 2. [[Digit-Recurrence Euclidean Norm]] is the shakiest recommendation in the folder despite being the cleverest circuit: reordering so $X$ is largest lets it skip $X^2$ entirely and initialise from $X$, but it computes $Y^2$ and $Z^2$ as plain products — forfeiting the symmetry saving a dedicated squarer would give — and its two internal additions are less area-efficient than one global compressor tree over three squares. The book knows of no implementation-based comparison against either the naive sum-of-squares-then-square-root or against [[CORDIC]], and says the net area benefit is unclear; in 2D, CORDIC in vectoring mode costs about $2n$ additions of $n$ bits plus $2n$ multiplexers and needs no selection function at all, so reach for it first. [[E-Method]] is the most general and the most conditional: it merges numerator, denominator and division of a rational approximation into one pass with no multiplier and no divider, but only when $|Q_i|+|x|<\mu$ and $|P_i|<\zeta$, possibly after power-of-two scaling. Those constraints involve $x$, so they define a *domain of convergence* rather than a property of the function, and most minimax rational fits fail them. For a pure polynomial ($Q_i=0$) some scaling always exists and the method always applies — and Horner still wins, so the E-method earns its keep only where a rational approximation genuinely beats a polynomial one, which means a near-singularity a polynomial cannot capture.
