# Choosing a Filter Structure

Every structure in this folder realises the same difference equation, so none of them is chosen on what it computes; the choice is decided by where the rounding error is injected, how wide the word carrying it has to be, and how much of the datapath sits between two register boundaries.

**The `LUT / DSP / ns` column is `—` in every row, and that is the source's position rather than an omission.** Chapter 23 reports no LUT count, no slice count, no DSP-block count, no delay in nanoseconds and no synthesis result of any kind; it defers all constant-multiplication mapping to Ch. 12 and cites bit-parallel MCM in LUTs and FPGA-shaped multiplier constructions without developing either (p. 697). The one hard cost comparison the whole chapter gives is the adder count in the last row, and adders there are counted as units — the chapter itself notes that to be optimal the count would have to be in full adders rather than adders (p. 702). **Cycle count is uniform and therefore not a column**: every structure above is a *parallel implementation* computing one output sample per clock cycle; time-shared, bit-serial and several-samples-per-cycle variants are named and then set aside (p. 677). The two `derived, not printed` cells in the [[Biquad Cascade]] row are the vault's own arithmetic over Fig. 23.12 — the book draws the cascade and never costs it.

> [!warning] The guard-bit derivation covers one structure, not all seven
> The chain (23.29)–(23.37) that ends in $\ell_{\text{ext}}=\ell'-1-\lceil\log_2\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle\rceil$ is carried through **only** for the direct form of Fig. 23.17, the book saying of everything else that "the techniques presented there can be used for other types of implementations" (p. 690). The error filter $\mathcal{H}_\delta(z)=1/(1+\sum a_iz^{-i})$ does depend on the $a_i$ alone, so its worst-case peak gain is a property of the *filter* and not of the structure — but *where* an error is injected, and what it is filtered by on its way out, is a property of the structure. Transposed forms close their loop around an internal node rather than around $\widetilde{y}$, which multiplies the bound by a numerator gain. Read every "see warning below" cell as *recompute this for your coefficients*, not as a published equality.

**Accuracy settles FIR against IIR before any structural argument starts.** Setting $a_i=0$ makes $\mathcal{H}_\delta(z)=1$, so $\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle=1$ and a faithful FIR filter carries exactly one guard bit — the one the final round-to-nearest costs. The lowest resonator of the bell model, a *second-order* filter, has $\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle=1.2806\times10^{6}$ and therefore carries $22$ (p. 694). That is the entire quantitative difference between the two families, and it is a difference in the width of every internal wire, every register and every multiplier operand, not a footnote about noise. Pick FIR when stability must be structural, when strict linear phase is part of the functional specification, or when the guard bits are unaffordable; the price is a much longer impulse response for the same transition band. Pick IIR when a long memory has to be compact and you can pay $1+\lceil\log_2\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle\rceil$ bits for it — and compute that number *first*, because "quantise to 16 bits and simulate" would have shipped the bell resonator six bits short.

**Within IIR, area at equal guaranteed accuracy points at one structure and the clock period points away from it.** The book's own ranking is a two-term product: whether the $b_i$ multipliers are fed by the narrow $u(k)$ or by the loop signal, and whether the two delay lines can be merged. [[Direct Form I IIR Filter]] wins the first and loses the second — cheapest multipliers of any form, $n{+}m$ unshared registers. [[Direct Form II IIR Filter]] wins the second and loses the first: the delay results are the same values so half of them can be saved when $m=n$, but every $b_i$ now multiplies $t'(k)$, which is computed by the feedback loop and may need many more bits than $u(k)$, so a stable implementation of direct form II "may well turn out to be cheaper, thanks to its smaller multipliers" than the register count alone suggests — the register saving and the multiplier growth pull in opposite directions and only the coefficients decide (p. 681). [[Transposed Direct Form II IIR Filter]] wins both, which is why the book calls it the best combination, and pays with a critical path through two multipliers and two adders. If that path fails timing the escape is the book's own: put one register on the $t'(k)$ wire of the plain direct form II and give up register sharing (p. 681). [[Transposed Direct Form I IIR Filter]] is the other latency answer — one multiplier and two adders for any $n$ and $m$ — but it buys that with area on both axes at once: $n{+}m$ registers, none shared, all at the internal width, with the $b_i$ still on a loop signal.

**Order decides whether a direct form is the right shape at all.** Interesting IIR filters put their poles very close to the unit circle, and a high-order denominator quantised as one polynomial can have its roots dragged outside it by a few ulps of coefficient perturbation (p. 685). [[Biquad Cascade]] removes the failure mode rather than budgeting for it: factor $H$ into second-order sections, and each section's pole pair is conditioned by its own two coefficients, so no single rounding can destabilise the whole denominator. The cost is a critical path of $S=\lceil n/2\rceil$ multiply–add pairs in series, which the $S-1$ natural section boundaries then buy back as pipeline registers at one cycle of latency each. [[Additive Synthesis Filter Bank]] is the parallel dual, and it is chosen for a different reason: when the model is physically a weighted sum of decaying modes, distributivity of convolution over addition makes the bank an identity rather than an approximation, and each [[Biquad Section]] gets its own $m'$ and its own guard bits instead of one worst case shared across the whole instrument. Both are assemblies of the same second-order unit, which is the numerically best-conditioned thing this folder contains.

**On fabric, two rows of the table move, and if you hold a frequency mask rather than a coefficient list none of them is the first question.** Every LUT in an FPGA slice already carries a flip-flop, so the registers [[Direct Form II IIR Filter]] saves were often riding free beside logic that had to exist anyway — the shared-delay-line argument is an ASIC argument first and shrinks on fabric — while guard bits stay cheap everywhere, because they widen a carry chain rather than a multiplier array, which is exactly why building the body as one [[SOPC, Sum of Products by Constants]] bit heap with a single rounding is what makes $\ell_{\text{ext}}$ affordable in the first place. The chapter states none of this in LUTs; see [[FPGAs]] for the primitives. The larger point is upstream: if what you have is a mask, choosing a structure and then quantising is the flow the chapter argues against, since quantising afterwards can break the frequency response or the stability outright (p. 685). [[Linear-Phase FIR Filter]] is the tractable entry point — symmetric coefficients halve the multiplications and make the zero-phase response real-valued, which is what lets the mask become linear constraints — and [[Minimum-Adder FIR Filter]] is what comes out when the mask, the coefficient quantisation and the shift-and-add sharing are solved as one integer program: $23$ adders against $37$ for the same specification, $38\%$ fewer, from asking one question instead of three (pp. 699–700).

$$\boxed{\ \text{FIR}\Rightarrow\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle=1\Rightarrow 1\ \text{guard bit};\qquad \text{IIR}\Rightarrow 1+\lceil\log_2\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle\rceil\ \text{guard bits, computed before the structure is picked}\ }$$

**Related Notes**

- [[Tapped Delay Line]] – depends-on: the memory every row above is wrapped around, at one register per sample of history.
- [[Direct-Form FIR Filter]] – compares: the baseline, and the only structure whose adder chain grows with $n$ between register boundaries.
- [[Transposed Direct-Form FIR Filter]] – compares: the same $n$ registers, $1.75\times$ wider in the worked example, for a critical path that no longer depends on $n$.
- [[Direct Form I IIR Filter]] – compares: cheapest IIR multipliers, and the one structure the book's guard-bit derivation actually covers.
- [[Transposed Direct Form I IIR Filter]] – compares: the latency answer that shares nothing, one multiplier and two adders at any order.
- [[Direct Form II IIR Filter]] – compares: halves the registers when $m=n$ and widens every $b_i$ multiplier doing it.
- [[Transposed Direct Form II IIR Filter]] – compares: narrow $b_i$ and shared registers together, at two multipliers of critical path.
- [[SOPC, Sum of Products by Constants]] – depends-on: one bit heap and one rounding, which is what makes $\ell_{\text{ext}}$ cheap for every recursive row.
- [[Biquad Section]] – depends-on: the second-order unit the cascade and the bank are both assembled from.
- [[Biquad Cascade]] – compares: trades critical path for pole conditioning when a single high-order denominator will not survive quantisation.
- [[Additive Synthesis Filter Bank]] – compares: the parallel dual of the cascade, chosen when the model is a sum of modes rather than a factored $H$.
- [[Linear-Phase FIR Filter]] – supports: halves the multiplications and is the property that lets a frequency mask enter a linear program.
- [[Minimum-Adder FIR Filter]] – counter-example: solving FD, Q and I jointly beats every structural choice made in sequence, $23$ adders against $37$.
- [[LTI Filters]] – supports: the topic hub these thirteen circuits were cut out of, and where the worked example's formats come from.
