An FPGA is an array of basic logic elements (BLEs) embedded in a programmable interconnect network. Both the function each BLE computes and the way they are wired is decided *after* the chip is manufactured; the decision is held in SRAM cells and loaded in a few milliseconds. Current devices hold millions of BLEs and run close to 1 GHz (p. 87). Everything else in this vault is priced in the units this note defines: when a later note says "3 LUTs per bit" or "one DSP per tile", this is where you come to find out what that bought you.

## Specification

The fabric itself is the *substrate*, not an operator, so its "specification" is a resource contract:

- **Input format.** Any Boolean function of $k$ inputs, $k \le 4\ldots6$ depending on family; word-level operands up to the DSP block's hard multiplier width; addresses up to the block RAM's depth.
- **Output format.** Bit-exact. LUTs, the carry chain, DSP blocks and block RAMs all compute exactly what they are configured to compute.
- **Accuracy.** $0$ ulp introduced by the substrate. Every ulp of error in this vault comes from a *choice* of format or algorithm (see [[Error Accuracy]]), never from the fabric.
- **Optimized for.** Area counted in LUT6/ALM, FF, DSP and BRAM; latency in ns; throughput via pipelining, which is nearly free here because the FF:LUT ratio is close to $1$ and unused FFs are wasted silicon (p. 135).

The one property that makes the whole book possible: the granularity is **one bit**. A processor offers 32 and 64; an FPGA lets you build a 13-bit datapath if 13 bits is what the error budget says. That is "computing just right" (p. 7).

## Shannon expansion, and why a $k$-LUT is universal

A LUT with $\alpha$ inputs, written **LUT$\alpha$**, is a $2^\alpha$-bit SRAM addressed by its inputs through a $2^\alpha$:1 multiplexer (Fig. 4.3, p. 90). It stores the truth table verbatim, so it realizes *any* Boolean function of $\alpha$ variables — that is a statement about truth tables, not about gates, and it is the reason FPGA area is measured in LUTs rather than in gates.

The recursive statement is **Boole's (Shannon's) expansion theorem**, the same cofactor identity used to build multiplexer trees in [[Combinational Logic]]. For $\alpha = 4$ (eq. 4.1, p. 90):

$$f(a,b,c,d) \;=\; \overline{d}\wedge f_{\overline d}(a,b,c) \;\vee\; d \wedge f_d(a,b,c)$$

with the **cofactors** obtained by substitution,

$$f_{\overline d}(a,b,c) = f(a,b,c,0), \qquad f_d(a,b,c) = f(a,b,c,1) \tag{4.2, 4.3}$$

The right-hand side is literally a 2:1 multiplexer selecting between two LUT3s, so **LUT$\alpha$ = two LUT$(\alpha-1)$ + one MUX** (Fig. 4.4, p. 91). Read forwards, this is how a LUT4 is built out of LUT3s; read backwards, it is why a fracturable LUT6 can be split into two LUT5s. The catch is the *shared inputs*: in a real BLE the two halves see the same $a\ldots e$ wires, so two independent LUT5s are only free when their support sets coincide.

**Cost of an arbitrary function of $n$ inputs.** Apply the expansion $n-6$ times against a LUT6 leaf. You get $2^{n-6}$ residual 6-input functions plus a MUX tree over $n-6$ select bits. A LUT6 makes a 4:1 MUX (4 data + 2 select), so the tree collapses 4 signals into 1 per LUT and costs $\lceil (2^{n-6}-1)/3 \rceil$:

$$L(n) \;=\; 2^{n-6} + \left\lceil \frac{2^{n-6}-1}{3} \right\rceil \;\approx\; \frac{4}{3}\,2^{\,n-6} \quad\text{LUT6 per output bit}$$

$L(6)=1$, $L(8)=5$, $L(10)=21$, $L(16)=1365$. **Every input past the sixth doubles the cost.** That single fact is why table-based methods ([[Table-Based Function Evaluation]]) spend all their effort on shrinking the address, and why a function that *factors* is worth orders of magnitude more than one that does not.

> [!important] Structure is the only thing that beats the exponential
> A raw $8\times8$ multiply is a 16-input, 16-output function: $16 \times 1365 \approx 21{,}800$ LUT6 as a blind table. Built as an array of partial products it is two orders of magnitude cheaper. Any time a LUT count looks reasonable, it is because the mapper found algebraic structure — not because the table was small.

## Look-up tables and flip-flops

The minimal BLE is a LUT plus an optional flip-flop, with a configuration bit choosing between registered and purely combinational output (Fig. 4.2, p. 89). Both the LUT contents and that bypass bit live in the same SRAM configuration memory.

The input count $\alpha$ is the critical architectural parameter, because SRAM cells *and* MUX size both grow as $2^\alpha$. Architectural exploration on real benchmark circuits found the best area–delay products between **4 and 6 inputs** [AR04], which is exactly the range every commercial family sits in (p. 90).

Flip-flops are close to free and should be treated as such: on FPGAs the LUT:FF ratio is near $1:1$, the FFs sit inside the BLE wired directly to the LUT and carry outputs, and an unused FF is simply wasted (p. 135). In ASIC a flip-flop costs about as much as a full adder, so pipelining is a real trade; on FPGA it usually is not. This asymmetry is why [[Adders]] concludes that pipelining beats clever carry-acceleration schemes on FPGAs.

## The BLE of AMD/Xilinx FPGAs

BLEs are grouped into **slices**, slices into **configurable logic blocks (CLBs)** (p. 91).

| Family | BLE contents | Slice | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Virtex 4 | 1 LUT4, carry XOR + MUX, dedicated AND, **1 FF** | 2 BLEs (4 slices/CLB) | the AND builds partial products for multipliers |
| Virtex 5/6, Spartan 6, 7 series | 1 **LUT6** fracturable into two LUT5 with **shared inputs**, carry XOR + MUX, **2 FFs** | 4 BLEs | AND gate dropped |
| UltraScale/UltraScale+ | same BLE | **8 BLEs**, 1 slice per CLB | more routing per CLB |
| Versal | **4 LUT4s**, 2 FFs, `cascin`/`cascout`, carry from a lookahead unit | 8 BLEs | no XOR in the carry chain |

The fracturable LUT6 is the workhorse: outputs `O6` (full 6-input function) and `O5` (one 5-input half). Versal goes further and splits both LUT5s into LUT4s, which lets the adder's XOR be computed *inside* a 4-input LUT and routed to the neighbour through the cascade connection (p. 92).

## The BLE of Intel/Altera FPGAs

The cluster is a **logic array block (LAB)** of **ten adaptive logic modules (ALMs)**; one ALM is two BLEs (p. 93). A half-ALM BLE contains **one LUT4, two LUT3s, a hardened full adder, and two FFs** (Stratix III through Stratix V and Stratix 10; the second FF arrived with Stratix V). Stratix 10 replaced the two LUT3s with a single LUT4, trading configurations for simplicity.

The eight input lines of an ALM are shared between its two BLEs, and multiplexers between the halves adapt the granularity. A complete ALM can be configured as:

- two independent 4-input LUTs;
- an independent 3-input and an independent 5-input LUT;
- one independent 6-input LUT;
- combinations up to six inputs where some inputs are shared;
- some specific 7-input LUTs [Alt13].

The important difference from AMD: the full adder is **hardened**, not built from the LUT's XOR. Both LUT layers stay free for other logic feeding the adder inputs (p. 128) — the basis of the merged-functionality adders in [[Adders]].

## Comparison of BLEs across commercial families

Every BLE in Figs. 4.5 and 4.6 follows one pattern (p. 94):

1. a first layer of LUT(s) that can be subdivided into smaller LUTs;
2. a fast carry chain to speed up addition;
3. optional FF(s) to store the result.

Design against that pattern, not against a part number: the vendor-specific numbers age, the three-layer structure has not.

## The fast carry chain

*(The book develops this in the adder chapter, Sect. 5.4; it is architecture, so it belongs here. The consequences for adder topology are in [[Adders]].)*

Generic programmable routing is slow. Every modern BLE therefore carries a dedicated wire that hands one carry bit to its immediate neighbour, bypassing the switch matrix entirely (p. 90).

On AMD series 4–7 and UltraScale(+) the LUT computes the **propagate** signal and the hardened logic does the rest (eqs. 5.50–5.51, p. 126):

$$p_i = x_i \oplus y_i, \qquad s_i = p_i \oplus c_i, \qquad c_{i+1} = p_i c_i \vee \overline{p_i}\,x_i$$

The carry recurrence is written as a **multiplexer**, not as $g_i \vee p_i c_i$: when $p_i = 1$ pass $c_i$ through; when $p_i = 0$ we know $x_i = y_i$, so passing $x_i$ gives the correct generate/kill value. Read it as a *switched* ripple-carry adder. A 2:1 MUX with a pre-computed select is about the fastest thing that can be built in silicon, which is where the name comes from.

The numbers, for Virtex 6 at speed grade 3 (p. 126–127, [Xil14]):

| Path | Delay |
| --- | --- |
| carry-in → carry-out, whole slice (4 BLEs) | $\le 0.06$ ns |
| carry-in → carry-out, per BLE | $\tau_{cp} = 0.015$ ns |
| LUT combinational | $\tau_{\text{LUT}} = 0.14 \ldots 0.32$ ns |
| carry-in → BLE output through the XOR | $\tau_{\text{XOR}} = 0.21 \ldots 0.25$ ns |
| worst-case full adder | $\tau_{fa} = 0.57$ ns $= 38\times \tau_{cp}$ |

A ripple carry crosses a bit **38 times faster** than it enters or leaves one. Worst-case adder delays come out at roughly **1 ns (8-bit), 1.5 ns (16-bit), 2.5 ns (32-bit)** (p. 127). The practical rule: below 32 bits, do not think about carry propagation at all.

Versal drops the XOR from the carry chain. A `LOOKAHEAD8` unit computes only the *even* carries of the eight BLEs in a slice from the propagates; the odd carries and the sums are computed in the LUT4s and passed through `cascin`/`cascout` (pp. 127–128). On Intel the question does not arise — the full adder is hardened, with dedicated wires to its neighbours, and a chain may start at the 1st or 6th ALM of a LAB (p. 128).

The **post-adder** chains DSPs into larger multipliers, and gives generic multiply–accumulate for polynomial evaluation and FIR filters. The **pre-adder** exists for symmetric FIR filters, for Karatsuba, and for the 3-multiplication complex product (eqs. 4.5–4.8, p. 94):

$$P_r = A_rB_r - A_iB_i = \underbrace{A_r(B_r+B_i)}_{\text{shared}} - (A_r+A_i)B_i$$
$$P_i = A_rB_i + A_iB_r = \underbrace{A_r(B_r+B_i)}_{\text{shared}} - (A_r-A_i)B_r$$

Expand either line and the cross terms cancel by distributivity; the boxed product is computed once. **3 multiplications and 5 additions instead of 4 and 2** — a good trade exactly because DSPs are the scarce resource and the pre/post-adders are already sitting there unused.

Two details that bite:

- **AMD sizes are signed.** A signed multiplier used unsigned must have its MSB tied to zero, so an $18\times27$ signed unit is a $17\times26$ unsigned unit. Intel documents both (p. 95).
- **Intel splits, AMD combines.** Intel's dual-multiplier mode plus its shared post-adder means a complex multiplication needs only **two** DSPs — one for the real part, one for the imaginary (p. 95). AMD offers fixed-size units and expects you to tile ([[Multiplier Tiling]]).

Stratix 10 DSPs additionally harden single-precision floating point: one block does an FP multiply, or an add/subtract, or a MAC [LP15].

## Memory elements

### Distributed memory

AMD slices come in two flavours: **SLICEL** (logic) and **SLICEM** (memory), the latter a superset. In a SLICEM the LUT's SRAM cells become writable flip-flops with an extra data input `DI1`, selected by a write address `WA` through a one-hot decoder; the ordinary LUT inputs act as the read address (Fig. 4.7a, p. 96–97). Read and write ports are independent, so this is a **simple dual-port** memory.

Capacity: each LUT5 half gives $32\times1$, so **one LUT6 = two $32\times1$ or one $64\times1$** bits. Intel's equivalent is the **MLAB**, a superset of the LAB, where one ALM realizes a $32\times2$ simple dual-port block.

This is the cheapest ROM on the chip and the natural target for small tables — a $2^6\times m$ table costs exactly $m$ LUT6, i.e. the same as the arbitrary-function bound $L(6)=1$ per output bit. That equality is not a coincidence: a LUT *is* a ROM.

### Shift-register LUTs

The other SLICEM mode. The FFs are chained instead of addressed: `DI1` feeds the chain, `MC31` cascades to the next LUT5, and the LUT inputs `A0..4` now select *which delayed version* of `DI1` to read, with a minimum delay of one (Fig. 4.7b, p. 97). A 32-deep addressable delay line in one LUT5 — the standard way to align pipeline stages without burning 32 FFs and their routing.

A second use, easy to miss: the FF contents can be written **sequentially**, so a LUT's table can be re-programmed in 32 clock cycles through a single wire. That is dynamic runtime reconfiguration with the structure held fixed and only the constants changing — directly relevant when a coefficient must be swapped occasionally [Möl17; Har+19] (p. 98).

### Block memory

Dedicated RAM tiles, embedded in the fabric the same way DSP blocks are (p. 98):

| Device | Block size | Configurations |
| --- | --- | --- |
| AMD/Xilinx 7th gen & UltraScale | 36 kbit | $32\text{k}\times1$, $16\text{k}\times2$, $8\text{k}\times4$, $4\text{k}\times9$, $2\text{k}\times18$, $1\text{k}\times36$, $512\times72$ |
| Intel Stratix 5/10 (M20K) | 20 kbit | $2\text{k}\times10$, $1\text{k}\times20$, $512\times40$ |

*Table 4.2, p. 99.*

Usually **true dual-port** (read and write on both ports); some configurations only offer simple dual-port. Large devices hold thousands of them, several Mbit total. On timing: a block RAM reaches the nominal frequency of the FPGA — about one logic element's delay — but **each access takes several cycles** through dedicated internal pipeline registers (p. 98). Block RAM is a throughput resource, not a latency resource.

Stratix 10 also has **eSRAM** (~5.90 Mb, single-port, a handful per device). The book calls it probably irrelevant to application-specific arithmetic; so does this note.

## The FPGA–ASIC performance gap

A circuit emulated in an FPGA runs roughly **one order of magnitude slower** than the same circuit cast directly into silicon [KR07] (p. 5). The cause is not the LUTs — it is the **reconfigurable routing**: wires are longer than they need to be, and every signal passes through the switch matrices that make reconfigurability possible. Routing is also the bulk of the chip's area and complexity (p. 88), and it is scarcely documented, hidden behind vendor backend tools.

Three things buy the gap back (pp. 5–7):

1. **No program management.** A processor cycle fetches, decodes, reads registers, executes, writes back; only one of those steps computes. A dataflow FPGA design implements the execute step and nothing else. Registers exist, but they are statically assigned — no register-file address decoding.
2. **Exact parallelism.** A processor's mix of operators is fixed and tuned for an average workload; computing $d = b^2 - 4ac$ on one, the 1:1 FP multiplier:adder ratio under-uses the adders and leaves the integer units and divider idle. An FPGA instantiates precisely the operators the application needs and pipelines them.
3. **Bit-level granularity.** A processor toggles 32 or 64 bits whether or not the application needs them. An FPGA toggles and transmits just the number of bits required *at that point in the datapath* — which is the entire premise of this vault.

On the positive side, FPGAs have not hit their power wall: lower clock frequencies plus the large routing area that dissipates the heat (p. 5).

## Trade-off: three ways to compute the same small function

Target: an unsigned $8\times8 \to 16$ bit product, i.e. a 16-input, 16-output Boolean function. Same specification, three substrates.

| | LUT logic | DSP block | Block RAM (as ROM) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Area, blind table | $16 \times L(16) = 21{,}840$ LUT6 (derived from Shannon, p. 90) | — | $2^{16}\times16 = 1$ Mbit $\Rightarrow$ **32** BRAM36 in $2\text{k}\times18$ + address decode (Table 4.2, p. 99) |
| Area, exploiting structure | array of partial products, see [[Multipliers]] | **1** DSP ($17\times26$ unsigned fits inside $18\times27$ signed, p. 95) | halve the address with [[Multipartite Methods]] |
| Latency | $\tau_{\text{LUT}} = 0.14\ldots0.32$ ns per level (p. 127) | pipelined, several cycles — not specified in Ch. 4 | nominal FPGA frequency, but **several cycles** per access (p. 98) |
| Native unit | LUT6 / ALM | DSP unit | 36 kbit (AMD) / 20 kbit (Intel) tile |
| Runs out when | the function has $>6$ inputs and does not factor: cost $\times2$ per extra input | operands exceed $18\times27$ / $27\times27$, or the device's DSP count | the address exceeds 11 bits: every extra address bit doubles the tile count |

- **Pick LUT logic** for anything under 6 inputs, for glue, and for anything with algebraic structure a mapper can find — it is the only resource that is genuinely plentiful.
- **Pick a DSP** whenever the operation is a multiply, a MAC or a pre-add-multiply that fits the hard width. It is a *free* multiplier until you run out, and then it is the most expensive resource on the chip; that cliff is what [[Multiplier Tiling]] optimizes against.
- **Pick block RAM** for tables whose address is ≤ 11 bits and whose latency can be hidden by pipelining — and only after [[Table-Based Function Evaluation]] has been used to shrink the address, because the block count doubles per address bit just as fast as LUT cost does.

## Worked example — 8-bit addition on the fast carry chain

$x = 109 = 0110\,1101_2$, $y = 54 = 0011\,0110_2$, $c_0 = 0$. Each BLE's LUT is configured as $p_i = x_i \oplus y_i$; the carry MUX selects $c_i$ when $p_i = 1$ and $x_i$ when $p_i = 0$.

$$p = x \oplus y = 0101\,1011_2$$

| $i$ | $x_i$ | $y_i$ | $p_i$ | MUX picks | $c_{i+1}$ | $s_i = p_i \oplus c_i$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | $c_0 = 0$ | 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | $c_1 = 0$ | 0 | 1 |
| 2 | 1 | 1 | 0 | $x_2 = 1$ | 1 | 0 |
| 3 | 1 | 0 | 1 | $c_3 = 1$ | 1 | 0 |
| 4 | 0 | 1 | 1 | $c_4 = 1$ | 1 | 0 |
| 5 | 1 | 1 | 0 | $x_5 = 1$ | 1 | 1 |
| 6 | 1 | 0 | 1 | $c_6 = 1$ | 1 | 0 |
| 7 | 0 | 0 | 0 | $x_7 = 0$ | 0 | 1 |

$$s = 1010\,0011_2 = 163 = 109 + 54 \;\checkmark$$

**Cost.** 8 BLEs — one LUT6 (using 2 of its 6 inputs), one carry MUX and one FF each. On Virtex 6 that is 2 slices; on UltraScale, 1 slice. The other 4 LUT inputs per BLE are idle, which is the opening the merged-functionality adders of [[Adders]] exploit.

**Delay.** $\tau \approx \tau_{\text{LUT}} + 7\tau_{cp} + \tau_{\text{XOR}} = 0.32 + 7(0.015) + 0.25 \approx 0.67$ ns, in line with the book's ~1 ns worst case for $w=8$ once routing to and from the chain is included (p. 127).

**Error.** $0$ ulp. Integer addition on the carry chain is exact; the only way to lose accuracy is to truncate the result, which is a format decision ([[Number Formats]]), not a fabric one.
