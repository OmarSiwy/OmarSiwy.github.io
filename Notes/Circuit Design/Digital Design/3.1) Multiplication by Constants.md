**Inputs.** One variable $X$ in $\mathrm{ufix}(m_X,\ell_X)$ or $\mathrm{sfix}(m_X,\ell_X)$ (notation per [[1.1) Number Formats]]), and one coefficient $C$ that is *not* a signal — it is a literal in the source, known when the netlist is built.
**Outputs.** $R\approx CX$ in a format whose LSB $\ell_R$ the caller must state. For integer $C$ the product is exact; for real $C$ ($\pi$, $\log 2$, $\sin\frac{k\pi}{n}$) it cannot be, so $\ell_R$ is part of the specification, not a consequence of it.
**Accuracy.** Exact ($0$ ulp) when $C$ and $X$ are integers. Faithful ($<1$ ulp) is the default target for real $C$; perfect rounding ($\le\tfrac12$ ulp) is reachable only by tabulation or at roughly $w_X$ extra bits of constant width.
**Optimized for.** Area — adder count for ASIC, LUT count for FPGA — with logic depth as the tie-break, because a constant multiplier is almost always replicated (a $64$-tap FIR instantiates $64$ of them).

> [!important] A constant is not an operand
> A generic multiplier must handle $2^{w_C}$ possible coefficients, so it builds a partial-product array and compresses it. A constant multiplier handles exactly one, and every partial product it would generate is *known at synthesis time*: the zero ones vanish, the nonzero ones are pure wiring. What is left is a graph of additions. The design problem stops being arithmetic and becomes **combinatorial optimization** — "what is the cheapest addition graph whose output is $CX$" — and that problem is NP-complete (p. 369, citing Cappello & Steiglitz). Everything in this note is a way of searching that space, or of side-stepping it by tabulation.

## Derivation: from bit vector to addition graph

Write the constant in binary, $C=(c_{w_C-1}\dots c_1c_0)_2$. Distributivity of multiplication over addition gives

$$X\times C \;=\; X\cdot\left(\sum_{i=0}^{w_C-1}2^ic_i\right) \;=\; \sum_{i=0}^{w_C-1}2^i\,\underbrace{X\cdot c_i}_{\text{partial product}} \tag{12.1, p.\,367}$$

and because each $c_i$ is a *literal*, $X\cdot c_i$ is either the constant $0$ or the wire bundle $X$. Multiplication by $2^i$ is a shift, which on hardware is a relabelling of wires and costs nothing. So the whole product is a sum of $\mathrm{nz}(C)$ shifted copies of $X$ — where $\mathrm{nz}$ is the Hamming weight — needing $N_{\mathrm A}=\mathrm{nz}(C)-1$ adders. This graph — nodes are adders, edge labels are shifts — is the **adder graph**.

### Signed digits and the run-of-ones identity

Subtraction costs the same as addition in area, delay and energy (p. 368), so nothing forbids negative partial products. The lever is the finite geometric sum, the same identity Booth's recoding exploits in [[2.4) Multipliers]]:

$$\sum_{i=p}^{p+k-1}2^i \;=\; 2^p\left(2^k-1\right) \;=\; 2^{p+k}-2^p \tag{run of ones}$$

A run of $k$ consecutive ones, which costs $k-1$ adders in binary, costs *one* subtracter in signed-digit (SD) form. Booth applies this to the multiplicand at run time; here we apply it to the constant at compile time, and we may apply it repeatedly. That is Algorithm 12.1 (p. 368): *find any substring $0\,\underbrace{1\cdots1}_{k\ge2}$ and rewrite it as $1\,\underbrace{0\cdots0}_{k-1}\bar1$; repeat until none is left.* Writing $\bar1$ for $-1$:

$$93=1011101_2 \;\to\; 1100\bar101_{\mathrm{SD}} \;\to\; 10\bar100\bar101_{\mathrm{CSD}}$$

from five nonzeros (four adders) down to four (three adders).

### Canonicity and the non-adjacency bound

The rewriting terminates, and its fixed point is unique; that fixed point is the **canonical signed digit** (CSD) form. The cleanest way to see both facts is to generate the digits directly. Given $C>0$, emit digit $d_0$ and recurse on a smaller number:

$$d \;=\; \begin{cases}0 & C\equiv0 \pmod 2\\ +1 & C\equiv1\pmod 4\\ -1 & C\equiv3\pmod 4\end{cases}\qquad C\leftarrow\frac{C-d}{2}$$

The digit set is $\{-1,0,1\}$ and each step divides by two, so the expansion is a valid radix-2 SD representation of $C$ and it terminates. The choice of $d$ when $C$ is odd is exactly the run-of-ones identity: $C\equiv3\pmod4$ means the two low bits are $11$, the start of a run, and subtracting $-1$ ends it.

> **Non-adjacency.** If $d\ne0$ then $C-d\equiv0\pmod 4$ by construction, so $(C-d)/2$ is **even** and the next digit is forced to $0$. Hence no two consecutive digits are both nonzero.

Non-adjacency is what bounds the cost. In a CSD string of $n$ digits the nonzero positions form an independent set in a path graph, so there are at most $\lceil n/2\rceil$ of them; an $n$-bit unsigned constant expands to at most $n+1$ CSD digits (the carry-out case, $2^n-1\to10\cdots0\bar1$), giving $\mathrm{nz}_{\mathrm{CSD}}(C)\le\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\rceil$ and $N_{\mathrm A}\le\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\rceil-1$, against $n$ nonzeros worst case in binary. The bound is tight: $93=10\bar100\bar101$ has $8$ digits and $4$ nonzeros. Note that CSD is *minimal in nonzero count* but not unique among minimal forms — replacing $10\bar1$ by $011$ or $\bar101$ by $0\bar1\bar1$ preserves the count and yields the **minimum signed digit** (MSD) representations, four of them for $93$ (eq. 12.2, p. 369). They cost the same in adders but not necessarily in full adders, which matters below.

Contrast with plain radix-2 Booth recoding $d_i=x_{i-1}-x_i$: applied to $0101_2$ it produces $1\bar11\bar1$, four nonzeros where binary had two. Booth rewrites *every* boundary; CSD rewrites only the ones that pay.

## Shift-and-add integer constant multiplication

### Formalizing the SCM problem

CSD is a greedy heuristic and it is not optimal. $93=3\times31$, and with $3=11_{\mathrm{MSD}}$, $31=1000\bar1_{\mathrm{MSD}}$, cascading the two multipliers needs **two** adders (Fig. 12.3c, p. 367) rather than CSD's three. The same solution is visible inside the CSD string as the shared pattern $10\bar1_{\mathrm{SD}}=-\bar101_{\mathrm{SD}}$ (eq. 12.3), which is the mechanism behind **common subexpression elimination** (CSE). But CSE cannot be complete: for $25=10\bar1001_{\mathrm{CSD}}=101_2\cdot101_2$ the shared pattern $101_2$ is invisible in CSD because its two ones overlap when squared (p. 369).

The **single constant multiplication (SCM)** problem — find the adder graph with fewest adders computing $CX$ — is NP-complete (p. 369). Two exact methods are practical for realistic word sizes.

### Minimal-adder constant multiplication by graph enumeration

Reverse the question: *which coefficients are reachable with at most $N_{\mathrm A}$ adders?* Enumerate all adder-graph topologies with $N_{\mathrm A}$ nodes, evaluate the constants they produce, tabulate. This is the MAG algorithm of Dempster and Macleod, covering all $12$-bit coefficients with up to four adders; Gustafsson et al. pushed it to five adders covering everything up to $19$ bits, and six adders up to $32$ bits (p. 370).

The stored form is a single recursion, $C=C_{\mathrm A}2^{s_{\mathrm A}}+C_{\mathrm B}2^{s_{\mathrm B}}$ (eq. 12.4), applied until both operands are $1$. For $C=173$: $173=45+1\cdot2^7$, then $45=15+15\cdot2^1$, then $15=1\cdot2^4-1$, giving three adders (eqs. 12.5–12.7, p. 371). Even coefficients are free: divide by two until odd, look up, and left-shift the result — $120=2^3\cdot15$ costs the one adder that $15$ costs.

### Minimal-adder constant multiplication by ILP

An ILP formulation is slower than a specialized branch-and-bound but is easy to re-implement and, crucially, **retargetable**: the same skeleton minimizes power, fan-out, pipeline depth, ternary-adder count or full-adder count by swapping the objective (p. 373). It also generalizes to MCM and to whole filters.

The trick is that $N_{\mathrm A}$ cannot be an ILP variable — it determines how many constraint copies exist. So the search runs *outward*: start at the lower bound and increment $N_{\mathrm A}$ until the model becomes feasible. A single adder can at most double the nonzero count of an MSD representation, so

$$N_{\mathrm{A,lb,SCM}}=\left\lceil\log_2\big(\mathrm{nz}(C)\big)\right\rceil \tag{12.8, p.\,373}$$

Each adder node $a$ obeys the nonlinear relation (eq. 12.9, p. 374)

$$c_a=(-1)^{\phi_{a,\mathrm L}}2^{s_{a,\mathrm L}}c_{a,\mathrm L}+(-1)^{\phi_{a,\mathrm R}}2^{s_{a,\mathrm R}}c_{a,\mathrm R}$$

linearized by introducing one binary decision variable per choice — source ($c_{a,i,k}$), shift ($\varphi_{a,i,s}$), sign ($\phi_{a,i}$) — and expressing each as an **indicator constraint** ("$x=y$ if $z=1$"), which solvers accept natively or which becomes the big-$M$ pair $y-M+Mz\le x\le y+M-Mz$ (eqs. 12.11–12.12, p. 377). Three structural constraints shrink the space a lot: adders are *ordered*, so node $a$ may only read nodes $k<a$ (kills feedback loops and symmetric duplicates); at most one input may be negated ($\phi_{a,\mathrm L}+\phi_{a,\mathrm R}\le1$); and only the left input may take a positive shift, with both shifts forced equal when negative (p. 376).

The practical ceiling is numerical, not conceptual: $M$ must exceed $\max c_a2^s$, and $c_a$ can exceed $C$ itself, so one bounds $c_a<2C$ and sets $M=2C2^{s_{\max}}$. With Gurobi, constants up to $12$ bits solve in under a second, $16$-bit in seconds to minutes, $20$-bit in up to an hour — beyond that, heuristics (p. 378).

### Using ternary adders

FPGA carry chains implement a three-input adder at the same cost and nearly the same delay as a two-input one (see [[Ternary Adders on FPGAs]]), so the natural node is $c_a=c^{\mathrm{sh,sg}}_{a,\mathrm L}+c^{\mathrm{sh,sg}}_{a,\mathrm M}+c^{\mathrm{sh,sg}}_{a,\mathrm R}$ with at most two of the three negated ($\mathcal C2^*$, $\mathcal C5\mathrm c^*$, p. 379). Enumerating ternary topologies shows **every constant up to $22$ bits is reachable with three ternary adders or fewer**; $7{,}154{,}955$ is the first that needs four (p. 378). Averaged over coefficients wider than five bits, this saves about $1/3$ of the adder resources (Fig. 12.7b, p. 380). $683$ drops from four two-input adders to two ternary ones (Fig. 12.6, p. 379).

### Minimizing logic resources instead of adder count

Adder count is a *high-level* metric and it lies. Because of the shifts, the two operands of an adder overlap only partially: below the overlap the bits pass through untouched (no logic), above it the full adders degrade to half adders (p. 381). The bit-level cost therefore depends on the shifts and on add-versus-subtract, and among the many minimal-adder graphs for one constant these costs differ widely.

The book's example is $49X$ with $w_X=8$ (Fig. 12.8, p. 382). Via the small intermediate $7X$: $11$ full-adder equivalents for $7X$ plus $14$ for $49X$, total $25$. Via the negative intermediate $-15X$: $-15X$ has four copyable LSBs and costs $8$, and $49X=-15X+2^6X$ costs another $8$, total $16$ — about $1/3$ less, **at the same adder count**. Negative intermediates are systematically useful here because they align cheaply. ILP extensions that carry an explicit full-adder cost model exist (Garcia & Volkova, p. 381).

## Integer constant multiplication using tables

This section is FPGA-only; $\alpha$ denotes the architectural LUT input count, $4$ to $6$ on mainstream parts (see [[Lookup-Tables]]).

### Tabulated constant multipliers

$X$ takes $2^{w_X}$ values, so $CX$ can simply be tabulated. If $w_X\le\alpha$ each output bit of $CX$ is a function of $\le\alpha$ inputs and costs **exactly one LUT** — the whole multiplier is $w_X+w_C$ LUTs with one LUT of delay, and no constant is cheaper (p. 383). One bit past that and it collapses: for $w_X=\alpha+k$ each output bit costs $2^k$ LUTs plus address decoding (p. 383). Exponential in $w_X$, so only usable for tiny inputs.

### The table-and-addition KCM algorithm

Ken Chapman's Multiplier fixes the scaling by tabulating *chunks*. Read $X$ in radix $2^\alpha$:

$$X=\sum_{i=0}^{\lceil w_X/\alpha\rceil-1}X_i\cdot(2^\alpha)^i,\qquad X_i\in\{0,\dots,2^\alpha-1\} \tag{12.13, p.\,384}$$

and distribute:

$$CX=\sum_{i}CX_i\cdot2^{\alpha i} \tag{12.14, p.\,385}$$

Each $CX_i$ is a function of $\alpha$ bits, so it is one LUT per output bit — a table of $w_C+\alpha$ LUTs — and the products are then summed with shifts of $\alpha$. Because of those shifts, the low $\alpha$ bits of each partial sum are final and the running adder only needs to be $w_C$ bits wide:

$$\#\mathrm{LUTs}=\left\lceil\frac{w_X}{\alpha}\right\rceil(w_C+\alpha)+\left(\left\lceil\frac{w_X}{\alpha}\right\rceil-1\right)w_C \tag{p.\,385}$$

Two properties make KCM the FPGA default. The area is **predictable and almost independent of the value of $C$** — no NP-complete search, no lucky-constant effect (though logic optimizers will still compress the tables). And it scales *linearly* in $w_X$, unlike plain tabulation. Wirthlin's refinement splits into $\alpha-1$ bit chunks so that one row of Xilinx LUTs holds both the table and its adder on the fast carry chain; Intel LUTs already ship with a full adder and need no such trick (p. 385).

The rake of adders is a plain accumulator, correct but not optimal: the partial products are just a set of weighted bits, so the right consumer is a compressor tree. Do not re-derive it — the shape of that summation, and why a single global bit heap beats a chain of adders, is [[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]]. KCM generators in FloPoCo are therefore written as **virtual operators**: they emit bits into a caller-supplied bit heap and deliberately do *not* compress it, so that an enclosing operator (a complex multiplier, an FIR tap) compresses everything once (p. 394).

## Multiplication of a fixed-point number by a real constant

Now $C\in\mathbb R$ — $\pi$, $1/\log2$, an FFT twiddle $\cos\frac{k\pi}{n}$, a raised-cosine FIR tap. The generator's interface gains an argument it did not need before: the output LSB $\ell_R$ (Fig. 12.11, p. 386). A real constant has infinitely many bits, so *where to stop* is an input to the problem, not an output.

### Tabulated perfectly rounded constant multipliers

For small $w_X$, tabulate $\lfloor C\cdot i\rceil_{\ell_R}$ computed offline in arbitrary precision. One single rounding happens, of at most half an ulp:

$$|\delta_{\text{total}}|\le2^{\ell_R-1}=\tfrac12\,\mathrm{ulp}$$

This is the best any operator can do (p. 386) and it is the reference every other technique is measured against. It dies with $w_X$ for the reason in the previous section.

### Faithful fix-by-real using shift-and-add

A shift-and-add graph can only realize a finite-precision $\widetilde C$, so there are two errors, and the whole design consists of splitting one budget between them. With $P(X)=\widetilde CX$ the exact graph output and $R(X)$ the rounded result, insert and cancel $P(X)$ (telescoping):

$$\delta_{\text{total}}(X)=R(X)-CX=\underbrace{\big(R(X)-P(X)\big)}_{\delta_{\text{final round}}}+\underbrace{(\widetilde C-C)}_{\delta_C}X \tag{12.16–12.19, p.\,388}$$

The final round contributes $|\delta_{\text{final round}}|\le2^{\ell_R-1}$. Faithful rounding means $|\delta_{\text{total}}|<2^{\ell_R}=1$ ulp (the definitions live in [[1.2) Error Accuracy]]), so the constant's own error may consume the *other* half-ulp:

$$\forall X,\quad|\delta_CX|<2^{\ell_R-1}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad|\delta_C|<\overline\delta_C=\frac{2^{\ell_R-1}}{X_{\max}} \tag{12.21–12.22, p.\,388}$$

That is the entire accuracy analysis, and it is the whole design constraint: the input format enters only through $X_{\max}$. Rounding $C$ to LSB $\ell$ gives $|\lfloor C\rceil_\ell-C|\le2^{\ell-1}$, so there is a largest $\ell_{\max}$ with $2^{\ell_{\max}-1}<\overline\delta_C$, and $\lfloor C\rceil_{\ell_{\max}}$ is the *narrowest* admissible constant.

Narrowest is not cheapest. Equation 12.22 defines an interval

$$C-\overline\delta_C<\widetilde C<C+\overline\delta_C \tag{12.24, p.\,389}$$

containing infinitely many admissible constants, at LSBs $\ell=\ell_{\max}-k$. Two reasons to search them (p. 389): the adder-graph cost depends erratically on the constant, so a *wider* $\widetilde C$ is often cheaper (Gustafsson & Qureshi); and a smaller $\delta_C$ leaves slack that can be spent truncating the shift-and-add graph itself, deleting full adders. ILP models that trade truncation error against deleted full adders are the state of the art here (de Dinechin et al. 2019; Garcia & Volkova).

Correct rounding is available but expensive: it needs $\ell'_{\max}\approx\ell_{\max}-w_X$, roughly $w_X$ extra constant bits for one extra bit of accuracy (p. 389).

### Faithful fix-by-real KCM

Same decomposition as integer KCM, but the input is scaled to an integer first and the chunk count follows from the *format*, not the width alone:

$$D=\left\lceil\frac{m_X-\ell_X+1}{\alpha}\right\rceil,\qquad 2^{-\ell_X}X=\sum_{k=0}^{D-1}2^{k\alpha}X_k,\qquad CX=\sum_{k=0}^{D-1}2^{k\alpha+\ell_X}CX_k \tag{12.25–12.27, p.\,390}$$

Each $CX_k$ has infinitely many bits, so each table stores it rounded to $\ell_R-g$ for $g$ **guard bits**:

$$T_k(X_k)=\left\lfloor2^{k\alpha+\ell_X}CX_k\right\rceil_{\ell_R-g}$$

Note the alignment: the shift $2^{k\alpha}$ is absorbed into the table content, so higher tables store *more significant* — not more numerous — bits. Every table shares the same output LSB $\ell_R-g$, because a table more accurate than its siblings is accurate in vain.

The budget, again in two pieces (p. 391–392). Fixed-point addition is errorless, so the only errors are the $D$ table roundings and the final one:

$$
|\delta_{\text{round}}|<D\times2^{\ell_R-g-1},\quad \delta_{\text{final round}}\le2^{\ell_R-1}
\;\Longrightarrow\;\overline\delta_{\text{total}}=2^{\ell_R-1}+D\times2^{\ell_R-g-1} \tag{12.35}
$$

Faithfulness $\overline\delta_{\text{total}}<2^{\ell_R}$ reduces, after dividing through by $2^{\ell_R-1}$, to $D\,2^{-g}<1$, i.e. $\boxed{g>\log_2D}$ (eq. 12.36, p. 392). Two adjustments. The final round to nearest costs nothing: pre-add the half-ulp $2^{\ell_R-1}$ into one table (for free, it is a constant) and truncate. And with half-unit-biased rounding ([[Rounding]]) the final error tightens to $2^{\ell_R-1}(1-2^{-g})$, relaxing the requirement to $g>\log_2(D-1)$ — not implemented in FloPoCo (eq. 12.38, p. 393).

The generic bound is pessimistic for special constants, and FloPoCo tightens it per constant, measuring in *gulps* (units of $2^{\ell_R-g}$) so a constant can report its error before $g$ is known (p. 393): $C=0$ and $C=\pm2^n$ with no bits lost contribute zero; a $C$ that is an integer multiple of $2^{\ell_R-\ell_X}$ produces nothing below $2^{\ell_R}$ and contributes zero; only the general case costs $D/2$ gulps. A table whose MSB falls below $\ell_R-g$ is dropped entirely, and the analysis stays valid (Fig. 12.16, p. 394).

## Multiplication by a rational constant

A rational $C/D$ has an *eventually periodic* binary expansion, and the period is a structure the generic algorithms cannot see.

**Lemma 12.1 (p. 396).** For an irreducible $C/D<1$ with $2\nmid C$ and $2\nmid D$, the binary expansion is *purely* periodic. Powers of two are not a constraint — they are shifts, factored out as $2^e$. If $C>D$, Euclidean division $C=HD+C'$ splits the constant into an integer header $H$ (handled by the previous sections) and a purely periodic fraction $C'/D$.

**Lemma 12.2 (p. 396).** The period $s$ is the multiplicative order of $2$ modulo $D$, and the pattern is $P=\lfloor2^sC/D\rfloor$. *Proof:* $2^s=kD+1$, hence $P=\left\lfloor\frac{(kD+1)C}{D}\right\rfloor=\lfloor kC+C/D\rfloor=kC$ since $C/D<1$; therefore $2^sC/D=P+C/D$, and that self-similarity *is* the periodicity. Examples: $1/3$ has $s=2$, $P=01$; $5/9$ has $s=6$, $P=100011$.

### Periodical shift-and-add graphs

Doubling the period per stage turns the expansion into a logarithmic-depth graph. Set $\pi_0=2^{-s}PX$ and $\pi_{i+1}=\pi_i+2^{-2^is}\pi_i$; then $\pi_i$ is $X$ times an approximation of $C/D$ on $2^is$ bits (p. 398–399). Each addition **doubles the number of correct bits** — the same repetition-squaring structure as a prefix tree — so a constant with $2^i$ repetitions of the period costs $i$ additions, which is optimal (Gustafsson, p. 399). One builds $PX$ (and $HX$ if $H\ne0$) by any SCM method, iterates until $2^is\ge w_0-w_H$, and finishes with a smaller last addition $R=\pi_j+2^{-2^is}\pi_i$ for the least $j$ with $(2^i+2^j)s\ge w_0-w_H$. For a $24$-bit mantissa times $7/5$ at $w_0=28$ ($P=0110_2$, $H=1$, $s=4$): $i=3$, five adders of widths $24\to26\to30\to38\to54\to56$ (Fig. 12.17, p. 399).

The honest caveats are the book's own (p. 400–401): periodicity only pays when the period is short, i.e. small denominators; the optimal SCM algorithms of §12.1.3–12.1.4 will find an equally good graph *once the rational is rounded*, they are just slower and scale worse; and dividing by small constants such as $3$ or $5$ is often better served by the specializations in [[3.2) Division by Constants]].

### Table-based rational multiplication

The same periodicity shows up in the KCM tables: every row of the table for $X_i/3$ has the same denominator, so the whole table is eventually periodic (Fig. 12.18, p. 401). A $22$-bit-wide table collapses to $5$ LUTs; in general the table for $A/B\cdot X_i$ with period $s$ needs about $\alpha+s$ LUTs, only the top $\alpha$ bits being aperiodic. Synthesis tools find this without being told, and the benefit is bounded — it shrinks the tables, not the KCM adders.

## Multiple constant multiplication

### MCM using shift-and-add

MCM multiplies **one input by several constants**, the operation at the heart of an FIR filter, an FFT butterfly or a DCT. The opportunity is sharing intermediate factors. For $\{19,43\}$: optimal SCM solutions cost $2+3=5$ adders; inspection shows $3X$ appears in both, saving one; but the optimal MCM solution costs **three**, by noticing $43=19\cdot2+5$ (Fig. 12.19, p. 403) — a sharing that no amount of staring at the two separate graphs reveals.

The two state-of-the-art heuristics both begin with an *optimal part*: repeatedly move into the realized set $R$ every target reachable from $R$ with a single adder. If targets remain, they diverge. $\mathrm H_{\mathrm{cub}}$ (Voronenko & Püschel) inserts a non-output fundamental chosen to maximize the cumulative benefit $B(R,s,t)=\mathrm{dist}(R,t)-\mathrm{dist}(R\cup\{s\},t)$ over remaining targets, where $\mathrm{dist}$ is the minimum number of $\mathcal A$-operations to reach $t$ (eq. 12.41, p. 404). DiffAG (Gustafsson) instead builds a complete graph on coefficient sets, computes *difference sets* between them, and merges the pair whose difference is already realized — better for large target sets with low coefficient word size (p. 404).

ILP extends by adding an output-selection variable $o_{a,j}$ ("adder $a$ realizes target $j$") replacing the single output constraint, with lower bound $N_{\mathrm{A,lb,MCM}}=N_{\mathrm{uq}}+\min_{t\in T}\lceil\log_2\mathrm{nz}(t)\rceil-1$ (eq. 12.43, p. 405), where $N_{\mathrm{uq}}$ counts the positive, odd, unique coefficients. When throughput matters the graph must be pipelined, and registers then dominate: ASAP-pipelining an $\mathrm H_{\mathrm{cub}}$ solution for $\{23,43\}$ needs nine extra registers, five purely to balance the pipeline, whereas RPAG finds a graph with the *same adder count* and only four registers — and on FPGAs those come free with the LUTs (Fig. 12.20, p. 406).

### Table-based MCM

The sharing opportunity in KCM is different: identical LUT *contents* between different constant multipliers. That sounds hopeless — an $\alpha$-input LUT has $2^{2^\alpha}$ possible fillings — but the tables are far from random. Their columns are quasi-periodic, strings of zeroes followed by strings of ones (Fig. 12.21, p. 408), and consequently only **$52$ distinct $1$-bit tables** (out of $65536$ possible for $\alpha=4$) suffice for all signed products by all constants from $1$ to $2^{12}$ (Faust et al., p. 407). Allowing each constant to drift inside a small error interval and choosing with 0-1 ILP cuts architectural LUTs by up to $20\%$ (Aksoy et al.). The general result: **on FPGAs, table-based methods give lower area and better delay for low input word sizes** (p. 407).

### Sum of constant products

SOPC computes $R=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}C_iX_i$ — many inputs, one output, the transpose of MCM. That is literal: reversing every edge of an MCM signal-flow graph turns branches into adders and adders into branches, and an MCM adder graph for a coefficient set becomes an SOPC adder graph for the same set (Fig. 12.22, p. 408–409). One graph, two operators.

### Constant matrix-vector multiplication

CMM allows several inputs *and* several outputs. Complex multiplication is the canonical instance:

$$\begin{pmatrix}Y_{\mathrm r}\\Y_{\mathrm i}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}19&-43\\43&19\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}X_{\mathrm r}\\X_{\mathrm i}\end{pmatrix} \tag{12.46, p.\,410}$$

Two MCM blocks plus two final adders cost eight additions; optimizing the matrix as a unit finds six, by sharing $(X_{\mathrm r}\pm X_{\mathrm i})$ across both outputs (Fig. 12.23, p. 411). Transposing an $M\times N$ CMM's adder graph yields a graph with $N_A-N+M$ adders, so for square matrices transposition is cost-neutral — and since graph algorithms scale better with rows than columns, when $N>M$ it is faster to solve the transposed problem and transpose the answer back (p. 411).

### Table-based sum of products by real constants

Fuse everything into one bit heap. Each $X_i$ splits into $D_i$ chunks, giving a double sum with one table per (input, chunk) pair:

$$R=\left\lfloor\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\sum_{k=0}^{D_i-1}2^{k\alpha+\ell_i}T_{ik}\right\rceil_{\ell_R},\qquad T_{ik}(X_{ik})=\left\lfloor2^{k\alpha+\ell_i}C_iX_{ik}\right\rceil_{\ell_R-g} \tag{12.49–12.50, p.\,413}$$

All $ND$ tables share one $g$ and one final rounding, so the worst-case bound generalizes directly:

$$g>\log_2(ND) \tag{12.51, p.\,413}$$

The gain is exactly the two intermediate roundings that a naive composition would commit. For $R=C_1X+C_2Y$ built from two separately-faithful KCMs, each must be accurate to $2^{\ell_R-2}$, forcing its internal tables to $\ell_R-4$; fused, all six tables need only $\ell_R-3$ (p. 395). With per-constant error bounds the requirement tightens further to $g>1+\log_2\overline{\delta^{(\mathrm{gulp})}_{\text{round}}}$ (eq. 12.52, p. 414). A $4$-tap half-sine FIR with $\ell_X=\ell_R=-12$ and $5$-bit chunks lands at $g=4$ (Fig. 12.25, p. 413).

> [!warning] Recursive filters are out of scope here
> An IIR SOPC re-injects its output into its own summation, and the infinite recursion needs an error analysis this chapter does not give (p. 414). Same for the freedom a frequency-response specification leaves in choosing the coefficients — both are deferred to the filter chapter, see [[6.1) LTI Filters]].

## Other FPGA-specific techniques

### Constant multiplication using DSP blocks

When DSP blocks are plentiful the constant multiplication is one instruction and there is nothing to optimize. The interesting regime is *scarcity*, where DSPs and adder graphs are mixed. The Xilinx DSP48E has an asymmetric $17\times24$-bit multiplier, so a $24\times24$ product cascades two blocks through the post-adder; when one argument is constant, the split has a free parameter:

$$c=c_{\mathrm{Low}}\pm2^Bc_{\mathrm{High}} \tag{12.53, p.\,415}$$

and $B$ may be chosen anywhere the parts fit the input widths. For $11508221$ the book lists five valid splits ($104957+2^{17}\cdot87$, $39421+2^{16}\cdot175$, …). This degree of freedom is what lets a search *share* sub-constants across different filter taps, or steer one part towards a cheap shift-and-add. A hybrid (P)MCM search inside RPAG cuts a five-coefficient single-precision filter from ten DSP blocks to seven (Fig. 12.27, p. 416). Recent Intel DSPs have symmetric inputs, which removes the split freedom, but carry a small coefficient memory holding up to eight constants — which reframes them as the next section's operator. See [[DSP Blocks]].

### Reconfigurable constant multiplication

Sometimes $C$ is drawn from a small set $\{C_0,\dots,C_N\}$, selected by $\lceil\log_2N\rceil$ control bits — time-multiplexed filters, or CNN weights that change between layers. A **reconfigurable constant coefficient multiplier (RCCM)** fuses the adder graphs of all the constants into a single graph with MUXes on the adder inputs, the select lines driven by the index. Fig. 12.28 (p. 417) multiplies by $C\in\{45,19\}$ with two adders and two MUXes: all MUXes left gives $(2^2+1)(2^3+1)X=45X$, all right gives $(2^1+1)X+2^4X=19X$.

The FPGA alternative is to reconfigure the KCM tables themselves through the LUT reconfiguration port. That costs about $32$ clock cycles on recent Xilinx parts (p. 417), which rules it out for cycle-by-cycle switching but is fine for weights that change per layer or per frame.

## Choosing a technique

| Technique | Adders | LUTs / area | Logic depth | Scaling in $w_X$ | Scaling in $w_C$ | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Binary shift-and-add | $\mathrm{nz}(C)-1$ | $\approx$ sum of adder widths | $\lceil\log_2\mathrm{nz}(C)\rceil$ if treed | linear (adders widen) | linear in $\mathrm{nz}$ | p. 367 |
| CSD shift-and-add | $\le\lceil\frac{n+1}{2}\rceil-1$ | same, but subtracters lose free LSBs | same | linear | halves the $w_C$ slope | p. 368 |
| Minimal-adder graph / ILP | optimal, $\ge\lceil\log_2\mathrm{nz}(C)\rceil$ | *not* minimized — see $49X$: $25$ vs $16$ FA-eq. at equal adder count | small, but unconstrained unless modelled | linear | search time explodes: $12$ bit $<1$ s, $16$ bit up to minutes, $20$ bit up to $1$ h | pp. 373, 378, 382 |
| Ternary-adder graph | $\le3$ for all $C\le22$ bits | $\approx1/3$ less than 2-input for $w_C>5$ | $\le3$ | linear | flat to $22$ bits | pp. 378–380 |
| Full tabulation | $0$ | $w_X+w_C$ LUTs if $w_X\le\alpha$; $\times2^k$ if $w_X=\alpha+k$ | $1$ LUT ($k=0$) | **exponential** | linear | p. 383 |
| KCM | $\lceil w_X/\alpha\rceil-1$, each $w_C$ wide | $\lceil\frac{w_X}{\alpha}\rceil(w_C+\alpha)+(\lceil\frac{w_X}{\alpha}\rceil-1)w_C$ | 1 LUT + adder rake (or a compressor tree) | linear, slope $\frac{w_C+2\alpha}{\alpha}$ | linear | p. 385 |
| Fix-by-real KCM | same, $+g$ guard bits | as above with $w_C\to m_R-\ell_R+g$, $g>\log_2D$ | same | linear | independent of $C$'s bit pattern | pp. 390–392 |
| DSP-based | $0$ (or 1 post-adder per split) | $1$–$2$ DSP blocks, no LUTs | 1–2 DSP stages, pipelined | flat until inputs exceed $17/24$ bits, then $\times2$ | flat until $c$ exceeds the split | pp. 415–416 |

**ASIC: shift-and-add, always** (p. 418). There is no LUT fabric to tabulate into, exact optimal graphs are computable for most practical constants, and good heuristics cover the rest.

**FPGA: KCM by default, shift-and-add for really simple constants** (p. 418). The rule of thumb inverts the ASIC one because the LUT is free area you have already paid for, and because KCM's cost is flat in the constant's bit pattern — a $2$-adder constant and a $12$-adder constant cost the same KCM. Below $\alpha$ input bits, drop to full tabulation. Above, the deciding question is $w_C$ versus $w_X$: KCM cost is driven by $w_X$ (chunk count), shift-and-add cost by $w_C$ (nonzero count), so wide constants times narrow inputs favour KCM and the reverse favours shift-and-add. The floating-point exponential's $\times\log2$ is the extreme case — a small integer input, an enormous constant, and many result bits: KCM without hesitation (p. 418).

**Everything else: generate both and measure.** The book's own conclusion is that the final choice belongs to the generator, evaluated on trial and error against real synthesis numbers — and admits FloPoCo does not yet do this (p. 418). See [[7.1) FloPoCo]].

## Worked example: $C=683=\texttt{0x2AB}$, $w_X=8$

$683=1010101011_2$, ten bits, six ones. Take $X$ unsigned $8$-bit, $X_{\max}=255$, product $683\cdot255=174165<2^{18}$, so $w_R=18$. Cost is counted in **full-adder equivalents**: an adder computing $P+2^sX$ whose result is $r$ bits costs $r-s$ cells, since the low $s$ bits of $P$ pass through as wires.

**1. Naive binary shift-and-add.** Six nonzeros at positions $0,1,3,5,7,9$, so five adders, accumulated LSB-first:

| step | value | binary of coefficient | result bits | cells |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $A_1=X+2^1X$ | $3X$ | $11$ | $0..9$ | $9$ |
| $A_2=A_1+2^3X$ | $11X$ | $1011$ | $0..11$ | $9$ |
| $A_3=A_2+2^5X$ | $43X$ | $101011$ | $0..13$ | $9$ |
| $A_4=A_3+2^7X$ | $171X$ | $10101011$ | $0..15$ | $9$ |
| $A_5=A_4+2^9X$ | $683X$ | $1010101011$ | $0..17$ | $9$ |

**$5$ adders, $45$ FA-equivalents, logic depth $5$ adders.**

**2. CSD.** Run the digit recurrence on $683$: it is odd with $683\equiv3\pmod4$, emit $\bar1$, and $684/2=342$ is even — and the pattern repeats all the way up:

$$683=10\bar10\bar10\bar10\bar10\bar1_{\mathrm{CSD}}=2^{10}-2^8-2^6-2^4-2^2-2^0$$

Check: $1024-256-64-16-4-1=683$. Eleven digits, six nonzeros, and $\lceil11/2\rceil=6$ — the bound of the previous section is *tight* here, which is the tell: **CSD buys nothing for $683$.** Its binary form is already alternating apart from the trailing $11$, and the run-of-ones identity has nothing to chew on. Worse, at bit level the single long subtraction $2^{10}X-341X$ has no free low bits — the negation must ripple from bit $0$ — costing about $18$ cells instead of $9$ and pushing the total to $\approx54$. Contrast $93=1011101_2$ ($5$ nonzeros $\to$ $4$ adders), whose CSD $10\bar100\bar101$ has $4$ nonzeros $\to$ $3$ adders: CSD pays exactly in proportion to how many runs of ones the constant contains.

**3. Minimal adder graph** (the TikZ figure above, Fig. 12.6a): $17X=X+2^4X$ ($13$ bits, $9$ cells), $3X=X+2^1X$ ($10$ bits, $9$ cells), $85X=17X+2^2\cdot17X$ ($15$ bits, $13$ cells), $683X=2^3\cdot85X+3X$ ($18$ bits, $15$ cells). **$4$ adders, $46$ FA-equivalents, logic depth $3$.** One adder *fewer* than the binary chain and one full adder *more* — precisely the §12.1.6 warning that adder count is not area. The win is the depth: $3$ instead of $5$. With ternary adders the same constant needs only $2$ adders (Fig. 12.6b, p. 379).

**4. KCM**, $\alpha=4$, $w_C=10$, $D=\lceil8/4\rceil=2$ chunks $X_0=x_{3..0}$, $X_1=x_{7..4}$:

$$\#\mathrm{LUTs}=2\cdot(10+4)+1\cdot10=38$$

Two $16$-entry tables ($14$ LUTs each, storing $683X_0$ and $683X_1$ up to $683\cdot15=10245$), one $10$-bit adder. **$38$ LUTs, depth $=1$ LUT $+$ one $10$-bit carry chain.** Cheaper *and* shallower than every shift-and-add variant — the FPGA rule of thumb of p. 418 in one line. And it would cost the same $38$ LUTs for any other $10$-bit constant, including the ones for which no clever graph exists.

### The real-constant case: $C=\pi/4$

Same $X$ (ufix$(7,0)$, $X_{\max}=255$), asking for $\ell_R=-4$, so $\mathrm{ulp}=2^{-4}=0.0625$ and $m_R=7$ (since $255\cdot0.7854=200.3$).

**Shift-and-add route.** The constant budget is

$$\overline\delta_C=\frac{2^{\ell_R-1}}{X_{\max}}=\frac{2^{-5}}{255}=1.2255\times10^{-4}$$

$\ell=-12$ gives $2^{\ell-1}=2^{-13}=1.2207\times10^{-4}<\overline\delta_C$ (just), while $\ell=-11$ gives $2.44\times10^{-4}>\overline\delta_C$. So $\ell_{\max}=-12$ and $\widetilde C=\lfloor\pi/4\rceil_{-12}=3217\cdot2^{-12}$, since $\pi/4\cdot4096=3216.99$. In binary $3217=110010010001_2=2^{11}+2^{10}+2^7+2^4+2^0$: five nonzeros, four adders. CSD folds the leading $11$ into $2^{12}-2^{10}$, giving $10\bar10010010001_{\mathrm{CSD}}=2^{12}-2^{10}+2^7+2^4+2^0$ — still five nonzeros, still four adders. The actual constant error is $|3217/4096-\pi/4|=2.2\times10^{-6}$, a factor $55$ inside budget, which is slack worth spending: per p. 389 one would now search $\widetilde C$ at $\ell=-13,-14,\dots$ for a cheaper graph, or truncate full adders out of this one.

**KCM route.** $\alpha=4\Rightarrow D=\lceil(7-0+1)/4\rceil=2$, so $g>\log_2 2=1$, take $g=2$; tables round at $\ell_R-g=-6$. Take $X=173=10101101_2$, i.e. $X_0=1101_2=13$, $X_1=1010_2=10$.

$$T_0(13)=\left\lfloor\tfrac{\pi}{4}\cdot13\right\rceil_{-6}=\lfloor10.2101761\rceil_{-6}=\tfrac{653}{64}=10.203125$$
$$T_1(10)=\left\lfloor2^4\cdot\tfrac{\pi}{4}\cdot10\right\rceil_{-6}=\lfloor125.6637061\rceil_{-6}=\tfrac{8042}{64}=125.65625$$

$$S=T_0+T_1=135.859375,\qquad \delta_{\text{round}}=S-\tfrac{\pi}{4}\cdot173=-0.014507$$

which respects, with little to spare, $|\delta_{\text{round}}|<D\cdot2^{\ell_R-g-1}=2\cdot2^{-7}=0.015625$. The half-ulp $2^{\ell_R-1}=2^{-5}$ is pre-added into $T_0$, so the final round is a truncation:

$$R=\left\lfloor135.859375+0.03125\right\rfloor_{-4}=\left\lfloor135.890625\right\rfloor_{-4}=\frac{2174}{16}=135.875$$

Exact value $\frac{\pi}{4}\cdot173=135.8738823$, so

$$\delta_{\text{total}}=135.875-135.8738823=+1.118\times10^{-3}=\boxed{0.018\ \mathrm{ulp}}$$

against the guaranteed bound $\overline\delta_{\text{total}}=2^{-5}+2\cdot2^{-7}=0.046875=0.75$ ulp $<1$ ulp. **Faithful.** Cost: $T_0$ spans $\ell=-6$ to $m=3$ ($10$ LUTs), $T_1$ spans $-6$ to $7$ ($14$ LUTs), plus a $14$-bit adder — about $38$ LUTs, one LUT plus one carry chain deep. Dropping to $g=1$ would give $\overline\delta_{\text{total}}=2^{-5}+2^{-6}\cdot2=0.0625=1$ ulp exactly, which fails the strict inequality: $g=2$ is not slack, it is the minimum.
