**Specification.** Input: a fixed-point significand $M \in [1,4)$ in `ufix(1,`$\ell$`)` for the square root (other functions rescale similarly); the floating-point case reduces to this by [[#Range reduction for floating-point and fixed-point binary]]. Output: $S_n \approx f(M)$ in `ufix(1,-n)`, one radix-$\beta$ digit produced per cycle. Accuracy: the recurrence maintains an **exact** residual, so the raw result is a round-down to $n$ digits ($<1$ ulp) and last-bit accuracy — round-to-nearest, $\le \tfrac12$ ulp — costs exactly one extra iteration, not a second pass. What is optimised: **area** and per-iteration **critical path**; latency is $O(n/\log\beta)$ cycles and is the price paid.

Every decision below traces back to that line. There is no multiplier in the datapath because the digit set is small enough that "multiply by $d$" is a multiplexer; the residual is kept in carry-save because the per-iteration adder is the critical path; the digit set is made redundant because deciding a digit from a carry-save number otherwise needs a full carry propagation.

> [!important] One idea, four algorithms
> An **algebraic function** is one defined implicitly as the solution of a polynomial equation in the result. $Q$ with $Q \cdot D - N = 0$ is the linear case — that is division, [[2.5) Dividers]]. $S$ with $S^2 - M = 0$ is the quadratic case, the square root. Cube root is cubic, the Euclidean norm is quadratic in three variables, and the E-method turns *any* rational function into a linear **system** $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{b}$.
>
> In every case the algorithm is the same: keep a **residual** $W_i$ that measures how far the current partial result $S_i$ is from satisfying the defining equation, choose the next digit $d_{-i-1}$ so that the residual stays inside a bound, append the digit, scale the residual by $\beta$, repeat. The residual is exactly computable because the defining equation is polynomial — that is the whole reason "algebraic" is the right class. Everything that differs between the four is *how hard the digit-selection inequality is to satisfy*.

## Digit-recurrence square root

### Range reduction for floating-point and fixed-point binary

The square root is defined on positive reals only, and its binary range reduction rests on the identity $\sqrt{2^{2p}x} = 2^p\sqrt x$ — the exponent must be **even** for the shift to stay in the format. Given a normalised $X = 2^E\cdot 1.F$ with $1.F\in[1,2)$: if $E$ is even take $2p=E$; if $E$ is odd, *denormalise by one bit*, $X = 2^{E-1}\cdot(2\times1.F)$, so that $2p = E-1$ is even and the significand moves into $[2,4)$. Merging the branches,

$$
\sqrt{2^E\cdot 1.F} = 2^{E'/2}\sqrt{M},\qquad
\begin{cases} E'=E,\; M=1.F & E \text{ even}\\ E'=E-1,\; M=2\times1.F & E \text{ odd}\end{cases}
$$

so $M\in[1,4)$ and the exponent path is a decrement plus a one-bit right shift — no adder in the critical path. A fixed-point input with a large range is best converted to floating point first with a normaliser ([[2.6) Shifters]]: leading-zero counter plus shifter), then shifted back afterwards. This matters most for methods that exploit differentiability, since $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is **not differentiable at 0** and polynomial approximation ([[4.3) Polynomial Approximation]]) degrades badly there; plain tabulation does not care, and does not need the reduction. *(pp. 574–575, Fig. 19.1.)*

### Generic radix-$\beta$ formulation

Digits come out most-significant first, so after $i$ iterations we hold

$$S_i = \sum_{j=0}^{i}\beta^{-j}d_{-j}$$

Because $M\in[1,4)$ gives $\sqrt{M}\in[1,2)$, the leading digit is free: $d_0=1$, $S_0=1$.

The error $S_i - \sqrt M$ is not computable inside the algorithm — $\sqrt M$ is exactly what we are looking for. So measure the residual through the **defining polynomial** instead: $S_i^2 - M$ is a polynomial in known quantities, hence exact. This substitution is the entire trick, and it is available precisely because $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is algebraic.

Start from integers to fix the bound. An integer square root is $N = S^2 + R$ with $S^2 \le N < (S+1)^2$; setting $R = N - S^2$ this is $0 \le R < 2S+1$. Move to ulp $\beta^{-i}$: the unicity condition becomes $S_i^2 \le M < (S_i+\beta^{-i})^2$, and expanding the square gives $0 \le R_i' < 2\beta^{-i}S_i + \beta^{-2i}$ for $R_i' = M - S_i^2$. Multiplying through by $\beta^i$ defines the **scaled remainder**

$$W_i = \beta^i\left(M - S_i^2\right),\qquad\text{with invariant}\qquad 0 \le W_i < 2S_i + \beta^{-i}$$

The scaling is what makes the bound scale-invariant across iterations. As with division, the bound can be recentred to target round-to-nearest rather than round-down:

$$-S_i \;\le\; W_i \;<\; S_i + \beta^{-i-1}$$

**The recurrence.** Substitute $S_{i+1}=S_i+\beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}$ and expand the square:

$$\left(S_i + \beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}\right)^2 = S_i^2 \;+\; \underbrace{2\beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}S_i}_{\text{cross term}} \;+\; \beta^{-2i-2}d_{-i-1}^2$$

Then, by the definition $W_{i+1} = \beta^{i+1}(M - S_{i+1}^2)$ and distributing $\beta^{i+1}$ over the three terms,

$$\boxed{\;W_{i+1} = \beta W_i \;-\; 2\,d_{-i-1}S_i \;-\; \beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}^2\;}$$

> [!note] Why square root is harder than division
> Division's recurrence is $W_{i+1} = \beta W_i - d_{-i-1}D$: the multiplicand $D$ is a **constant**, known before iteration 0, so the digit-selection boundaries are fixed lines and the P-D diagram is drawn once. Here the cross term is $2d_{-i-1}S_i$ — the multiplicand is $S_i$, which is *being built by the very digits we are selecting*. The selection region therefore changes shape at every iteration, and the trailing $\beta^{-i-1}d^2$ term means the boundary lines do not even pass through the origin. That term decays geometrically in $i$, which is why a single selection function can be shared for all $i \ge i_0$ once $i_0$ is large enough. *(pp. 575–576, 581–582, Figs. 19.4–19.6.)*

### Simple binary restoring square root

In binary $d_{-i}\in\{0,1\}$, so the laziest possible selection works: **assume $d_{-i}=1$, do the subtraction, look at the sign.** If it went negative the invariant $W_{i+1}\ge0$ is violated, so the digit was really 0 and the subtraction is undone (restored).

$$d_0 = 1,\quad S_0 = 1,\quad W_0 = M-1$$
$$T_{i+1} = 2W_i - \left(2S_i + 2^{-i-1}\right)$$
$$
d_{-i-1} = \begin{cases}1 & T_{i+1}\ge0\\ 0 & T_{i+1}<0\end{cases}\qquad
W_{i+1} = \begin{cases}T_{i+1} & T_{i+1}\ge0\\ 2W_i & T_{i+1}<0\end{cases}
$$
$$S_{i+1} = S_i + 2^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}$$

Two things make this cheap and both are format observations, not arithmetic:

- $S_i$ is in `ufix(0,-i)`, so appending $d_{-i-1}$ is **bit concatenation** — $S_{i+1}$ needs no adder.
- $2S_i$ is `ufix(1,-i+1)`, so $2S_i + 2^{-i-1}$ is obtained by **appending the two bits $01_2$** to the right of $2S_i$, giving a `ufix(1,-i-1)` number. Again no adder.

So the only real hardware is one subtracter and one mux. Furthermore $T_{i+1}$ is `sfix(1,-i-1)`, and the effective subtraction only touches positions down to $-i-1$: everything below is copied straight from $2W_i$, and recursively from $M-1$. Unrolled in space, **iteration $i$ needs a subtracter on just $i+3$ bits** — the early iterations are fast and narrow, exactly like paper-and-pencil division consuming one new dividend digit per step. *(pp. 576–578, Fig. 19.2.)*

Rounding to nearest at precision $\ell$ comes for free: run $n = -\ell+1$ iterations and use the extra digit $d_{-n}$ as the round bit, $S_{n-1}+2^{\ell}d_{-n}$. Ties round up, but ties essentially never happen for a square root ([[1.2) Error Accuracy]]).

### Binary non-restoring square root

The restore step costs a mux and, worse, serialises a decision. As for division, keep the **tentative** remainder $T_i$ as the state instead of $W_i$ and fold the restore into the next iteration's sign. Two cases:

- Previous iteration had $T_i \ge 0$, so $d_{-i}=1$ and $W_i = T_i$; substituting directly gives $T_{i+1} = 2T_i - (2S_i + 2^{-i-1})$.
- Previous iteration had $T_i < 0$, so $d_{-i}=0$ and the restore says $W_i = T_i + 2S_{i-1} + 2^{-i}$. Substituting and using $S_i = S_{i-1}$ (the digit was 0):
$$T_{i+1} = 2\left(T_i + 2S_{i-1} + 2^{-i}\right) - 2S_i - 2^{-i-1} = 2T_i + \left(2S_i + 2^{-i+1} - 2^{-i-1}\right)$$
and $2^{-i+1}-2^{-i-1} = 3\cdot2^{-i-1}$, i.e. the two-bit pattern $11_2$ at position $-i-1$.

The single formula $2^{-i}\overline{d_{-i}} + 2^{-i-1}$ covers both cases ($d=1 \Rightarrow$ the pattern $01$; $d=0\Rightarrow$ the pattern $11$), giving the non-restoring algorithm:

$$S_{-1}=0,\qquad T_0 = M-1$$
$$d_{-i} = \begin{cases}1 & T_i\ge0\\0&T_i<0\end{cases}\qquad S_i = S_{i-1} + 2^{-i}d_{-i}$$
$$
U_i = 2S_i + 2^{-i}\overline{d_{-i}} + 2^{-i-1}\qquad
T_{i+1} = \begin{cases}2T_i + U_i & d_{-i}=0\\ 2T_i - U_i & d_{-i}=1\end{cases}
$$

The hardware is now **one inverter and one adder/subtracter**: the digit is literally the negated sign bit of $T_i$, and $S_i$ and $U_i$ are concatenations. $U_i$ is `ufix(1,-i-1)`, so the effective add/sub again only spans positions $-i-1$ up to $2$ — $i+4$ bits, one more than the restoring version because the sign must be carried. *(pp. 578–580, Fig. 19.3.)*

### Exploiting redundant number systems in the square root

Return to the general recurrence. Its critical path is the carry propagation inside $\beta W_i - 2d_{-i-1}S_i - \beta^{-i-1}d^2$, and that path grows with $i$ as $S_i$ widens. The obvious fix — keep $W_i$ in carry-save ([[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]]) — fails immediately: you cannot read the *sign* of a carry-save number without propagating the carry, and the sign is exactly what the digit selection needs.

The rescue is the same one SRT uses for division, and it is worth reading there first: [[2.5) Dividers#SRT Division]]. Take digits from a **redundant** digit set $d_{-j}\in\{-\gamma,\dots,\gamma\}$ ([[1.1) Number Formats]]). Redundancy means several digit choices are legal at each step, which widens the selection intervals enough that a few **leading** digits of an approximate $W_i$ suffice to pick a valid one — so the carry propagation inside an iteration is limited to those few digits.

Formally, define the absolute error $\delta_i = S_i - \sqrt M$ and look for the invariant $|\delta_i| \le \rho\beta^{-i}$. Imposing it on $\delta_{i+1} = \delta_i + \beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}$ requires $|\delta_i + \beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}| \le \rho\beta^{-i-1}$. The worst positive error $\delta_i = \rho\beta^{-i}$ must be correctable by the most negative digit $-\gamma$, giving $\rho\beta^{-i} - \gamma\beta^{-i-1} \le \rho\beta^{-i-1}$, i.e. $\rho \le \gamma/(\beta-1)$. Taking the largest admissible value — more freedom is always better here — recovers **the same redundancy factor as division**:

$$\rho = \frac{\gamma}{\beta-1}$$

a property of the number system alone, not of the function being computed. Now convert that error bound into a bound on the residual. From $-\rho\beta^{-i} \le S_i - \sqrt M \le \rho\beta^{-i}$ all three members are positive, so squaring preserves the order, giving $S_i^2 + \rho^2\beta^{-2i} \mp 2\rho\beta^{-i}S_i$ as the two outer members; subtracting $S_i^2$ and multiplying by $\beta^i$ turns the middle into $\beta^i(M-S_i^2)=W_i$ by definition:

$$-2\rho S_i + \rho^2\beta^{-i} \;\le\; W_i \;\le\; 2\rho S_i + \rho^2\beta^{-i}$$

Imposing this on $W_{i+1}$ and substituting the recurrence plus $S_{i+1}=S_i+\beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}$ collapses to the pair of selection constraints

$$2(d_{-i-1}-\rho)S_i + \beta^{-i-1}(d_{-i-1}-\rho)^2 \;\le\; \beta W_i \;\le\; 2(d_{-i-1}+\rho)S_i + \beta^{-i-1}(d_{-i-1}+\rho)^2$$

These are the boundary lines of the P-D diagram, plotted with $S_i$ on the $x$-axis and $\beta W_i$ on the $y$-axis. The $\beta^{-i-1}(d\pm\rho)^2$ offsets are why the lines miss the origin, and they shrink geometrically. Drawn for $\beta=4$, $\gamma=2$ at iteration $i=1$:

**Truncation, and how much of $W_i$ the selection actually sees.** Truncating the carry-save $\beta W_i$ at position $t_W$ gives $\beta\widetilde{W_i} = \beta W_i + \delta$ with $\delta \in \{0, -2^{t_W}\}$ — a **one-sided** approximation, $\beta\widetilde{W_i}\le\beta W_i \le \beta\widetilde{W_i}+2^{t_W}$. Tightening both constraints by that gap is enough, so the missing carry information simply shrinks the selection interval:

$$
2(d_{-i-1}-\rho)S_i + \beta^{-i-1}(d_{-i-1}-\rho)^2 \;\le\; \beta\widetilde{W_i},\qquad
\beta\widetilde{W_i} + 2^{t_W} \;\le\; 2(d_{-i-1}+\rho)S_i + \beta^{-i-1}(d_{-i-1}+\rho)^2
$$

For $\beta=4$, $\gamma=2$ (hence $\rho=2/3$), Ercegovac and Lang's analysis finds a single selection function valid for all $i \ge i_0 = 3$, using $t_W = -4$ (**4 bits of $\widetilde{W_i}$**) and $t_S = -4$ (**4 leading fractional bits of $S_i$**). The alternative to that analysis is a programmatic sweep: enumerate candidate $(t_W,t_S)$ by increasing table cost $2^{t_W+t_S}$, prefer the smaller $t_W$ at equal cost (it gives the shorter latency), and exhaustively check feasibility with directed rounding. *(pp. 584–587.)*

**Initialisation.** With a redundant digit set the free $d_0=1$ is no longer always valid: for $M\in[2,4)$ starting from $S_0=1$ gives $W_0\in[0,3)$, exceeding the bound $2\rho S_0+\rho^2 \approx 1.778$. Choosing $d_0 = 2$ for $M\in[2,4)$ and $d_0=1$ otherwise restores it. The alternative is **tabulation**: look up $S_{i_0}$ (and, to cut latency, $S_{i_0}^2$) from the leading bits of $M$. Splitting the tabulation error as

$$
\delta_{i_0} = \underbrace{S_{i_0}-\sqrt{\widetilde M + 2^{\ell-1}}}_{\text{rounding, } \le\, 2^{-\alpha i_0-1}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sqrt{\widetilde M + 2^{\ell-1}}-\sqrt M}_{\text{input truncation}}
\;=\;\dots+\;\frac{\widetilde M + 2^{\ell-1}-M}{\sqrt{\widetilde M+2^{\ell-1}}+\sqrt M}
$$

where the second term was rationalised by multiplying by its conjugate. With $|\widetilde M + 2^{\ell-1}-M|\le 2^{\ell-1}$ and both roots $\ge1$, this gives $|\delta_{i_0}| \le 2^{-\alpha i_0-1}+2^{\ell-2}$; forcing it under $\rho\beta^{-i_0}$ yields the input-width constraint

$$\ell \;\le\; 2 + \log_2\!\left(\rho-\tfrac12\right) - \alpha i_0$$

For $\beta=4,\gamma=2,\rho=2/3$ this admits $\ell=-\alpha i_0-1$; with $i_0=3$, $S_3$ is tabulated from the **8 leading bits of $M$**. Note the constraint is *infeasible* if the table rounds down instead of to nearest, because that needs $\rho>1$. *(pp. 582–585.)*

**On-the-fly conversion of $S_i$.** After tabulated initialisation the digits arrive redundant, so $S_i$ must be converted to standard binary before the selection function can truncate it. $S_{i+1}=S_i+\beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}$ is a concatenation when $d\ge0$ but a genuine borrow-propagating subtraction when $d<0$. The ASIC fix is the same on-the-fly converter used for division: carry a **pre-decremented** copy $S_i^{\ominus} = S_i - \beta^{-i}$ alongside $S_i$, so the subtraction result is always already available. Writing each digit in two's complement as $d_j = -\beta d_j^{\oplus} + d_j^{\ominus}$,

$$
d_{-i}>0:\;\begin{cases}S_i = S_{i-1}+\beta^{-i}d^{\oplus}_{-i}\\ S_i^{\ominus}=S_{i-1}+\beta^{-i}(d^{\oplus}_{-i}-1)\end{cases}\quad
d_{-i}=0:\;\begin{cases}S_i=S_{i-1}\\ S_i^{\ominus}=S_{i-1}^{\ominus}+\beta^{-i}(\beta-1)\end{cases}\quad
d_{-i}<0:\;\begin{cases}S_i=S_{i-1}^{\ominus}+\beta^{-i}d^{\ominus}_{-i}\\ S_i^{\ominus}=S_{i-1}^{\ominus}+\beta^{-i}(d^{\ominus}_{-i}-1)\end{cases}
$$

Every addition here is a concatenation, and induction on $i$ maintains the invariants $S_i = \sum_j\beta^jd_j$ and $S_i^{\ominus}=S_i-\beta^{-i}$. Cost per iteration: **two multiplexers the width of $S_i$ and two registers** in a pipelined design — fast, but not free in area. *(pp. 587–588.)*

## Cube root

Identical derivation, one degree higher. For $M\in[1,8)$, with $d_0=1$, $S_0=1$ and $W_i=\beta^i(M-S_i^3)$, expand the cube $\left(S_i+\beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}\right)^3$ by the binomial theorem and distribute $\beta^{i+1}$:

$$W_{i+1} = \beta W_i \;-\; 3d_{-i-1}S_i^2 \;-\; 3\beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}^2S_i \;-\; \beta^{-2i-2}d_{-i-1}^3$$

The new object is $S_i^2$ inside the recurrence, but it is itself computable by a **parallel digit recurrence** (the square of a number being built digit by digit), so the whole thing again reduces to additions and digit-by-number multiplications — still no multiplier. Digit selection imposes on each $W_i$ the constraint expected of the final remainder, exactly as for division and square root.

The radix-2 study with digit set $\{-1,0,1\}$ (Piñeiro et al.) shows digit selection can be decided from the **5 most significant digits of $W_i$**, and the recurrence maps to **four 4:2 compressor rows, one 3:2 compressor row, and four multiplexers** for the digit-by-number products. Against a multiplier-based cube root under the same ASIC models: the digit recurrence is **twice as slow but twice as small**, i.e. comparable area-time product — which the authors of the book suggest generalises as a rule of thumb.

The decisive asymmetry is rounding. Because the digit recurrence computes the remainder explicitly, it delivers **correct rounding** directly. A multiplier-based polynomial approximator gives only a faithful result, and deducing the correctly rounded cube root from it requires *cubing* the answer to test it — which is likely to eat the entire latency advantage. *(pp. 588–589.)*

## 2D and 3D Euclidean norms

Takagi and Kuwahara's algorithm evaluates $\sqrt{X^2+Y^2+Z^2}$ directly, for $\tfrac12\le X<1$, $0\le Y<1$, $0\le Z<1$, in radix $\beta$ with a redundant digit set. Two sequences run in parallel:

$$S_j = S_{j-1}+\beta^{-j}d_{-j},\qquad W_j \approx \beta^{-j}\!\left(S_j - X^2 - Y^2 - Z^2\right)$$

Note the $\approx$: for fixed-point $X,Y,Z$ the remainder *could* be exact, but it deliberately is not, because for floating-point inputs with widely separated exponents the exact $X^2+Y^2+Z^2$ needs an enormous number of bits. A truncated remainder therefore **forces** a redundant representation — the sign of an inexact remainder cannot be trusted for a non-redundant selection. Two tricks carry the design:

1. **Reorder so that $X\ge Y$ and $X\ge Z$.** Then the iteration initialises with $S_0\approx X$, and $X^2$ is *never computed at all*. $Y^2$ and $Z^2$ are accumulated incrementally, one partial-product line per iteration, inside the $W_{j+1}$ computation.
2. **Radix-2 with $\{-1,0,1\}$**, one result bit per iteration, selection from **6 bits of $W_{j-1}$**, and

$$W_{j+1} = 2W_j + 2^{-3}\left(y_{-j-4}Y + z_{-j-4}Z\right) - 2d_{-j-1}S_j - 2^{-j-1}d^2_{-j-1}$$

a sum of five terms, kept in carry-save to avoid the carry propagation, with $W_0 = YY_{[3]}+ZZ_{[3]}$ where $Y_{[3]}$ is the 3 leading bits of $Y$. The offset $2^{-3}$ compensating the index $-j-4$ is the technicality encoding "accurate enough". Two versions of $S_j$ are maintained for on-the-fly conversion, as above. Higher radices still add five terms but the digit-by-word multiplications and the selection function get more expensive.

**Is it worth it?** Against the naive route — sum the squares, then take a square root — the digit recurrence obviously has shorter latency and avoids $X^2$ entirely. But it computes $Y^2$ and $Z^2$ as plain products, missing the dedicated squarer optimisation of [[3.3) Squarers and Integer Powers]], and the two additions hiding in the recurrence are less area-efficient than one global compressor tree. The book states plainly that the net area benefit is unclear. The alternative for the 2D case is CORDIC in vectoring mode ([[5.1) CORDIC]]): unrolled in space it costs roughly **$2n$ additions of $n$ bits plus $2n$ multiplexers**.

> [!warning] Not covered in the source — needs outside research
> No implementation-based comparison of the norm digit recurrence against either the naive sum-of-squares-then-sqrt or against CORDIC exists in the literature the book surveys; it says so explicitly on pp. 590–591. Any area/delay claim here would be invented.

## The E-method for evaluating rational functions

### The digit recurrence

Ercegovac's E-method generalises the whole pattern to functions expressible as the solution of a **linear system** $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{b}$ with $\mathbf{A}$ non-singular. It computes $\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b}$ one digit at a time; it is literally a *vector* version of digit-recurrence division ([[2.5) Dividers]]), where $\mathbf{A}$ plays the role of the divisor.

Each component of the approximation $\mathbf{Y}=(Y_0\;Y_1\;\dots\;Y_n)^T$ is built as $Y_i = \sum_{j=-1}^{-m}\beta^jd_{i,j}$ with $d_{i,j}\in\{-\gamma,\dots,\gamma\}$ and $m$ the target precision. Note the sum starts at $j=-1$: **the solution is assumed to have no nonzero digit of weight larger than $\beta^{-1}$**, which is already a constraint — the exact analogue of requiring a normalised divisor for division to converge. Collecting the digits of weight $\beta^j$ across all components into $\mathbf{d}_j = (d_{0,j}\;d_{1,j}\;\dots\;d_{n,j})^T$, the recurrence runs $j=-1$ down to $-m$:

$$\mathbf{W}_0 = \mathbf{b},\qquad \mathbf{d}_j = \mathrm{Sel}(\mathbf{W}_{j+1}),\qquad \mathbf{W}_j = \beta\left(\mathbf{W}_{j+1}-\mathbf{A}\mathbf{d}_j\right)$$

Compare with the scalar $W_{i+1}=\beta W_i - d\,D$: same shape, with the vector residual $\mathbf{W}_j$ and the matrix-vector product $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{d}_j$ replacing the scalar. The selection function is componentwise **rounding to nearest, clamped at $\gamma$**:

$$d_{i,j} = \begin{cases}\mathrm{sign}(W_{i,j})\times\left\lfloor|W_{i,j}|+\tfrac12\right\rfloor & |W_{i,j}|\le\gamma\\ \mathrm{sign}(W_{i,j})\times\gamma & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

**Convergence.** Ercegovac proves by induction that if there exist $\zeta,\mu$ with

$$\tfrac12 \le \zeta < 1,\qquad 0<\mu<\frac1\beta\left[1-\frac{\zeta}{\gamma}(\beta-1)\right] = \frac1\beta\left(1-\frac{\zeta}{\rho}\right)$$
$$\|\mathbf I - \mathbf A\| \le \mu \quad\text{with}\quad \|\mathbf M\| = \max_i\left(\sum_{k=0}^n|m_{ik}|\right),\qquad \|\mathbf b\|\le\zeta \quad\text{with}\quad \|\mathbf b\|=\max_i|b_i|$$

then $\|\mathbf W_j - \mathbf d_j\|\le\zeta$ at every iteration and, in the limit, $\|\mathbf Y - \mathbf y\| < \beta^{-m}$. The second condition immediately forces $\zeta<\rho$, so a **redundant** digit set ($\rho$ large) buys headroom in exactly the same way it does for SRT. And when $\zeta>\tfrac12$ there is slack left over, which can be spent relaxing the selection function so it decides on a truncation $\widetilde{\mathbf W}_j$ of the residual to its most significant digits — the same truncation move as in the square root. *(pp. 591–592.)*

### Rational approximations compatible with the E-method

Take a rational approximation of some target $f$ ([[4.3) Polynomial Approximation]] gives the machinery for finding one):

$$R(x) = \frac{P_0+P_1x+P_2x^2+\dots+P_nx^n}{1+Q_1x+Q_2x^2+\dots+Q_nx^n}$$

Consider the $(n{+}1)\times(n{+}1)$ system with $1$ on the diagonal, $-x$ on the superdiagonal, and the $Q_i$ down the first column below the diagonal, right-hand side $(P_0\;P_1\;\dots\;P_n)^T$. Row $n$ gives $y_n = P_n - Q_ny_0$; back-substituting upward, $y_i = \sum_{k=i}^{n}x^{k-i}(P_k-Q_ky_0)$ for $i\ge1$. Row 0 is $y_0 = P_0 + xy_1$, so

$$
y_0 = P_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{n}x^kP_k - y_0\sum_{k=1}^{n}Q_kx^k
\;\Longrightarrow\;
y_0\left(1+\sum_{k=1}^nQ_kx^k\right) = \sum_{k=0}^nP_kx^k
$$

that is, $\boxed{y_0 = R(x)}$. The E-method therefore **merges the numerator evaluation, the denominator evaluation and the division into a single recurrence** — one pass, no separate divider.

Now read the convergence conditions back onto this matrix. $\mathbf I - \mathbf A$ has zero diagonal, $+x$ on the superdiagonal and $-Q_i$ in the first column; its row sums are $|x|$ for row 0, $|Q_i|+|x|$ for rows $1..n-1$, and $|Q_n|$ for row $n$. Taking the max:

$$\forall i\in\{1,\dots,n\}:\quad |Q_i| + |x| < \mu \qquad\text{and}\qquad \forall i:\; |P_i| < \zeta$$

> [!important] The E-method is not universal, and this is why
> $|Q_i|+|x|<\mu$ **involves $x$**, so it is not a property of the function alone — it defines a *domain of convergence* in the input. Combined with $|P_i|<\zeta$ and $\zeta<\rho\le1$, it says the rational approximation's coefficients must be small. Most rational approximations produced by a minimax fit are not. Scaling input and output by powers of two can transform the two constraints into a constraint on the input interval, but if no scaling works, the E-method simply does not apply to that $R$ — you fall back to evaluating numerator and denominator separately and dividing. Rational fractions that do satisfy the constraints (possibly after an output-side power-of-two scaling) are called **simple E-fractions**; finding them with machine-representable $P_i, Q_i$ is a search problem in its own right, solved in Brisebarre et al. (2018).
>
> For a *polynomial* ($\forall i, Q_i=0$) some power-of-two scaling always exists, so the E-method always applies — but it is not a particularly efficient way to evaluate a polynomial, and Horner ([[4.3) Polynomial Approximation]]) wins. The E-method earns its keep only where a **rational** approximation is genuinely better than a polynomial one, which is where the target has near-singularities a polynomial cannot capture. *(pp. 593–594.)*

## FPGA mapping

- **Non-restoring beats restoring.** On current FPGAs the non-restoring square-root formulation maps better onto LUTs and the fast-carry structure, giving both lower area and a shorter critical path than the restoring version. It is the current default in [[7.1) FloPoCo]] (`flopoco FPSqrt wE=8 wF=23`; `method=0` selects restoring). *(p. 580.)*
- **On-the-fly conversion is an ASIC idiom.** On FPGAs the subtraction $S_{i+1}=S_i+\beta^{-i-1}d_{-i-1}$ with $d<0$ can be done with fast carry logic at a latency comparable to computing $W_{i+1}$ itself. But doing it *per iteration* adds a second adder per iteration, which destroys radix-4's advantage over the non-restoring radix-2 (half the iterations, but each twice as expensive). The alternative is to convert only once at the end and feed the selection function a value $\widetilde{S_i}$ accurate to $t_S$ only. For even $t_S$ the truncated tail is bounded by $\sum_{j\ge t_S/2}2\beta^{-j}=\rho\beta^{-t_S/2}$, so the extra constraint is $\widetilde{S_i}-\rho\beta^{-t_S/2} < S_i < \widetilde{S_i}+\rho\beta^{-t_S/2}$, which may make the selection function more expensive. The book states this trade-off has not been fully studied. *(p. 587.)*
- **Redundant residuals are less obviously right on FPGA.** The reference E-method implementation of Brisebarre et al. (2018) keeps partial remainders in **standard two's complement**, not the redundant representation of Ercegovac's original ASIC-targeted work, precisely because FPGA fast-carry hardware makes plain binary addition cheap. Same paper: **high radices, $\beta=8$ to $\beta=32$**, give area-efficient FPGA implementations by exploiting large architectural LUTs. *(p. 594.)*

## Trade-offs

> [!warning] Not covered in the source — needs outside research
> Chapter 19 gives **no LUT, slice, delay or DSP counts** for the square-root variants, and no implementation-based comparison of digit-recurrence square root against Newton-Raphson or against polynomial approximation. The only quantified head-to-head in the chapter is the cube-root one (p. 589). The table below marks every entry the source does not quantify.

**Square root, three ways.**

| | Digit recurrence | Newton-Raphson ([[2.5) Dividers#Division by Reciprocal]]) | Polynomial approximation ([[4.3) Polynomial Approximation]]) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Latency (cycles) | $\approx n/\log_2\beta$, linear in precision | $\approx\log_2 n$ iterations, quadratic convergence | 1 pass, depth $=$ polynomial degree |
| Multiplier needed | **No** — digit-by-number products are shifts and muxes (p. 589) | Yes, one full-width multiplier per iteration | Yes, plus tables |
| Area | small, one add/sub of $i{+}4$ bits at step $i$ (p. 580) | dominated by the multiplier | tables $+$ multipliers |
| Last-bit exactness | **remainder is explicit $\Rightarrow$ correct rounding directly** (p. 589) | faithful only; correct rounding needs a squaring test | faithful only; same problem (p. 589) |
| Behaviour near 0 | fine — no derivative used | needs a seed table | poor: $\sqrt{\cdot}$ not differentiable at 0, range reduction mandatory (p. 575) |
| Quantified comparison | cube-root case: **2× slower, 2× smaller, comparable area-time** vs multiplier-based, ASIC models (p. 589) | not quantified in source | not quantified in source |

**Rational function: E-method vs. Horner-plus-divider.**

| | E-method | Horner numerator + Horner denominator + divider |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | one recurrence, $m$ iterations, merges both polynomials and the division (p. 593) | three sequential stages |
| Per-iteration hardware | $n{+}1$ selection functions and $n{+}1$ short adders; no multiplier | $2n$ multiply-accumulates plus a full divider |
| Applicability | **only if $|Q_i|+|x|<\mu$ and $|P_i|<\zeta$**, possibly after power-of-two scaling (p. 593) | any coefficients |
| Polynomial case ($Q_i=0$) | always converges after scaling, but not efficient (p. 593) | the right answer |
| FPGA | two's-complement residuals, $\beta=8\dots32$ area-efficient (p. 594) | DSP-block friendly |

Pick the **digit recurrence** when you need a correctly rounded algebraic function, have no spare multiplier, and can afford one cycle per digit — which is most fixed-function ASIC and small-FPGA square roots. Pick **Newton-Raphson** when a multiplier already exists in the datapath and latency matters more than the last bit. Pick **polynomial approximation** when the function is smooth over the reduced range and you are pipelining for throughput rather than minimising area. Reach for the **E-method** only when a rational approximation genuinely beats a polynomial one — a near-singularity in the target — and the coefficients happen to be scalable into an E-fraction.

**Checks.**

- *Residual invariant.* At $i=2$: $W_2 = \beta^2(M-S_2^2) = 4(2-1.5625)=1.75$, and indeed $T_3 = 2W_2 - (2S_2+2^{-3}) = 3.5-2.625=0.875$ ✓. The sign of $T$ wanders negative — that is the whole point of non-restoring; the invariant $0\le W_i$ is restored implicitly by the next iteration's addition.
- *On-the-fly assembly.* Every entry in the $S_i$ and $S_i^{\ominus}$ columns is a **concatenation**, never an addition. Where $d_{-i}=1$ the rule is $S_i = S_{i-1}\,\|\,1$ and $S_i^{\ominus}=S_{i-1}\,\|\,0$; where $d_{-i}=0$ it is $S_i = S_{i-1}\,\|\,0$ and $S_i^{\ominus}=S_{i-1}^{\ominus}\,\|\,1$ (that is $S^{\ominus}_{i-1}+2^{-i}(\beta-1)$ with $\beta=2$). Check row 4: $S_4^{\ominus}=$ `1.0101` $=1.3125 = 1.375-2^{-4}$ ✓.
- *Digits.* $\sqrt2 = 1.0110101000001\ldots_2$, so all 7 digits are correct.

**Error.** $\sqrt2 = 1.41421356\ldots$, $S_7 = 1.4140625$, ulp $= 2^{-7}=0.0078125$:

$$\frac{\sqrt2 - S_7}{2^{-7}} = \frac{1.5106\times10^{-4}}{7.8125\times10^{-3}} = \mathbf{0.019\ \text{ulp}}$$

and it is positive, as it must be — the raw recurrence rounds **down**, error in $[0,1)$ ulp.

**Round to nearest for free.** Target $\ell=-6$ instead and use the 7th digit as a round bit: $S_6 + 2^{-6}d_{-7} = 1.406250 + 0.015625 = 1.421875$. Since $\sqrt2/2^{-6} = 90.51$, round-to-nearest at 6 fractional bits is $91\cdot2^{-6}=1.421875$ ✓, with error $0.49$ ulp $\le\tfrac12$ ulp. One extra iteration, no extra hardware.
