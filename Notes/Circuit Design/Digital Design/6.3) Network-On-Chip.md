**Specification.** Input: a packet of $L$ flits of $w$ bits, the head flit carrying a destination address $(x_d,y_d)$ of $2\lceil\log_2 k\rceil$ bits. Output: the same $L$ flits, bit-exact, in order, at the destination's local port. Accuracy: **exact, $0$ ulp** — an interconnect computes the identity function on its payload, so the payload's format and error budget ([[1.1) Number Formats]], [[1.2) Error Accuracy]]) pass through untouched and the only error the network contributes is *cycles*, not value. What is optimized: **throughput** — the sustainable injection rate $\lambda$ in flits/cycle/node — subject to a per-tile area budget and a zero-load latency target.

Three hard constraints fall out of that specification and drive every decision below: the delivery must be **lossless** (no drop, so backpressure is mandatory), **in order** (so a packet may not be split across divergent paths), and **deadlock-free** (a livelock-free routing function is not enough — a cyclic buffer dependency stalls forever with no error signal).

## Why the bus stops scaling

A shared bus is one channel with $N$ taps. Four independent things break as $N$ grows, and only the first is the one people remember.

**Capacitance.** The bus wire spans the die, so its length is $\Theta(\sqrt{A})$ for die area $A$, and it carries $N$ receiver loads: $C_{\text{bus}} = N C_{\text{in}} + c_w \ell$. Delay through a repeated wire is linear in $\ell$, energy per transfer is $C_{\text{bus}}V_{dd}^2$ — and **every** transfer pays the whole $C_{\text{bus}}$, even a point-to-point message between neighbours. Energy per bit is $\Theta(N)$ for information that needed $\Theta(1)$.

**Arbitration.** One global arbiter resolves $N$ requests to one grant. That is a priority encoder over an $N$-bit request vector, logic depth $\lceil\log_2 N\rceil$ even with a prefix tree ([[0.2) Combinational Logic]]), and it must complete *plus* drive the grant back across the die inside one bus cycle. The arbiter is a floor on the clock period that rises with $N$.

**Bandwidth.** Aggregate throughput is $w/T_{\text{bus}}$ regardless of $N$, so per-node bandwidth is $\Theta(1/N)$. In the vocabulary of the next section, a bus has a bisection of exactly one channel.

**Timing closure.** The bus is a single combinational span in a single clock domain. Past roughly 45 nm, cross-die wire delay exceeds a useful clock period, so the bus cannot be closed at all without pipelining it — and a pipelined bus is already a degenerate network with one hop.

A NoC replaces the shared medium with **segmented, registered, point-to-point links plus per-tile switches**. Each link spans one tile pitch, so wire delay is a fixed fraction of a cycle independent of $N$; arbitration is local, over $p=5$ ports instead of $N$; and the channel count grows as $\Theta(N)$, so aggregate bandwidth grows with the chip instead of being divided by it. The price is a router per tile and a latency that is now $\Theta(\bar H)$ hops rather than one cycle.

## Topologies in closed form

Fix the counting convention, because half the disagreements in the literature are convention: $N$ nodes; a **channel** is unidirectional and $w$ bits wide; **diameter** $D$ is the maximum hop count between any pair, counting router traversals; **bisection bandwidth** $B_B$ counts the channels crossing a minimum cut into two $N/2$-node halves, *in one direction*, times $w$; **radix** $p$ is the port count of one router including its local port.

For a $k\times k$ mesh or torus, $N=k^2$ and $k=\sqrt N$.

| Topology | Diameter $D$ | $B_B/w$ (channels) | Radix $p$ | Channels | Switch area |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bus | $1$ | $1$ | — | $1$ | $\Theta(1)$ |
| Ring | $\lfloor N/2\rfloor$ | $2$ | $3$ | $2N$ | $9Nw$ |
| $k\times k$ mesh | $2(\sqrt N-1)$ | $\sqrt N$ | $5$ | $4N-4\sqrt N$ | $25Nw$ |
| $k\times k$ torus | $2\lfloor\sqrt N/2\rfloor$ | $2\sqrt N$ | $5$ | $4N$ | $25Nw$ |
| Crossbar | $1$ | $N/2$ | $N{+}1$ | $N$ | $N^2w$ |
| $m$-ary fat tree | $2\log_m N$ | $N/2$ | $2m$ | $\approx 2N\log_m N$ | $4m^2w\cdot\tfrac{N}{m}\log_m N$ |
| $m$-ary butterfly | $\log_m N$ | $N/2$ | $2m$ | $\approx 2N\log_m N$ | same as fat tree |

"Switch area" is the crossbar term only: a $p\times p$ crossbar of $w$-bit datapaths is $w$ copies of $p$ independent $p{:}1$ multiplexers, hence $p^2w$ crosspoints, and that quadratic in $p$ is why radix is the number to watch.

**Throughput follows from the bisection, not from the diameter.** Under uniform-random traffic every node injects $\lambda$ flits/cycle and, by symmetry, half of all traffic crosses any bisection. Total crossing rate is $N\lambda/2$, and the cut can carry $B_B/w$ flits/cycle, so

$$\frac{N\lambda}{2}\;\le\;\frac{B_B}{w}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{\;\lambda_{\max}=\frac{2B_B}{Nw}\;}$$

Substituting the table: mesh $\lambda_{\max}=2/\sqrt N$, torus $4/\sqrt N$, ring $4/N$, bus $2/N$, crossbar and fat tree $1$. The torus buys exactly $2\times$ the mesh throughput for the same radix — that is the entire argument for wraparound links.

Diameter matters for *latency*, and for random traffic the mean hop count matters more. On a $k$-node line the mean $|i-j|$ over uniform pairs is $(k^2-1)/3k\approx k/3$; a mesh is two independent dimensions, so $\bar H_{\text{mesh}}\approx \tfrac23\sqrt N$. On a $k$-node ring the mean is $\approx k/4$, so $\bar H_{\text{torus}}\approx\tfrac12\sqrt N$ — three quarters of the mesh, and each hop costs a router traversal.

> [!note] Why the mesh wins in 2D despite the worst bisection
> $B_B$ wires must physically cross a cut whose length is $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ tile pitches. At a fixed wire pitch the cut admits $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ tracks. The mesh's $\sqrt N$ bisection saturates that budget exactly; a crossbar or fat tree demands $N/2$, which is $\Theta(\sqrt N)$ times too many and must be paid for in extra metal layers or a $\sqrt N$-fold pitch reduction. The mesh is not the best graph — it is the best graph that *fits on a plane*.

## Routing and deadlock freedom

A routing function $R$ maps (current node, destination) — or (current channel, destination) — to a set of legal output channels. **Deterministic** means $|R|=1$ always; **adaptive** means $|R|>1$ and a selection function picks among productive outputs at runtime.

**Dimension-order (XY) routing** is the deterministic default and is a handful of gates. With $\Delta x = x_d - x_c$ and $\Delta y = y_d - y_c$:

$$
\text{port}=\begin{cases}
E & \Delta x>0\\ W & \Delta x<0\\ N & \Delta x=0,\ \Delta y>0\\ S & \Delta x=0,\ \Delta y<0\\ L & \Delta x=\Delta y=0
\end{cases}
$$

Two magnitude comparators of $\lceil\log_2 k\rceil$ bits ([[2.2) Comparators]]) feeding a 5-way priority mux — structurally the priority encoder of [[0.2) Combinational Logic]], with the ordering "$x$ before $y$" hard-wired as the priority. For $k\le 16$ that is one LUT level. It is stateless, so it costs no flops and cannot be the critical path.

Adaptive routing replaces the priority mux with a *set* of productive directions and a selection over them, usually by free-VC count or downstream credit — which means the credit counters of the next section become an input to the routing decision, and the routing stage acquires a dependence on runtime state that XY does not have.

> [!important] Deadlock freedom is exactly acyclicity of the channel dependency graph
> Build the **channel dependency graph** $\mathcal D=(C,E)$: one vertex per channel, and an edge $c_i\to c_j$ iff the routing function permits some packet to *hold* $c_i$ while *requesting* $c_j$. Then, for deterministic routing on a lossless network:
>
> $$\textbf{routing is deadlock-free}\iff \mathcal D \textbf{ is acyclic}$$
>
> **($\Leftarrow$)** Acyclicity gives a topological order $\prec$ on $C$. Suppose a deadlock exists: a non-empty set $P$ of permanently blocked packets, each holding at least one channel and waiting on a channel held by another member of $P$. Let $c^\ast$ be the $\prec$-maximal channel held by any packet in $P$, and let $q$ be the packet holding it. $q$ is blocked waiting on some channel $c'$; by construction of $\mathcal D$ there is an edge $c^\ast\to c'$, so $c^\ast\prec c'$. But $c'$ must be *held* by a blocked packet — if it were free, $q$ would advance and there would be no deadlock — so $c'$ is a channel held by a member of $P$ with $c^\ast\prec c'$, contradicting maximality. No such $P$ exists.
>
> **($\Rightarrow$)** Given a cycle $c_0\to c_1\to\dots\to c_{n-1}\to c_0$, fill each $c_i$'s buffer with a packet whose next requested channel is $c_{i+1 \bmod n}$. Every packet holds what its successor needs; nothing ever moves. The cycle is realizable, hence deadlock is reachable.
>
> Everything else in this section — dimension order, turn models, dateline virtual channels — is one construction or another for making $\mathcal D$ acyclic, and each is verified by exhibiting the topological order.

**XY routing breaks the cycles by labelling.** Label each channel $\ell(c) = (\dim(c),\ \text{coord}(c))$ ordered lexicographically with $X<Y$. A packet under dimension order finishes all its $X$ hops before its first $Y$ hop, so no dependency edge ever runs $Y\to X$ — the dimension component is non-decreasing. Within a dimension the packet never reverses, so along a row the East channels form a chain ordered by column and the West channels form the reverse chain, with no edge between the two. Every edge of $\mathcal D$ strictly increases $\ell$; therefore $\mathcal D$ is acyclic. Done — no virtual channels, no buffers, no state.

**The turn model shows XY is over-restrictive.** In a 2D mesh there are eight turns, and they organize into exactly two abstract cycles:

$$\text{cw}=\{N\!\to\!E,\ E\!\to\!S,\ S\!\to\!W,\ W\!\to\!N\},\qquad \text{ccw}=\{N\!\to\!W,\ W\!\to\!S,\ S\!\to\!E,\ E\!\to\!N\}$$

Every cycle in $\mathcal D$ must traverse four corners and therefore must use all four turns of one abstract cycle. So **prohibiting one turn from each abstract cycle suffices** — two prohibitions, not four. XY forbids $\{N\!\to\!E, N\!\to\!W, S\!\to\!E, S\!\to\!W\}$, which is four: two more than necessary, and the surplus is exactly the adaptivity it throws away.

| Turn model | Forbidden turns | cw broken by | ccw broken by | Adaptivity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| XY (dimension order) | $N\!\to\!E,N\!\to\!W,S\!\to\!E,S\!\to\!W$ | $N\!\to\!E$ | $N\!\to\!W$ | none |
| West-first | $N\!\to\!W,\ S\!\to\!W$ | $S\!\to\!W$ | $N\!\to\!W$ | full for eastbound |
| North-last | $N\!\to\!E,\ N\!\to\!W$ | $N\!\to\!E$ | $N\!\to\!W$ | full for southbound |
| Negative-first | $E\!\to\!S,\ N\!\to\!W$ | $E\!\to\!S$ | $N\!\to\!W$ | full for NE / SW quadrants |

**Tori need virtual channels regardless of turn model,** because the wraparound closes a cycle *inside a single dimension* — the ring $c_0\to c_1\to\dots\to c_{k-1}\to c_0$ needs no turn at all. The fix is the **dateline**: split each physical channel into two virtual channels $\text{VC}_0,\text{VC}_1$ with private buffers, and require a packet to use $\text{VC}_0$ until it crosses a fixed dateline link, $\text{VC}_1$ after. Label $\ell(c)=(\text{vc index},\ \text{position})$; no packet ever moves from $\text{VC}_1$ back to $\text{VC}_0$, so the ring cycle is cut and $\mathcal D$ is acyclic again. The cost is $2\times$ the buffers and a $2{:}1$ mux on every physical link — the VC index is *not* a wire, it is a buffer-selection field in the flit header.

## Flow control

Flow control decides the granularity at which buffers and channels are allocated. Three schemes, and the difference is which resource is allocated per *packet* and which per *flit*.

- **Store-and-forward** allocates both buffers and channel per packet, and forwards nothing until the tail arrives. Zero-load latency $T_0 = H(t_r + L)$ — the packet is serialized at every hop. Requires $B\ge L$ flits per input.
- **Virtual cut-through** allocates both per packet, but forwards the head as soon as the downstream has room for the *whole* packet. $T_0 = H\,t_r + (L-1)$: serialization is paid once. Still requires $B\ge L$, because a blocked packet must collapse into one node.
- **Wormhole** allocates the *channel* per packet but *buffers* per flit. $T_0 = H\,t_r + (L-1)$, same as cut-through, but $B$ is decoupled from $L$ entirely. When the head blocks, the worm stalls in place, spread across several routers, holding every channel it occupies — that is the head-of-line cost, and it is why wormhole without virtual channels performs badly under load.

The floor on $B$ under wormhole is not $L$; it is the **credit round-trip**. Let $t_{crt}$ be the number of cycles from sending a flit to the corresponding credit being usable upstream:

$$t_{crt}=t_{\text{link}}^{\rightarrow}+t_{\text{router}}+t_{\text{credit}}+t_{\text{link}}^{\leftarrow}$$

The sender may have at most $B$ flits outstanding, so the sustained link utilization is

$$\eta=\min\!\left(1,\ \frac{B}{t_{crt}}\right)$$

A 3-stage router with one-cycle links each way and one cycle of credit logic gives $t_{crt}=5$, so $B<5$ throttles the link *even with no contention anywhere*. This is the single most common NoC sizing mistake: shrinking buffers to save area and silently capping every link at $B/5$ of its bandwidth.

**Credit-based backpressure** itself is two counters ([[2.8) Counters]]). Downstream, per input VC, a free-slot count; upstream, a *mirror* of it: an up/down counter of $\lceil\log_2(B{+}1)\rceil$ bits, decremented on flit send, incremented on credit return, with the send gated on $\text{count}>0$. Simultaneous send-and-credit is a $\pm 0$ update, so the counter needs a $2$-bit increment select, not two ports. The alternatives are worse: **on/off** signalling replaces the counter with one wire plus a threshold, but the off threshold must leave $t_{crt}$ slots of headroom or flits are dropped, so it wastes buffer to save $\lceil\log_2(B{+}1)\rceil$ wires; **ack/nack** needs no reverse state at all but retransmits, wasting energy proportional to the drop rate.

| Scheme | Buffer per input (flits) | $T_0$ (no load) | Head-of-line blocking |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Store-and-forward | $\ge L$ | $H(t_r+L)$ | packet-granular, one router |
| Virtual cut-through | $\ge L$ | $H t_r+(L-1)$ | packet-granular, one router |
| Wormhole | $\ge t_{crt}$, indep. of $L$ | $H t_r+(L-1)$ | worm-granular, spans $\lceil L/B\rceil$ routers |

A single FIFO per input port saturates at $2-\sqrt2\approx 0.586$ of capacity under uniform random destinations (Karol's input-queued switch bound) — the blocked head hides a ready flit behind it. $V$ virtual channels give the arbiter $V$ independent heads to choose from and push throughput back toward $1$ as $V$ grows; the gain is steep to $V=4$ and flat after.

The canonical pipeline is four stages, one flit per stage per cycle:

1. **RC** — route computation, head flit only. Two comparators plus a priority mux; sub-LUT-level, never critical.
2. **VA** — virtual channel allocation. Match up to $pV$ requesting input VCs to $pV$ output VCs, at most one each way. This is a bipartite matching, and it is done *once per packet*.
3. **SA** — switch allocation. Every cycle, every flit with an output VC and a credit competes for a crossbar port. The standard **separable input-first** allocator is $p$ input arbiters of $V{:}1$ (choose one VC per input) followed by $p$ output arbiters of $p{:}1$ (choose one input per output). Delay $\Theta(\log V + \log p)$ with prefix trees; a full $pV\times p$ wavefront allocator matches better but is slower.
4. **ST/LT** — switch traversal and link traversal.

**The arbiter is a priority-encoder problem.** A fixed-priority arbiter over an $n$-bit request vector $r$ grants the lowest set bit, which is the two's-complement identity

$$g = r \wedge (-r) = r \wedge (\overline r + 1)$$

— an increment, hence a carry chain, hence exactly the prefix reassociation of [[2.1.5) Parallel Prefix Adders]]: write $h_i=\bigvee_{j<i} r_j$ as a prefix-OR and $g_i = r_i\wedge\overline{h_i}$, and the depth drops from $n$ to $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$ ([[0.2) Combinational Logic]]). A **round-robin** arbiter is two of those plus a pointer register: mask $m$ = bits at or above the pointer, take $\mathrm{PE}(r\wedge m)$ if non-zero else $\mathrm{PE}(r)$, then set the pointer one past the grant. Two prefix trees, one $n$-bit mux, one $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$-bit flop — and it is on the critical path of every cycle, so this is where the router's clock period is actually decided.

Where the area goes, for a $p=5$, $V=4$, $B=6$, $w=128$ router:

| Component | Expression | This router |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Input buffers | $pVBw$ bits | $15{,}360$ bits |
| Crossbar | $p^2w$ crosspoints | $3{,}200$ |
| SA arbiters | $p$ of $V{:}1$ $+$ $p$ of $p{:}1$ | $5\times4{:}1 + 5\times5{:}1$ |
| VA | $pV$ of $pV{:}1$ | $20\times20{:}1$ |
| Credit counters | $pV\lceil\log_2(B{+}1)\rceil$ flops | $60$ flops |

Buffers dominate area and leakage; the crossbar dominates the *scaling*, since $p^2w$ is quadratic in radix — doubling radix to shorten the diameter quadruples the switch. The allocators are small in area and large in delay.

Two standard pipeline collapses: **lookahead routing** computes hop $i{+}1$'s route during hop $i$, removing RC from the critical path entirely; **speculative switch allocation** runs SA in parallel with VA and discards the grant when VA fails, which costs a small misspeculation rate and buys a stage. Together they take the canonical 4 stages to 2, and with a bypass path for empty buffers to 1 at low load.

## Clocking: synchronous, mesochronous, GALS

The interconnect spans the whole die, which is precisely the region over which a single clock is hardest to distribute. Three regimes:

**Synchronous.** One clock, one grid, skew budgeted globally. The setup and hold inequalities of [[0.3) Introduction to Flip-Flops & Latches]] apply link by link, and since each link is one tile pitch the data path is easy — it is the *clock* distribution that costs, growing as $\Theta(N)$ in wire and buffer power. Fine to a few dozen tiles.

**Mesochronous.** One frequency, arbitrary but *constant* phase per tile. Because the phase is constant there is no metastability risk in principle: each link crossing needs only a small phase-tolerant FIFO (2–4 flits) with a strobe positioned in the receiver's safe window, plus one-time training at reset. Cost is a few flits of buffer per link and a lock sequence; benefit is that the clock tree becomes a set of unbalanced local trees with no global skew constraint.

**GALS.** Every tile has its own oscillator; frequencies are unrelated. Two implementations:

*Asynchronous FIFO.* The link buffer becomes a dual-clock FIFO. The write pointer lives in the sender's domain, the read pointer in the receiver's, and each must be sampled by the other to compute full/empty. A **binary** pointer sampled mid-transition can be caught with several bits in flight — $0111\to1000$ changes four bits, and an unlucky sample yields any of sixteen values, most of them neither the old nor the new one. A **Gray-coded** pointer ([[2.8) Counters]]) changes exactly one bit per increment, so a mid-transition sample can only return the old value or the new one — both are conservatively safe (a stale write pointer under-reports fullness at the read side, a stale read pointer over-reports fullness at the write side, and both errors are in the safe direction). That single-bit-change property is the entire justification, and it is why the pointers are Gray and the *data* is not.

The one bit that does cross is still metastable-prone, hence the two-flop synchronizer, with

$$\text{MTBF}=\frac{e^{t_r/\tau}}{T_0\,f_c\,f_d}$$

for resolution time $t_r$, sender rate $f_d$ and sampling clock $f_c$. Each crossing costs 2–3 cycles of the destination clock — which lands directly in $t_{crt}$ and therefore inflates the required buffer depth $B$ from the flow-control section.

*Fully asynchronous links.* Drop the receiver clock on the link entirely and use bundled-data four-phase or two-phase handshaking — exactly the request/acknowledge sequencing of [[0.5) Asynchronous Sequential Logic]]. The join element is the Muller C-element,

$$y^{+} = ab + y(a+b)$$

a majority-of-three with its own output fed back as the third input ([[0.1) Boolean Algebra]]): it switches only when both inputs agree and holds otherwise, which is precisely "wait for both". Bundled data requires the request wire's delay to exceed the data path's worst-case delay — a timing assumption, and the reason bundled-data links need margin; dual-rail encoding removes the assumption at $2\times$ the wires and roughly $2\times$ the switching energy. Latency is data-dependent rather than quantized, so a GALS link has no fixed cycle cost, but it also cannot be timed by a standard STA flow.

## Quality of service and virtual channels

Virtual channels do three unrelated jobs and it is worth keeping them apart, because each implies a different value of $V$:

1. **Deadlock avoidance** — the dateline construction. Needs $V=2$ per dimension on a torus, $V=1$ on a mesh with dimension order.
2. **Head-of-line mitigation** — gives the switch arbiter more independent heads. Wants $V=4$-ish; the marginal gain past that is small while $VB w$ buffer area keeps growing linearly.
3. **Traffic-class separation** — the QoS role. Needs one VC per class that must not block another.

Role 3 is the one that turns VCs into a service guarantee. Two mechanisms:

**Priority / best-effort.** Assign each class a VC and make the switch arbiter fixed-priority across classes, round-robin within. A high-priority flit then never queues behind a low-priority one, but low-priority traffic can starve. The standard fix is **aging**: a saturating counter per waiting flit, $\text{prio} = \min(P_{\max}, \lfloor t_{\text{wait}}/\tau\rfloor)$, added to the class priority ([[2.8) Counters]]) — bounded starvation at the cost of $\lceil\log_2 P_{\max}\rceil$ bits per VC and a wider arbiter comparison.

**TDM / guaranteed throughput.** Give every router a slot table of $S$ entries mapping (slot, input) to output, computed offline so no two connections ever contend. A connection holding $n$ slots receives

$$\text{bandwidth} = \frac{n}{S}\,w \ \text{bits/cycle},\qquad \text{latency} \le (S-1) + H$$

— $S-1$ cycles of worst-case wait for the reserved slot at injection, then contention-free travel. This is a *hard* bound, not a statistical one, which is what makes it usable for real-time traffic; the price is that unused reserved slots are wasted and the table is $S\lceil\log_2 p\rceil$ bits of configuration per router. The usual compromise is a hybrid: reserved slots for the guaranteed class, best-effort VCs scavenging every idle slot.

**Weighted sharing** within the best-effort class is deficit round robin: per VC, add a quantum $Q_i$ each round and transmit while the deficit is at least one flit. Throughput share converges to $Q_i/\sum_j Q_j$ with $O(1)$ work per flit — one adder and one comparator per VC, which is cheaper than any real fair-queueing scheme and accurate to within a flit.

## FPGA mapping

The router maps unevenly onto FPGA primitives, and knowing which parts are cheap changes the design point.

**Crossbar.** A $p{:}1$ mux of $w$ bits is $w$ instances of a $p{:}1$ mux. A 6-input LUT natively implements a $4{:}1$ mux (4 data + 2 select), so a $5{:}1$ mux costs 2 LUTs plus a dedicated wide-mux resource ([[1.3.1) Lookup-Tables]]). For $p=5$, $w=64$: roughly $5\times64\times2\approx 640$ LUTs of crossbar alone. Rounding radix down to 4 (a mesh with the local port muxed onto an existing direction) drops that to one LUT per bit per output — a $2\times$ saving that comes entirely from LUT arity, not from graph theory.

**Buffers.** A per-VC FIFO of $B=6\times w=64$ bits is 384 bits. A BRAM is 18 Kb, so instantiating one per VC wastes 98% of it; the right primitive is distributed RAM or the shift-register LUT mode, which gives a depth-32 FIFO per LUT ([[1.3.1) Lookup-Tables]]). Move to BRAM only when $VB$ is large enough to fill one — the crossover is around $V\ge8$ with deep queues, and at that point sharing one BRAM across all VCs of a port with a pointer table beats per-VC storage ([[1.3.3) Block Memory]]).

**Links.** FPGA routing delay dominates logic delay, so the mesh's nearest-neighbour links map well and the torus's wraparound links do not — either fold the torus (every link becomes two tile pitches, uniformly) or pipeline the long links, remembering that each added register stage extends $t_{crt}$ and therefore forces deeper buffers.

**When it wins.** For a handful of masters, an FPGA's crossbar-style bus fabric (AXI interconnect) is smaller and lower latency than a NoC, because the FPGA already provides the global routing the NoC exists to avoid. The NoC's advantage begins where the flat crossbar's $p^2w$ term stops fitting — in practice around 16 masters, or earlier if the design must span multiple clock domains anyway, since GALS links are then free.

## Worked example: a 4-flit packet under XY and wormhole

Take the $4\times4$ mesh in the first figure. Source $(1,0)$, destination $(3,2)$, packet $=\{\text{H},\text{B}_1,\text{B}_2,\text{T}\}$ with $L=4$. Addresses are 2 bits per coordinate.

**Route computation** at $(1,0)$: $\Delta x = 3-1 = 2 > 0 \Rightarrow E$. At $(2,0)$: $\Delta x = 1 > 0\Rightarrow E$. At $(3,0)$: $\Delta x = 0$, $\Delta y = 2 > 0\Rightarrow N$. At $(3,1)$: $\Delta y = 1 > 0\Rightarrow N$. At $(3,2)$: $\Delta x=\Delta y=0\Rightarrow L$. Path $A\!=\!(1,0)\to B\!=\!(2,0)\to C\!=\!(3,0)\to D\!=\!(3,1)\to E\!=\!(3,2)$, so $H=5$ router traversals and 4 link traversals. Every hop is monotone in the label $\ell = (\dim,\text{coord})$: $(X,1)\prec(X,2)\prec(X,3)\prec(Y,0)\prec(Y,1)$, confirming acyclicity concretely for this route.

Assume a 1-cycle router ($t_r=1$, lookahead routing plus speculative SA), $B=2$ flits per VC, credit return in 1 cycle so $t_{crt}=2$ and $\eta=\min(1,2/2)=1$: the link runs at full rate with no contention.

**No contention.** $T_0 = H t_r + (L-1) = 5 + 3 = 8$ cycles from head injection to tail ejection.

| Cycle | $A(1,0)$ | $B(2,0)$ | $C(3,0)$ | $D(3,1)$ | $E(3,2)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | H | | | | |
| 2 | B$_1$ | H | | | |
| 3 | B$_2$ | B$_1$ | H | | |
| 4 | T | B$_2$ | B$_1$ | H | |
| 5 | | T | B$_2$ | B$_1$ | H $\to$ eject |
| 6 | | | T | B$_2$ | B$_1$ $\to$ eject |
| 7 | | | | T | B$_2$ $\to$ eject |
| 8 | | | | | T $\to$ eject |

The worm occupies four routers simultaneously at cycle 4 and holds three channels — $A\!\to\!B$, $B\!\to\!C$, $C\!\to\!D$ — for the duration. Under store-and-forward the same transfer would take $H(t_r+L)=5\times5=25$ cycles.

**With backpressure.** Now let a different packet hold $D$'s North output VC during cycles 5–7. At the end of cycle 4 the head sits in $D$ and cannot win switch allocation. Trace the credit counter $\kappa_{CD}$ that $C$ keeps for $D$'s input VC, starting at $\kappa=B=2$:

| Cycle | $A$ | $B$ | $C$ | $D$ | $\kappa_{CD}$ | Event |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4 | T | B$_2$ | B$_1$ | H | $1$ | H consumed a credit entering $D$ |
| 5 | T | B$_2$ | B$_1$ | H | $0$ | B$_1$ enters $D$; $D$'s N output blocked |
| 6 | T | B$_2$ | B$_1$ | H,B$_1$ | $0$ | $\kappa=0\Rightarrow C$ stalls, holds B$_1$ |
| 7 | T | B$_2$ | B$_1$ | H,B$_1$ | $0$ | stall propagates: $B$ then $A$ stall |
| 8 | T | B$_2$ | B$_1$ | B$_1$,B$_2$ | $1$ | N freed, H advances, credit returns |
| 9 | | T | B$_2$ | B$_2$,T | $1$ | worm resumes at 1 flit/cycle |

Three observations, and each is the point of a section above. First, the stall propagates **one router per cycle upstream** — that propagation delay is $t_{crt}$, and it is why $B\ge t_{crt}$ is the buffer floor: with $B=1$ the link would have gone idle a cycle before the block even mattered. Second, while stalled, the worm holds the channels $A\!\to\!B$, $B\!\to\!C$, $C\!\to\!D$ **idle but unavailable** to any other packet — head-of-line blocking made physical, and the direct motivation for role 2 of virtual channels. Third, the tail never left $A$, so $A$'s East output VC is held for the full 9 cycles; a second packet at $A$ destined east gets nothing, unless it has its own VC.

Total latency $=11$ cycles against $T_0=8$: the 3-cycle block is passed through to the packet exactly once, not amplified, because wormhole never re-serializes. Error in ulp: **$0$** — the payload is delivered bit-identical and in order. The cost of the block is measured in cycles, which is the only currency an interconnect trades in.
