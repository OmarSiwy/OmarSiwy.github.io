**Specification.** Inputs: two $w$-bit vectors $X,Y$ plus a carry-in $c_\text{in}$ of weight $1$. Output: the $(w+1)$-bit vector $S=X+Y+c_\text{in}$. Accuracy: **exact, 0 ulp** — a full-width adder never rounds, so the only error an adder can introduce is the one you introduce yourself by truncating its output (see [[Rounding]]). What is optimized: **latency**, because every one of the $w$ output bits can in principle depend on every one of the $2w$ input bits through the carry, and that dependency chain is the entire design problem. Area and energy are the currencies you spend to shorten it.

Addition is the operation that shows up inside every other operator in this vault — [[Multipliers]] end in one, [[Dividers]] iterate one, [[Compressor Trees]] exist only to feed one — so its latency is a floor on everything else.

## Carry propagation as a prefix problem

Start from the full adder. Its carry-out $c_{i+1}=x_ic_i\vee y_ic_i\vee x_iy_i$ (Eq. 5.3, p. 107) depends on $c_i$, but only *conditionally*. Condense the truth table on $(x_i,y_i)$ (Table 5.1, p. 112):

| $x_i\,y_i$ | $c_{i+1}$ | case |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $0\,0$ | $0$ | **kill** — carry dies here regardless of $c_i$ |
| $0\,1$ / $1\,0$ | $c_i$ | **propagate** — carry passes through untouched |
| $1\,1$ | $1$ | **generate** — carry born here regardless of $c_i$ |

Encode the three cases with signals that depend on the *inputs only*, so they are all available after one gate delay, in parallel, for every bit:

$$k_i=\overline{x_i\vee y_i},\qquad p_i=x_i\oplus y_i,\qquad g_i=x_iy_i,\qquad a_i=\bar k_i=x_i\vee y_i$$

with $a_i$ the **alive** signal, merging propagate and generate. The carry recurrence and the sum then split cleanly:

$$c_{i+1}=g_i\vee a_ic_i \tag{5.13}$$
$$s_i=p_i\oplus c_i \tag{5.11}$$

Eq. (5.11) is worth pausing on: **once you have all the carries, all the sums cost one XOR each, in parallel.** So "fast adder" means exactly "fast computation of all $c_i$", and nothing else.

One more rewriting is worth carrying forward. Generate is only reachable when we are *not* propagating, so $g_i=\overline{p_i}x_i=\overline{p_i}y_i$ (Eq. 5.14, p. 113) — **by case exhaustion on Table 5.1**, since $p_i=0$ forces $x_i=y_i$. Substituting into (5.10) turns the carry into a **multiplexer** steered by $p_i$:

$$c_{i+1}=\overline{p_i}\,x_i\vee p_ic_i \tag{5.15}$$

"if the bits differ, pass the carry through; otherwise pass either bit." No OR-of-ANDs, just a select. This is the form the switched ripple-carry realizes with transistors, and the form the [[2.1) Adders#AMD carry chain|FPGA hard carry chain]] implements in silicon.

### The prefix operator and its associativity

Pair the two per-bit signals into $X_i=(g_i,a_i)$ and define $\circ$ by (Eqs. 5.35–5.37, p. 120)

$$(g_L,a_L)\circ(g_R,a_R)\;=\;\big(\,g_L\vee a_Lg_R,\;\;a_La_R\,\big)$$

Read it as *"a carry leaves the combined block if the left (more significant) half generates one, or if the left half is alive and the right half hands one over"*. Now the load-bearing fact:

> [!important] $\circ$ is **associative**, and that is the whole reason prefix trees exist
> Expand $(U\circ V)\circ W$ on the generate component:
> $$g_{(U\circ V)\circ W}=g_{U\circ V}\vee a_{U\circ V}\,g_W=\big(g_U\vee a_Ug_V\big)\vee a_Ua_Vg_W$$
> using the definition twice. Now the other bracketing:
> $$g_{U\circ(V\circ W)}=g_U\vee a_U\,g_{V\circ W}=g_U\vee a_U\big(g_V\vee a_Vg_W\big)$$
> and **by distributivity of AND over OR**, $a_U(g_V\vee a_Vg_W)=a_Ug_V\vee a_Ua_Vg_W$. The two expressions are literally the same term, so the generate halves agree. The alive halves are
> $$a_{(U\circ V)\circ W}=a_Ua_Va_W=a_{U\circ(V\circ W)}$$
> **by associativity of AND**. Hence $(U\circ V)\circ W=U\circ(V\circ W)$. $\blacksquare$ *(Eqs. 5.38–5.43, p. 120.)*
>
> Associativity means the reduction $X_{i-1}\circ X_{i-2}\circ\cdots\circ X_{-1}$ has **no canonical bracketing**. Every valid parenthesization computes the same carry, and each one is a different circuit. The RCA is the fully **right**-nested bracketing — innermost pair at the LSB end, one operator per level; a prefix tree is a balanced one. Nothing else changes, and nothing else *has* to be proved.

Unrolling the recurrence with $X_{-1}=(g_{-1},a_{-1})=(c_0,c_0)$ gives, for the first step,

$$(g_0,a_0)\circ(c_0,c_0)=(g_0\vee a_0c_0,\;a_0c_0)=(c_1,\;a_0c_0)$$

and by induction the first component of the $i$-fold product is exactly the carry (Eq. 5.48, p. 121):

$$\big(c_i,\;a_{i-1}a_{i-2}\cdots a_0c_0\big)=\bigcirc_{j=-1}^{\,i-1}(g_j,a_j)$$

So computing **all** carries $c_1\ldots c_w$ is computing **all prefixes** of one associative reduction — a *parallel prefix* problem, the same shape as the running-sum example the book opens the section with (Fig. 5.15, p. 119). The design space is the space of prefix graphs.

> [!note] Simplified prefix nodes
> Where a downstream stage never needs the alive component (typically the bottom row, and any node whose only consumer is a carry output), the $a$ half is dropped: the node takes the full pair $(g_L,a_L)$ on the left but only $g_R$ on the right, and emits the single bit $g_{out}=g_L\vee a_Lg_R$ — two gates instead of four, one output wire instead of two. The book draws these as **filled** circles and the full 2-bit ones as **hollow** circles (Fig. 5.16, p. 121). This is not a footnote — it is why the compound adder below is nearly free.

## Fixed-point considerations

Two $w$-bit unsigned integers live in $[0,2^w-1]$, so their sum lives in $[0,2^{w+1}-2]$: it fits in $w+1$ bits **with one slot to spare**, which is exactly where the unit carry-in goes (p. 105). Signed $w$-bit operands live in $[-2^{w-1},2^{w-1}-1]$, so the sum lives in $[-2^w,2^w-2]$ — again $w+1$ signed bits with room for a carry-in. The same hardware serves both; see [[Number Formats]].

Three interfaces follow from what you do with $c_\text{out}$ (p. 105):

- **Generic adder** — keep $c_\text{out}=s_w$. Exact, output one bit wider.
- **Modulo $2^w$ adder** — drop $c_\text{in}$, discard $c_\text{out}$. Computes $X+Y \bmod 2^w$. Two inputs, one output, all $w$ bits.
- **Saturating adder** — same interface as modulo, but a multiplexer controlled by $c_\text{out}$ substitutes the maximal representable value on overflow. Common in signal processing, where wraparound is catastrophic and clipping is merely lossy.

A fixed-point number in $\text{ufix}(m,\ell)$ is an integer of $w=m-\ell+1$ bits scaled by $2^\ell$ (see [[Fixed-Point Representations]]). For two operands **in the same format**, scaling factors out (Eq. 5.1, p. 106): $S=2^\ell X_\text{int}+2^\ell Y_\text{int}=2^\ell(X_\text{int}+Y_\text{int})$, so the exact sum is in $\text{ufix}(m+1,\ell)$ and the hardware is *bit-for-bit an integer adder*. There is no such thing as fixed-point addition hardware.

For **different** formats, first align by embedding both into the smallest common format — zero-extend at the bottom, sign-extend at the top for signed operands. The exact result lives in $m=\max(m_X,m_Y)+1$, $\ell=\min(\ell_X,\ell_Y)$ *(Fig. 5.2, p. 106)*. Alignment is pure wiring: no gates, only a wider adder. If the result is then forced into a narrower $\text{ufix}(m_S,\ell_S)$, **overflow can happen iff $m_S<m$** and **rounding must happen iff $\ell_S>\ell$** — the two failure modes are independent and both are format decisions, not adder decisions ([[Error Accuracy]]).

## Addition basics

**Full adder.** $(c_{i+1},s_i)=\text{FA}(x_i,y_i,c_i)$ with $s_i=x_i\oplus y_i\oplus c_i$ and $c_{i+1}$ the **majority** of the three inputs (Eqs. 5.2–5.4, p. 107). The three inputs are *functionally* interchangeable but never *implementationally* so: the $c_i\to c_{i+1}$ path is the one worth transistor-level effort, and foundries ship several hand-tuned FA cells per technology for exactly this reason.

**Half adder.** With one input known zero the FA degenerates to $s_i=x_i\oplus y_i$, $c_{i+1}=x_iy_i$ (Eqs. 5.5–5.6, p. 107). Cheap, and used to shave the LSB cell of any adder with $c_0=0$.

**Ripple-carry (RCA).** $w$ FAs chained (Fig. 5.5, p. 108) — the most area-efficient and the slowest:

$$\tau_\text{add}(w)=(w-1)\,\tau_\text{cp}+\max(\tau_\text{cp},\tau_\text{fa}) \tag{5.7}$$

where $\tau_\text{cp}$ is the $c_i\to c_{i+1}$ delay and $\tau_\text{fa}$ the input-to-output delay of a cell. Linear in $w$. The RCA is the archetype of a **carry-propagate adder (CPA)**; every architecture below is also a CPA and (5.7) is what they are trying to beat.

**Two's complement.** No new hardware. Two's complement is arithmetic modulo $2^w$ with a different *reading* of the MSB, so the RCA of Fig. 5.5 adds signed numbers unmodified. Only the overflow test changes:

- unsigned: overflow $\iff c_\text{out}=1$;
- signed: overflow $\iff$ both operands share a sign bit and the result's differs (p. 109).

> [!warning] The book's overflow formula on p. 109 is wrong
> The text (and Fig. 5.6) computes the signed overflow flag as $s_{w-1}\oplus c_\text{out}$. Counterexample at $w=8$: $X=100=01100100_2$, $Y=-50=11001110_2$. The sum is $50=00110010_2$ with $c_\text{out}=1$, so $s_7\oplus c_\text{out}=0\oplus1=1$ — flagged, although $+50$ is perfectly representable. The correct classic form is the XOR of the **top two carries**,
> $$\text{ovf}=c_w\oplus c_{w-1}$$
> which gives $1\oplus1=0$ here. It agrees with the book's own prose definition (equal input signs, differing output sign) in all cases; $s_{w-1}\oplus c_\text{out}$ does not.

**Subtraction.** By the two's-complement identity $-Y=\overline{Y}+1$, we get $X-Y=X+\overline{Y}+1$. The bitwise negation is $w$ inverters and the $+1$ is **free** — it rides in on $c_\text{in}$ (Fig. 5.7, p. 109).

**Reconfigurable adder/subtractor.** Make the inversion conditional: XOR each $y_i$ with a `sub` signal and drive $c_\text{in}$ with the same `sub`, giving $S=X+(-1)^{\texttt{sub}}Y$ (Eq. 5.8, Fig. 5.8, p. 110). Cost over a plain adder: $w$ XOR gates and nothing else. **Take-away of §5.2.3–5.2.5 (p. 110): signed or unsigned, add or subtract, everything reduces to one RCA — so every acceleration below applies to all four.**

**Carry-save (CSA).** Do the opposite of rippling: let each FA *keep* its carry instead of passing it on. The now-unused carry-in slots form a third input $Z$, and the cell row computes $C+S=X+Y+Z$ (Eq. 5.9, p. 111) as two $w$-bit vectors in **carry-save form**. One of the two additions has genuinely been done, with minimal hardware and — the point — **delay independent of $w$**, since no carry crosses a cell boundary. The price is a redundant representation with digit set $\{0,1,2\}$ (see [[Redundant Number Systems]]): twice the bits, and comparison is so awkward that the standard way to compare two carry-save numbers is to first collapse them with a real addition. Carry-save shines in *iterative* loops where the collapse happens once at the end — the core trick behind [[Multipliers]] and [[Dividers]]. Details in [[CSA, Carry-Save Adder]].

**High-radix carry-save (HRCS).** Propagate carries within $\alpha$-bit groups and expose one carry per group (Fig. 5.10, p. 112): a radix-$2^\alpha$ redundant form with digit set $\{0,\ldots,2^\alpha\}$, also called *partial redundant form*. $\alpha=4$ gives hexadecimal carry-save. Fewer carry bits to register, longer critical path — a clean area/speed knob.

## Fast adders for VLSI

All of these compute the carries faster than the RCA and then spend one XOR per bit on the sums, per (5.11).

### Switched ripple-carry

Same topology as the RCA, but each cell is built from *switches* rather than gates: a GKP block computes $g_i,k_i,p_i$, and these three mutually exclusive signals steer fast switches that either pass $c_i$ through ($p_i=1$), pull $c_{i+1}$ to GND ($k_i=1$) or push it to $V_{cc}$ ($g_i=1$) (Fig. 5.11, p. 114). Zuse's relay-based Z3 did exactly this — the relays established an electrical carry path with no further mechanical movement, making a full addition possible in one cycle. In modern CMOS the switch is a transmission gate, but transmission gates **do not regenerate the signal**, so the approach does not scale to long chains and (5.7) stops being a valid delay model (p. 114). Worth knowing because it is precisely what an FPGA carry chain is.

### Carry-select

Split into groups of $m$ bits. A group's carry-in is unknown, so compute **both** answers with two CPAs in parallel and select once the carry arrives (Fig. 5.12, p. 115). Delay = one $m$-bit CPA plus $\lceil w/m\rceil-1$ multiplexer delays: still linear in $w$, but the per-group constant is a mux instead of $m$ carry stages. Roughly $2\times$ the adder area. A free refinement: let group sizes *grow* right-to-left, since leftmost groups have more slack before their carry arrives.

### Recursive carry-select (conditional-sum)

Apply carry-select to itself. With $w=2^q$, build a $w$-bit adder from three $w/2$-bit adders (one for the low half, two speculative for the high half) plus a $w/2$-bit mux:

$$\tau_\text{add}(w)=\tau_\text{add}(w/2)+\tau_\text{mux} \tag{5.16}$$
$$\Longrightarrow\quad \tau_\text{add}(w)=\tau_\text{add}(1)+q\,\tau_\text{mux},\qquad q=\log_2 w \tag{5.17}$$

**The first $O(\log w)$ adder.** The "$\times 3$ per level" does *not* compound into exponential area: at the leaves there are still only two possible carry-ins, so leaf CPAs are shared across all upper levels, and area stays **linear in $w$** (p. 116). Replacing the linear mux chain of carry-select with this mux tree gives the **conditional-sum adder** (Fig. 5.13, p. 116) — same CPA blocks, different mux routing.

The catch is fan-out: $c_8$ in Fig. 5.13 drives a $w/2$-bit multiplexer, i.e. $w/2$ gate inputs. The combined capacitance slows that mux down, and synthesis will silently buy the delay back in buffers and area (p. 116). This is the exact problem the prefix family lets you control.

### Carry-lookahead

Unroll $c_{i+1}=g_i\vee p_ic_i$ instead of recursing on the datapath (Eqs. 5.19–5.23, p. 117):

$$c_k=g_{k-1}\vee p_{k-1}g_{k-2}\vee p_{k-1}p_{k-2}g_{k-3}\vee\cdots\vee p_{k-1}p_{k-2}\cdots p_0c_0$$

Two levels of logic — a rank of AND gates into one wide OR — and therefore *constant* depth. On paper. In practice gate fan-in grows linearly with $k$, and a 32-input AND is not a gate, it is a tree, so the depth comes back and the area explodes. The fix is to cap $k$: chain CLA-$m$ blocks with $m=4$ (Fig. 5.14, p. 117). Delay is again linear in $w$, with a much smaller constant.

### Recursive carry-lookahead

Have each CLA block also emit the **group** generate and propagate over its span $[i:j]$, at no extra cost, so that (Eq. 5.24, p. 118)

$$c_{i+1}=g_{i:j}\vee p_{i:j}\,c_j$$

which for a 4-bit group reads $c_4=g_{3:0}\vee p_{3:0}c_0$ with $p_{3:0}=p_3p_2p_1p_0$ and $g_{3:0}=g_3\vee p_3g_2\vee p_3p_2g_1\vee p_3p_2p_1g_0$ (Eqs. 5.25–5.27). The book's own description is the tell: *"this formulation is simply a reparenthesizing of the carry-lookahead equations"* (p. 118). Group $g/p$ pairs composed by a tree gives $\log_m(w)$ levels — shallower than the conditional-sum's $\log_2(w)$, but each node is fatter. This *is* the prefix formulation, one paragraph before the book names it.

### Parallel prefix adders

Take the derivation above at face value: the carries are the prefixes of an associative reduction, so **pick any prefix graph**. A prefix graph on $w$ inputs is any DAG of $\circ$-nodes in which the output at position $i$ is a correctly-parenthesized product of $X_{i-1}\circ\cdots\circ X_{-1}$. Since $\circ$ is associative, *correctness is free* — every such graph is right. What differs is only **depth** (latency), **node count** (area, static power), **max fan-out** — an output driving $f$ inputs sees $f\times$ the load capacitance, so either slow edges or a big driver and more dynamic power (p. 121) — and **wire tracks**, which set routing congestion and wire delay, and on FPGAs decide whether you are on the hard chain or the general fabric.

The RCA itself is the degenerate prefix graph — the fully sequential chain of $w$ simplified nodes (Fig. 5.17, p. 122). The three classics below are three bracketings of the *same* reduction, and they land at three corners of the design space.

**The trade-off, on one output bit.** Take $c_7=[6{:}0]=X_6\circ X_5\circ X_4\circ X_3\circ X_2\circ X_1\circ X_0$, a single associative product of seven factors and therefore $C_6=132$ legal bracketings (Catalan). Four of them are architectures — read straight off the figures below:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\textbf{RCA:}\quad & X_6\circ\Big(X_5\circ\big(X_4\circ\big(X_3\circ\big(X_2\circ(X_1\circ X_0)\big)\big)\big)\Big) && \text{depth }6\\[2pt]
\textbf{Kogge-Stone:}\quad & \big((X_6\circ X_5)\circ(X_4\circ X_3)\big)\circ\big((X_2\circ X_1)\circ X_0\big) && \text{depth }3\\[2pt]
\textbf{Sklansky:}\quad & \big(X_6\circ(X_5\circ X_4)\big)\circ\big((X_3\circ X_2)\circ(X_1\circ X_0)\big) && \text{depth }3\\[2pt]
\textbf{Brent-Kung:}\quad & X_6\circ\Big((X_5\circ X_4)\circ\big((X_3\circ X_2)\circ(X_1\circ X_0)\big)\Big) && \text{depth }4
\end{aligned}
$$

Every line is the same value **by associativity alone** — no arithmetic identity, no case analysis, nothing about carries. What separates them is which sub-products the *neighbouring* outputs can reuse:

- Kogge-Stone's sub-products $[6{:}5],[4{:}3],[2{:}1]$ sit on the offset that $c_8$'s tree ($[7{:}6],[5{:}4],[3{:}2]$) does not touch. Every output gets what is essentially its own balanced tree, shifted by one — so **no node ever needs more than two consumers**, and the price is the largest node count and the longest wires.
- Sklansky reaches the same depth by making $[3{:}0]$ the right operand of *all four* upper outputs $c_5,c_6,c_7,c_8$. That is maximal sharing at minimal depth — and it is precisely why $[3{:}0]$ carries fan-out $w/2$.
- Brent-Kung refuses to duplicate anything: $[5{:}0]$ is built once and $c_7$ is $X_6$ composed onto it. Every sub-product it names is already needed by some other output, giving the fewest nodes — paid for with one extra level.

Depth, node count, fan-out: pick two. Node counts below are counted directly off the $8$-bit graphs drawn here, which omit $X_{-1}=(c_0,c_0)$ — with a nonzero carry-in it enters as one extra rightmost column, or equivalently via $c_{i+1}=G_{i:0}\vee A_{i:0}c_0$ folded into the sum-completion row.

### Kogge-Stone

At stage $t=1,2,4,\ldots$ every position $i\ge t$ combines its own current prefix with the one $t$ positions down: $[i:i-2t+1]=[i:i-t+1]\circ[i-t:i-2t+1]$. After $\log_2 w$ stages every span reaches position $0$.

You pay in nodes ($w\log_2 w-w+1$; $17$ here) and, worse, in **wiring**: every stage carries $\approx w$ long horizontal spans of length $t$, so the wire tracks, not the gates, dominate the layout. Pick it when the operand is on the critical path of a high-frequency ASIC datapath and you can afford the metal.

Fan-out is a constant $3$, not $2$: count the consumers of $[3{:}0]$ in the graph above and you get the up-sweep node at column $7$, the down-sweep node at column $5$, and the down-sweep node at column $4$. That $3$ does not grow with $w$ (at $w=16$, $[7{:}0]$ likewise feeds exactly three nodes), which is the property that matters — bounded, unlike Sklansky's $w/2$. One extra stage compared to Kogge-Stone, roughly $2/3$ the nodes and a fraction of the wire tracks. Pick it when area, power or routing is the binding constraint, and on FPGAs where a stage costs little but a long wire costs a lot.

### Sklansky

Recursive halving on the *outputs*: at level $t$, every position in the upper half of each $2^t$-block hangs directly off that block's midpoint.

*after Fig. 5.18, p. 122.* Depth $\log_2 w$ — **as shallow as Kogge-Stone** — with only $(w/2)\log_2 w=12$ nodes, the same $12$ the book counts for its 8-point prefix-sum example against $8$ for the sequential version (p. 119). The bill arrives as **fan-out $w/2$**: the node at position $3$ feeds four consumers at $w=8$, sixteen at $w=32$. That is the identical pathology as the conditional-sum adder's mux tree, which is no accident — the conditional-sum adder *is* Sklansky with muxes in place of $\circ$-nodes. Pick it when $w$ is small enough that $w/2$ fan-out is still a light load, or when you are willing to buffer.

> [!note] The three are one construction
> Kogge-Stone, Brent-Kung and Sklansky are not three algorithms. They are three parenthesizations of $X_{w-1}\circ\cdots\circ X_{-1}$, all correct **for the single reason proved above**. Depth $\times$ node count $\times$ fan-out is a conserved-ish budget: Sklansky buys depth with fan-out, Brent-Kung buys area with depth, Kogge-Stone buys both with wires. Zimmermann's thesis catalogues the continuum (area, delay, wire cost, fan-out) and Harris's taxonomy parameterizes it; NVIDIA has since searched the space with reinforcement learning (p. 122–123). Prefix trees are also the natural target when input bits *arrive* at different times or output bits are *required* at different times — you shape the tree to the arrival profile.

### Compound fast adder

In a prefix adder, everything above the bottom row is independent of $c_0$; only the last row of simplified nodes touches it, and that row is **linear in $w$**. So duplicate just that row and get $X+Y$ and $X+Y+1$ simultaneously for a small additive cost (Fig. 5.20, p. 123). This is the standard significand adder in floating-point rounding, where the rounded result is either the sum or its successor — see [[Floating-Point Operators]].

### Fast absolute difference

For positive $X,Y$, compute $|X-Y|$ by producing both differences and selecting on a sign bit. From the two's-complement identity, $X-Y=X+\overline{Y}+1$ and $Y-X=\overline{X+\overline{Y}}$ *(proof, footnote p. 124: $X-Y-1=X+\overline{Y}$, and negating, $-(X-Y-1)=\overline{X+\overline{Y}}+1$, i.e. $Y-X=\overline{X+\overline{Y}}$.)* So **one** compound prefix tree over $(X,\overline{Y})$ yields both, and the selection is one constant-time mux (Fig. 5.21, p. 124). Neither bitwise negation costs delay: $\overline{Y}$ folds into the initial $(g_i,a_i)$ computation and $\overline{X+\overline{Y}}$ folds into the sum-completion XORs. The sign bit of either difference falls out for free, and one extra NOR gives an $X=Y$ flag — cheaper than a separate [[Comparators|comparator]].

### Fast compound triple sum

Some floating-point adders need $X+Y$, $X+Y+1$ and $X+Y+2$ at once. A row of half adders first rewrites $X+Y=S+C$ (Fig. 5.22, p. 125) — a carry-save step whose point is that **position $0$ collapses to the single bit $s_0$**, since $C$ starts at weight $2$. Run a compound adder on positions $1\ldots w+1$ to get $Z$ and $Z'$ (one more at that scale, i.e. $+2$ overall), and read off (Eq. 5.49, p. 125), writing $Zb$ for concatenation with LSB $b$:

$$\text{if } s_0=0:\;\begin{cases}X+Y=Z0\\X+Y+1=Z1\\X+Y+2=Z'0\end{cases}\qquad\qquad \text{else}:\;\begin{cases}X+Y=Z1\\X+Y+1=Z'0\\X+Y+2=Z'1\end{cases}$$

Overhead over a compound adder: $w$ half adders and three $(w+2)$-bit multiplexers. No extra prefix tree.

## Adders on FPGAs

Two facts change everything (p. 125). First, carry propagation runs on **hardened, non-programmable** carry logic and dedicated routing — *fast carry logic* — which is dramatically faster than the general fabric ([[FPGAs]], [[Lookup-Tables]]). Second, the adder barely uses the LUT it sits in, so **arbitrary logic can be merged in front of it for free**.

### AMD carry chain

On series 4–7 and UltraScale/UltraScale+, each BLE's carry logic is one XOR plus one MUX. The LUT computes $p_i=x_i\oplus y_i$, and the BLE realizes $s_i=p_i\oplus c_i$ and $c_{i+1}=p_ic_i\vee\overline{p_i}\,x_i$ (Eqs. 5.50–5.51, p. 126), which is the mux form (5.15) of the FA: if $p_i=1$ pass $c_i$, else pass $x_i$ (valid because $p_i=0$ means $x_i=y_i$). **This is a switched ripple-carry adder in silicon** (Fig. 5.23, p. 126).

On Versal the XOR leaves the chain; the MUX equivalent moves into a fast carry-lookahead unit (`LOOKAHEAD8`) that computes **every second carry** from the propagates and intermediate carries held in the LUTs. Of the eight BLEs A–H in a slice, only `COUTB/D/F/H` are produced by lookahead; the odd carries are computed in LUT4s as $c_{i+1}=x_iy_i\vee x_ic_i\vee y_ic_i$ (Eqs. 5.54–5.56) and passed by `casc_in`, and the sum becomes $s_i=p_i\overline{c_i}\vee\overline{p_i}c_i=p_i\oplus c_i$ (Eqs. 5.52–5.53, p. 127). Carry propagation is then only from even to odd BLEs, which combined with the lookahead unit yields fast adders flexible in size up to 32 bits.

### Intel carry chain

Simpler: each BLE contains a **complete hardened FA** with dedicated carry wires to its neighbours, so an RCA is a direct mapping (p. 128). The only constraints are placement ones — ten ALMs per LAB (an ALM being two BLEs with partially shared inputs), and a carry chain may start only at the first or the sixth ALM of a LAB.

### Merging extra functionality into an adder

Because the adder leaves most of the LUT unused, the LUT in front of each FA input is free logic. On Intel (Fig. 4.6, p. 93), an RCA can compute $(c_{i+1},s_i)=\text{FA}\big(p(a,b,c,d),\,q(b,c,d,e),\,c_i\big)$ (Eq. 5.57, p. 129) with $p,q$ two parallel LUT4s sharing most inputs. On AMD (Fig. 5.25, p. 130) the fracturable LUT6 gives either two LUT5s with shared inputs, one per FA input (Eq. 5.58), or a single LUT6 on one FA input (Eq. 5.59); Versal gives two LUT4s per input (Eq. 5.60). What people put in that free logic (p. 129): a third addend (below), an adder/subtractor select, a source multiplexer, a table lookup on <5 bits (KCM — see [[Multiplication by Constants]]), or partial-product generation for [[Multipliers]]. This is the book's canonical **target-specific optimization**.

### Ternary adders

Spend the free LUT layer on a row of FAs acting as a **carry-save compressor**: three vectors in, two out, no carry propagation in that layer; the RCA below then collapses them on the hard carry chain (Fig. 5.26a, p. 131). One row of BLEs computes $S=X+Y+Z$ (Eq. 5.61). In VLSI this saves nothing — same FA count. On FPGAs it is nearly free, because the CSA layer lands in LUTs that were idle. Details in [[Ternary Adders on FPGAs]].

- **Intel** (p. 132): Arria I/II/V and Stratix II–V ALMs have a *shared arithmetic mode* for exactly this; **it was dropped in Stratix 10**. Where supported, a ternary adder uses **exactly the same number of ALMs as a binary adder of the same output width**, and is only slightly slower. Quartus infers it from `S <= X+Y+Z`. Negated forms are not inferrable: invert the inputs, set the carry-in bits, and **extend the word by 1 bit for $X-Y+Z$ and $X+Y-Z$, by 2 bits for $X-Y-Z$**, costing extra ALMs.
- **AMD** (p. 132): maps to every family with a fracturable 6-LUT — Virtex 5-7, Spartan 6, Kintex 7, Artix 7, Zynq, UltraScale/+ (Fig. 5.26c). The CSA's FA shares the LUT with the XOR of Fig. 5.23. The catch: the $c_i'$ carries are computed in parallel but must reach the next FA **through the general routing fabric**, so at low word sizes a ternary adder is **up to 50 % slower than a binary one**. AMD tools will not infer this mapping — instantiate primitives, or generate with [[FloPoCo]] (`flopoco target=virtex6 XilinxTernaryAddSub wIn=16`, with `AddSubBitMask=3` selecting $-X-Y+Z$; p. 133). Ternary subtract variants cost no extra resources here.

### Reconfigurable adder/subtractor

The §5.2.5 adder/subtractor costs **nothing** over a plain adder on FPGA: its XORs land in the same LUTs the RCA already occupies (p. 133). Negating *both* inputs is the hard case — $-X-Y$ needs a constant $2$, and there is only one carry-in — so use the ternary structure instead [KKZ16], computing $S=(-1)^{\texttt{sub}_X}X+(-1)^{\texttt{sub}_Y}Y$ (Eq. 5.62). The $+1$ needed when exactly one select is set is an XOR of the two selects; the $+2$ needed when both are set is their AND (Figs. 5.27–5.28, pp. 134–135). Driving $\texttt{sub}_X,\texttt{sub}_Y$ from the sign bits of $X,Y$ turns this into an absolute-value accumulator — the sum-of-absolute-differences (SAD) kernel of image processing.

## Fast adders on FPGAs

> [!important] The hard carry chain beats every clever tree, up to surprising widths
> *"Fast adder architectures as discussed in Sect. 5.3 are useless when targeting FPGAs for typical sizes (up to 32 bits)"* — p. 134. The numbers, for Virtex 6 at the fastest speed grade (pp. 126–127):
> - carry-in to carry-out across a whole slice of four BLEs: $\le 0.06$ ns $\Rightarrow$ $\tau_\text{cp}=\mathbf{0.015\ \text{ns}}$ **per bit**;
> - LUT combinational delay $\tau_\text{LUT}=0.14\ldots0.32$ ns; carry-in to BLE output $\tau_\text{XOR}=0.21\ldots0.25$ ns; worst-case full adder $\tau_\text{fa}=\mathbf{0.57\ \text{ns}}$ — **$38\times$ the carry chain delay**;
> - dedicated CLB-to-CLB carry links, bypassing the general routing fabric entirely;
> - so from (5.7): $w=8,16,32$ cost about **1, 1.5 and 2.5 ns**.
>
> Rippling 32 bits costs $0.48$ ns of chain. A prefix tree would replace that with $\log_2 32=5$ levels of *general-fabric* logic at $\ge 0.14$ ns of LUT plus routing each — and routing, not logic, is what dominates on an FPGA. A tree loses before it starts. Combinatorial FPGA speedups only pay off **beyond 256 bits** (p. 137), which is why the research is concentrated in cryptography, where RSA keys run past 1024 bits (p. 104).

### Pipelined adders

In VLSI a flip-flop costs about what a full adder costs, so pipelining an adder is not competitive against a fast adder — and it delays the result by cycles, forcing more FFs elsewhere for synchronization. On FPGAs the LUT/FF ratio is close to 1, the FFs sit inside the BLEs already wired to the adder output, and an unused FF is **wasted, not saved**. So pipelining is the natural FPGA answer (p. 135).

Classic pipelining (Fig. 5.29a, p. 136) cuts the RCA into $m$-bit groups, one per stage. Each stage beyond the first needs $2m$ input FFs, $m$ output FFs and **one** carry FF — and only that single carry FF actually cuts the critical path; the other $3m$ are pure balancing. The output registers are free (BLE registers); FF chains can be folded into shift-register LUTs (SRLs). The [DNP10] variant (Fig. 5.29b) eliminates about half the balancing BLEs by observing that a bare FF costs about as much BLE as a FF+FA: put real $m$-bit adders in the first stage to *compress* what the later stages must store, and let the later adders only add the previous stage's carries. Result: **any word size at reasonable cost, at the price of latency**. Generate with [[FloPoCo]]: `flopoco frequency=300 IntAdder wIn=1024` (p. 137).

### Fast combinatorial adders

The **add-add-multiplex (AAM)** carry-select architecture [DNP10; NPP11] (Fig. 5.30, p. 138). Two adders per $m$-bit segment produce carry candidates $c_i^0,c_i^1$ for both carry-in cases, as in ordinary carry-select — but the selection is *not* a mux tree. Observe (Table 5.2, p. 139) that the candidate pair reproduces the kill/propagate/generate cases exactly, with $c_i^0c_i^1=10$ impossible, so $c_i=c_i^0\vee c_{i-m}c_i^1$ (Eq. 5.63) — which is the prefix recurrence again, at group granularity. Rather than route $c_{i-m}$ through general logic, feed the candidates into an RCA — the **carry computation circuit (CCC)**, literally a ripple-carry adder on the fast chain — whose sum bit is

$$s_i=c_i^0\oplus c_i^1\oplus c_{i-m}=\begin{cases}c_{i-m}&\text{kill or generate}\\\overline{c_{i-m}}&\text{propagate}\end{cases}$$

so $c_{i-m}$ in (5.63) can be replaced by $\overline{s_i}$, giving $c_i=c_i^0\vee\overline{s_i}c_i^1$ (Eq. 5.66). The residual **carry recovery (CR)** logic plus its mux fits in a single LUT5 per output bit. The whole thing pipelines cheaply, since most FFs are idle anyway. [NPP11] report resources comparable to the pipelined adder above at equal speed, but with **single-cycle latency for adders beyond 256 bits**. [LPB19] swap the CCC for full prefix networks — Brent-Kung, Han-Carlson, Kogge-Stone, Sklansky — on **1024 to 8192-bit** adders and find a *"quasi linear relationship between prefix tree complexity and performance"*: on FPGA, unlike ASIC, the trees line up in a predictable order once the operands are huge enough for anything to matter.

## Trade-offs

Depth is in $\circ$-node / gate stages; node counts for the prefix rows are counted off the 8-bit graphs above (closed forms in parentheses).

| Architecture | Logic depth | Area | Max fan-out | Wiring | src |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **RCA** | $O(w)$: $(w-1)\tau_\text{cp}+\max(\tau_\text{cp},\tau_\text{fa})$ | minimal: $w$ FA | 1 | nearest-neighbour only | p. 108 |
| **Carry-select**, group $m$ | $\tau_\text{CPA}(m)+(\lceil w/m\rceil-1)\tau_\text{mux}$ | $\approx2\times$ RCA + muxes | $m$ (mux select) | local + one carry per group | p. 115 |
| **Conditional-sum** | $O(\log w)$: $\tau_\text{add}(1)+\log_2w\,\tau_\text{mux}$ | linear in $w$ (leaf CPAs shared) | $w/2$ | mux tree | pp. 115–116 |
| **CLA**, group $m$ | 2 gate levels/group, $O(w/m)$ overall | high; fan-in grows with $m$ | $m$ | wide AND/OR per group | p. 117 |
| **Recursive CLA** | $O(\log_m w)$ | tree of fat CLA-$m$ nodes | $m$ | shallowest tree, heaviest nodes | p. 118 |
| **Kogge-Stone** | $\log_2w=3$ — **minimum** | 17 ($w\log_2w-w+1$) | **2** | heaviest: $\approx w$ long spans per stage | pp. 121, 123 |
| **Sklansky** | $\log_2w=3$ | 12 ($\tfrac{w}{2}\log_2w$) | $w/2=4$ | light | pp. 119, 122 |
| **Brent-Kung** | $2\log_2w-2=4$ | **11** ($2w-2-\log_2w$) | $3$, constant in $w$ | lightest, very regular | p. 122 |
| **FPGA hard carry chain** | $O(w)$ but $\tau_\text{cp}=0.015$ ns/bit; $\approx$**1 / 1.5 / 2.5 ns** at $w=8/16/32$ | $w$ BLEs, LUTs mostly free | 1 | dedicated carry links, no general fabric | pp. 126–127 |
| **FPGA ternary adder** | $\approx$ RCA + 1 LUT layer; up to **50 % slower** than binary at small $w$ (AMD) | same ALM count as a binary adder (Intel, shared-arith mode) | 1 | CSA carries cross the general fabric (AMD) | p. 132 |
| **FPGA AAM combinatorial** | benefit only at $w\ge$ **256**; prefix variants at 1024–8192 | $\approx$ pipelined adder at equal speed | — | CCC rides the fast chain | pp. 137, 139 |

**Picking one.** On **ASIC**: below ~16 bits or off the critical path, use an RCA and stop thinking. On the critical path, use a prefix tree — Kogge-Stone if you can pay for metal and want minimum depth, Brent-Kung if area/power/routing binds, Sklansky if $w$ is small enough that $w/2$ fan-out is still light. On **FPGA**: use `+` and let the tool infer the hard carry chain, up to at least 32 bits and in practice well past it. If you need more throughput, **pipeline** it; only past ~256 bits does a combinatorial FPGA-specific scheme like AAM earn its keep. And if you are adding three numbers, ask for a **ternary adder** before you ask for two adders.

## Worked example

$w=8$, two's complement, $c_0=0$, $X=01101101_2=109$, $Y=01010011_2=83$. Per-bit signals ($i=7\ldots0$), from $g_i=x_iy_i$, $a_i=x_i\vee y_i$, $p_i=x_i\oplus y_i$:

| $i$ | 7 | 6 | 5 | 4 | 3 | 2 | 1 | 0 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $x_i$ | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| $y_i$ | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| $g_i$ | 0 | **1** | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | **1** |
| $a_i$ | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| $p_i$ | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 0 |

**Ripple-carry.** Apply $c_{i+1}=g_i\vee a_ic_i$ strictly left to right — eight sequential steps:

$$
\begin{aligned}
c_1&=g_0\vee a_0c_0=1\vee1\!\cdot\!0=\mathbf1 &
c_2&=g_1\vee a_1c_1=0\vee1\!\cdot\!1=\mathbf1 \\
c_3&=g_2\vee a_2c_2=0\vee1\!\cdot\!1=\mathbf1 &
c_4&=g_3\vee a_3c_3=0\vee1\!\cdot\!1=\mathbf1 \\
c_5&=g_4\vee a_4c_4=0\vee1\!\cdot\!1=\mathbf1 &
c_6&=g_5\vee a_5c_5=0\vee1\!\cdot\!1=\mathbf1 \\
c_7&=g_6\vee a_6c_6=1\vee1\!\cdot\!1=\mathbf1 &
c_8&=g_7\vee a_7c_7=0\vee0\!\cdot\!1=\mathbf0
\end{aligned}
$$

The carry born at bit $0$ survives **all seven** propagate stages — the worst case (5.7) predicts. Sum completion, $s_i=p_i\oplus c_i$:

where the carry vector is written LSB-aligned, $c_7c_6\ldots c_0=11111110_2$ (bit $0$ sees $c_0=0$):

$$S=p\oplus c=00111110_2\oplus 11111110_2=\mathbf{11000000}_2=192 \;\;\checkmark\;(109+83)$$

Overflow: unsigned, $c_8=0$, none — $192$ is representable in 8 unsigned bits. Signed, $c_8\oplus c_7=0\oplus1=\mathbf1$: overflow, correctly, since $192>127$. (Here the book's $s_7\oplus c_\text{out}=1\oplus0=1$ happens to agree; see the warning above for where it does not.)

**Kogge-Stone, same operands.** Write $[i{:}j]=(G_{i:j},A_{i:j})$ and apply $\circ$ level by level.

*Level 1* ($t=1$), $[i{:}i{-}1]=(g_i,a_i)\circ(g_{i-1},a_{i-1})$ — e.g. $[1{:}0]=(0,1)\circ(1,1)=(0\vee1\!\cdot\!1,\;1\!\cdot\!1)=(1,1)$ and $[7{:}6]=(0,0)\circ(1,1)=(0\vee0\!\cdot\!1,\;0\!\cdot\!1)=(0,0)$:

$$[7{:}6]=(0,0),\;\;[6{:}5]=(1,1),\;\;[5{:}4]=[4{:}3]=[3{:}2]=[2{:}1]=(0,1),\;\;[1{:}0]=(1,1)$$

*Level 2* ($t=2$), $[i{:}i{-}3]=[i{:}i{-}1]\circ[i{-}2{:}i{-}3]$:

$$[7{:}4]=(0,0),\;\;[6{:}3]=(1,1),\;\;[5{:}2]=[4{:}1]=(0,1),\;\;[3{:}0]=[2{:}0]=(1,1)$$

*Level 3* ($t=4$), $[i{:}0]=[i{:}i{-}3]\circ[i{-}4{:}i{-}7]$:

$$[7{:}0]=(0,0)\circ(1,1)=(0,0),\qquad [6{:}0]=[5{:}0]=[4{:}0]=(1,1)$$

With $c_0=0$ the extraction $c_{i+1}=G_{i:0}\vee A_{i:0}c_0$ collapses to $c_{i+1}=G_{i:0}$, giving $(c_1,\ldots,c_8)=(1,1,1,1,1,1,1,0)$ — **identical to the ripple-carry column**, as associativity guarantees. Same $p_i$, therefore the same $S=11000000_2$.

**Critical paths.** Both start with one gate level for $(g_i,a_i)$ and end with one XOR for $s_i$. Between them:

| | $\circ$-stages on the critical path | total |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ripple-carry | 8 (strictly sequential) | $1+8+1=10$ |
| Kogge-Stone | 3 ($\log_2 8$) | $1+3+1=5$ |

Half the depth at $w=8$; at $w=64$ it is $66$ versus $8$. The cost is $17$ prefix nodes instead of $8$, and the wire spans of lengths $1,2,4$ visible in the graph above.

**Error: 0 ulp.** Both results are exact — the $9$-bit output $c_8S$ represents $192$ with no rounding whatsoever. Every bit of error in a fixed-point datapath comes from the format decisions of [[Error Accuracy]], never from the adder.
