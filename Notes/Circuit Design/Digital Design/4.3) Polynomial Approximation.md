**Input.** One fixed-point word $X$ of $w_X$ bits, normalized to $[0,1)$ or $[-1,1)$ (format conventions in [[1.1) Number Formats]]; the normalization itself is [[4.1) Fixed-Point Function Approximation]]).
**Output.** $R(X)$ of LSB $\ell_R$, approximating a function $f$ that is continuously differentiable to order $d+1$ on the interval.
**Accuracy.** *Faithful* (last-bit accurate): $\bar\delta_{\text{total}}<2^{\ell_R}$. That one ulp is split four ways — approximation, coefficient quantization, evaluation rounding, final rounding — and every width in the datapath is derived from that split ([[1.2) Error Accuracy]]).
**Optimized for.** The product of two currencies: **table bits** (the coefficient ROM, exponential in the segmentation parameter $\alpha$) and **multipliers** (linear in the degree $d$). Latency is the tie-breaker.
**Why this family exists.** [[4.2) Table-Based Function Evaluation]] runs out of room around $w_X\approx24$ because its table stays exponential in $w_X/2$. A degree-$d$ polynomial moves that exponent to $w_X/(d+1)$ — you buy it with $d$ multipliers.

## A Primer on Polynomial Approximation

A degree-$d$ polynomial $p(X)=C_0+C_1X+\dots+C_dX^d$ needs only adds and multiplies, which is the entire reason it is here. The quality of the fit is the **error function**

$$\delta_{\text{approx}}(X)=p(X)-f(X),\qquad \bar\delta_{\text{approx}}=\|f-p\|_{I,\infty}=\max_{X\in I}|f(X)-p(X)|$$

and its useful form for hardware is $\log_2\bar\delta_{\text{approx}}$, the **accuracy in bits**: a polynomial with $\bar\delta_{\text{approx}}=2^{-15}$ can drive an operator faithful to no LSB smaller than $-15$.

Normalizing $I$ to $[0,1)$ or $[-1,1)$ is not cosmetic. Since $|X|\le1$ implies $|C_iX^i|\le|C_i|$, **the MSB of every monomial is the MSB of its coefficient**. The alignment of the summands in the datapath is therefore read straight off the coefficient table, with no interval-dependent analysis (Fig. 18.6, p. 537).

### Taylor: the wrong tool

Taylor at $X_0$ truncated at degree $d$ has Lagrange remainder

$$f(X)-T_d(X)=\frac{f^{(d+1)}(\xi)}{(d+1)!}(X-X_0)^{d+1}.$$

The error is (to leading order) a *single monomial*: it has a zero of multiplicity $d+1$ at $X_0$ and grows monotonically away from it. On $[0,1]$ expanded at $0$ it is exactly zero at one end and maximal at the other. All the accuracy is spent where you did not need it.

Concretely, from Table 18.3 (p. 534), $e^X$ on $[0,1]$:

| degree | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\bar\delta_{\text{Taylor}}$ (at $X_0=0$) | $0.22\ (2^{-2})$ | $0.052\ (2^{-4})$ | $0.0099\ (2^{-6})$ | $0.0016\ (2^{-9})$ |
| $\bar\delta_{\text{Remez}}$ | $0.0088\ (2^{-6.8})$ | $0.00054\ (2^{-10.8})$ | $0.000027\ (2^{-15.2})$ | $0.0000011\ (2^{-19.8})$ |
| ratio | $25$ | $96$ | $367$ | $1454$ |

## Remez Minimax

The fix is to stop demanding a high-order zero anywhere and instead demand that the error be **equioscillating**. Chebyshev's alternation theorem: $p$ is the unique degree-$d$ minimax approximation of $f$ on $I$ iff there exist $d+2$ points $x_0<\dots<x_{d+1}$ in $I$ with

$$f(x_j)-p(x_j)=(-1)^{j}\sigma\bar\delta_{\text{approx}},\qquad \sigma=\pm1 .$$

Remez's 1934 algorithm [Rem34] is the obvious fixed point of that characterisation: guess $d+2$ points, solve the **linear** system in the $d+2$ unknowns $C_0,\dots,C_d,E$

$$\sum_{i=0}^{d}C_i x_j^{\,i}+(-1)^jE=f(x_j),\qquad j=0\dots d+1,$$

then replace each $x_j$ by the nearest local extremum of the new error and repeat. It converges quadratically; two or three exchanges is typical. That is all you need to know — call `remez(f,d,[a;b])` in Sollya and move on (p. 534).

Why the gain is so large is worth one line of algebra. Write the error as $\frac{f^{(d+1)}}{(d+1)!}q(X)$ with $q$ monic of degree $d+1$. Taylor picks $q(X)=(X-X_0)^{d+1}$, whose sup norm on an interval of half-width $h$ is $h^{d+1}$ if $X_0$ is the centre and $(2h)^{d+1}$ if $X_0$ is an endpoint. Minimax picks the monic polynomial of least sup norm, which is the scaled **Chebyshev polynomial** $2^{-d}h^{d+1}T_{d+1}$, of sup norm $h^{d+1}/2^{d}$. So against edge-Taylor,

$$\frac{\bar\delta_{\text{Taylor}}}{\bar\delta_{\text{Remez}}}\approx\frac{(2h)^{d+1}}{h^{d+1}/2^{d}}=2^{2d+1}$$

— $d+1$ bits for moving the expansion point to the centre, $d$ more for equioscillation. Predicted $32,128,512,2048$ against the measured $25,96,367,1454$ above; the residual factor $\approx1.35$ is $f^{(d+1)}$ not being constant on the interval.

> [!note] Taylor is not always wrong
> Taylor coefficients are often exactly $0$, $1$ or $1/2$ (p. 539), and those cost nothing in the datapath — which can outweigh the worse error, as in [Che15]'s degree-3 evaluator. It is also the right tool when *deriving* coefficients analytically, as in the sine identity below.

## The Two Scaling Laws

Everything downstream is set by how $\bar\delta_{\text{approx}}$ responds to the two knobs.

**Law 1 — degree.** Accuracy in bits is roughly *linear in the degree* (Fig. 18.4, p. 535): each extra degree buys a fixed, function-dependent number of bits, so error falls geometrically. Rule of thumb: **to double the number of accurate bits, double the degree.** For $\log_2(1+X)$ on $[0,1)$, measured here: $d=1,2,3\Rightarrow2^{-4.54},2^{-7.66},2^{-10.62}$, i.e. $\approx3.1$ bits per degree.

**Law 2 — interval size.** From the Chebyshev bound above, on an interval of width $2h$,

$$\bar\delta_{\text{approx}}\le\frac{\|f^{(d+1)}\|_{\infty}}{2^{2d+1}(d+1)!}\,(2h)^{d+1}\ \propto\ h^{d+1}.$$

Shrink the interval by $2^{k}$ and the error falls by $2^{-k(d+1)}$:

$$\boxed{\ \text{shrinking the interval by }2^{k}\text{ buys }k(d+1)\text{ bits.}\ }$$

Verified here for $\log_2(1+X)$ on the worst subinterval, $\alpha=0\to3$ (bits of accuracy):

| $d$ | $\alpha=0$ | $1$ | $2$ | $3$ | increments | $d+1$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 4.54 | 6.08 | 7.80 | 9.64 | 1.54, 1.72, 1.84 | 2 |
| 2 | 7.66 | 9.97 | 12.55 | 15.31 | 2.31, 2.58, 2.76 | 3 |
| 3 | 10.62 | 13.69 | 17.13 | 20.82 | 3.07, 3.44, 3.69 | 4 |

Convergence to $d+1$ is from below because the *worst* subinterval is the one nearest $0$, where $f^{(d+1)}$ is largest.

Combining: to reach $w_R$ accurate bits with degree $d$ over $2^{\alpha}$ segments,

$$\alpha(d+1)+\beta_f\,d\ \gtrsim\ w_R\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \alpha\approx\frac{w_R}{d+1}\ \ (\text{minus a function-dependent bonus}).$$

The coefficient table holds $2^{\alpha}$ entries of $\sum_i(m_i-\ell_p)$ bits, so its size is $\Theta\!\left(2^{w_R/(d+1)}\right)$: **exponential in $1/(d+1)$**. This reproduces, from first principles, the bounds Chap. 17 quotes for table-and-addition methods — $\alpha\gtrsim w_X/2$ for order one, $\alpha\gtrsim w_X/3$ for order two ([[4.2) Table-Based Function Evaluation]], p. 523). Measured at $w_X=w_R=24$: $d=1\Rightarrow\alpha=12$ (exactly $w_R/2$), $d=2\Rightarrow\alpha=7$, $d=3\Rightarrow\alpha=5$.

> [!important] More table versus more multipliers — and why the optimum is a low degree over many segments
> The two laws are in direct opposition. A larger $\alpha$ means smaller intervals, so a lower degree suffices, so fewer multipliers; but the number of stored polynomials is $2^{\alpha}$, and that exponential *dwarfs* the per-polynomial saving from fewer coefficients (p. 542). **A larger $\alpha$ buys arithmetic with memory.**
>
> The asymmetry that settles it: the table exponent is $w_R/(d+1)$, so the marginal saving from one more degree is $\frac{w_R}{d+1}-\frac{w_R}{d+2}=\frac{w_R}{(d+1)(d+2)}$ bits of exponent — $w_R/6$ going from $d=1$ to $2$, $w_R/12$ from $2$ to $3$, $w_R/20$ from $3$ to $4$. Quadratically diminishing. Meanwhile each degree costs exactly one more multiplier and one more Horner stage of latency, forever. The first step or two is overwhelmingly worth it and the rest is not: for 24 bits the table falls $167936\to7808$ bits ($\times21$) for the first multiplier, then only $\times3.1$ and $\times2.7$. **Degree 2 or 3 over a few dozen to a few hundred segments is the answer for single precision**, which is exactly where all the published parallel evaluators sit (p. 560).
>
> Whichever knob the generator exposes — FloPoCo takes $d$ and finds the smallest $\alpha$ — it takes so few values that plain trial and error settles it in seconds (p. 542).

## Machine-Efficient Polynomial Approximation

Remez coefficients are reals; the hardware needs bit vectors. Rounding them gives a *different* polynomial $\tilde p$, and there is no reason the rounded minimax is the best polynomial *with representable coefficients*.

Two ways to account for it (p. 536). The clumsy one adds a term $\delta_{\text{round-coeff}}=\tilde p_{\text{Remez}}-p_{\text{Remez}}$. The right one **redefines** $\delta_{\text{approx}}(X)=\tilde p(X)-f(X)$ — simpler, and tighter by the triangle inequality. Coefficient quantization is thereby *inside* the approximation error and never appears again in the budget.

Sollya's `fpminimax` [BC07] searches directly over polynomials obeying per-coefficient LSB constraints, landing extremely close to the constrained minimax. Which LSBs to ask for? All monomials must be about equally accurate — the sum is no better than its worst term — and for $X$ near $1$, $C_iX^i$ cannot beat $C_i$ itself. So give every coefficient the *same* LSB $\ell_p$. Summing $d+1$ round-to-nearest errors of $2^{\ell_p-1}$:

$$(d+1)\,2^{\ell_p-1}\lesssim\bar\delta^{\text{target}}_{\text{approx}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ell_p\approx\log_2\bar\delta^{\text{target}}_{\text{approx}}-\log_2(d+1),$$

which the book rounds up to the starting guess $\ell_p=\lceil\log_2\bar\delta^{\text{target}}_{\text{approx}}\rceil+d$ and then *decrements* until `fpminimax` succeeds (Algorithm 18.1, p. 539). Surprisingly, polynomials meeting the target often exist even when $2^{\ell_p}$ is slightly larger than it — hence the search rather than a formula. Terminating requires $d$ large enough that the unconstrained degree-$d$ Remez error is strictly under target.

One relaxation is nearly free: $C_0$ only feeds an addition, never a product ([[#Horner Evaluation]]), so extra LSBs there are cheap. Trading bits of $C_0$ for bits of the other coefficients is usually a win (p. 538).

## Polynomials Are a Special Case of Rational Fractions

$$R(x)=\frac{P_0+P_1x+\dots+P_nx^n}{1+Q_1x+\dots+Q_mx^m},\qquad m=0\ \Rightarrow\ \text{polynomial}.$$

Remez extends to the rational case, but the book largely declines it (p. 540):

- **No singularity in $I$:** rule of thumb [BM04] is that degree $(n,m)$ gives about the accuracy of a polynomial of degree $n+m$ — same multiplier count, *plus a divider* ([[2.5) Dividers]]). Not attractive unless the division fuses into the multiplications, as in the E-method.
- **Singularity in $I$** ($\tan$, $\sqrt{\cdot}$ at $0$): rationals can capture it as a denominator zero, but usually a range reduction avoids the singularity more cheaply, and an explicit divider keeps the algorithm visible — $\tan=\sin/\cos$ wants a *shared* range reduction that a blind rational fit would destroy.
- The parallelism argument (numerator and denominator concurrently) is voided by the division's latency, and [[#Parallel Evaluation]] gives the same parallelism without a divider.

### Uniform piecewise splitting

Split $I$ into $2^{\alpha}$ equal subintervals. The change of variable is a *wire split* (Fig. 18.9, p. 541): $A$ is the top $\alpha$ bits, $Y$ the bottom $w_X-\alpha$.

$$X=2^{-\alpha}(A+Y),\quad Y\in[0,1),\ A\in\{0\dots2^{\alpha}-1\};\qquad f_{u,A}(Y)=f\!\left(2^{-\alpha}(A+Y)\right).\tag{18.3–18.4}$$

**Centre the reduced argument.** Better to use $Y_s\in[-1,1)$:

$$X=2^{-\alpha}\!\left(A+\tfrac12+\tfrac{Y_s}{2}\right),\qquad f_{s,A}(Y_s)=f\!\left(2^{-\alpha}\!\left(A+\tfrac12+\tfrac{Y_s}{2}\right)\right).\tag{18.5–18.6}$$

The argument is more reduced (half-width $2^{-\alpha-1}$ about the centre instead of full width about the left edge), so by Law 2 the coefficients shrink — Fig. 18.11, p. 544 shows $C_1,C_2,C_3$ gaining several leading zeros each on the same subinterval of $\exp$. The entire hardware cost is **one inverter** on the MSB of $Y$.

**Coefficient sizing.** Each subinterval gets its own polynomial with its own formats $(m_i(A),\ell_i(A))$, but the wires are shared, so take the worst case (Eqs. 18.7–18.8, p. 543):

$$m_i=\max_A m_i(A),\qquad \ell_p=\min_A\ell_i(A).$$

Better still, hoist the $A$-loop *inside* Algorithm 18.1 so all subintervals share one $\ell_p$ from the start: the spare LSBs on the easy intervals then store *more accurate* coefficients instead of zeros, shrinking $\bar\delta_{\text{approx}}$ and enlarging the evaluation budget for free (p. 565).

**Compression opportunities.** Plot $C_i$ against $A$ (Figs. 18.12–18.14, pp. 545–546) and the curves are smooth — perfectly smooth if the polynomials were Remez or Chebyshev; the wiggles are `fpminimax` artefacts, mostly on the highest-degree coefficient. Two consequences:

- Hsiao's lossless differential compression ([[4.2) Table-Based Function Evaluation]]) applies to *every* coefficient table, not just $C_0$. It costs one addition, which merges into a bit heap ([[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]]) and hides behind the later Horner stages.
- All coefficients of a given degree usually share a sign; then the sign bit is not stored at all — one bit per coefficient, checked numerically, not proved (p. 547).

The smoothness also hints that a *function-specific* reduction can compute the coefficients rather than store them. For $\sin(\frac\pi2X)$, apply the addition theorem to (18.3) with $a=\frac\pi22^{-\alpha}A$ and $b=\frac\pi22^{-\alpha}Y$, then a degree-3 Taylor on the small argument $b$:

$$C_0=\sin a,\quad C_1=\tfrac\pi22^{-\alpha}\cos a,\quad C_2=-\tfrac{\pi^2}{2^3}2^{-2\alpha}C_0,\quad C_3=-\tfrac{\pi^3}{2^3\cdot6}2^{-3\alpha}C_1\tag{18.12–18.15}$$

— all four read off one table of $C_0$, with $\cos$ folded in by $\cos x=\sin(\frac\pi2-x)$. (The book labels 18.15 "$C_2$"; it is $C_3$.) This is the seed of [[5.1) CORDIC]]'s rival in Chap. 20.

### Interpolation

Tabulate *function values* and derive the coefficients from neighbouring entries [Lee+08], instead of tabulating coefficients. Attractive on paper — only degree-0 data is stored — but (pp. 547–548):

- computing the coefficients is extra hardware, in area and delay;
- it scales backwards with degree: higher degree needs *more* tabulated values, so table **and** arithmetic both grow, whereas with tabulated polynomials the table *shrinks* as the arithmetic grows;
- the tabulated values must be more accurate than the target, because the interpolation amplifies their error;
- it does not compose with non-uniform reductions.

Verdict [Lee+08]: tabulating polynomials wins, except possibly for degree-1 uniform — and there multipartite beats both [DT05].

### Logarithmic piecewise splitting

When $f$'s derivative varies wildly across $I$ (Fig. 18.15, p. 549 plots $\sqrt{\cdot}$), uniform segmentation is unbalanced: the degree is set by the hardest subinterval and wasted on all the others. Index by the **leading-zero count** instead ([[2.6) Shifters]] — a combined LZC-and-shifter):

$$X=2^{-A-1}(1+Y),\quad f_{u,A}(Y)=f\!\left(2^{-A-1}(1+Y)\right);\qquad\text{signed: }X=2^{-A-2}(3+Y_s).\tag{18.16–18.17}$$

For $X=.00101$: $A=2$, shift left by 2 gives $10100$, drop the constant leading 1, $Y=.0100$.

Three consequences (p. 550):

1. **$w_Y$ is not reduced.** At $A=0$ there is no shift, so all of $X$ survives minus the constant leading bit — multipliers are wider than uniform segmentation's $w_X-\alpha$. Mitigation: $Y=\lfloor X_{\text{shifted}}\rfloor$, shaving LSBs. Why it works: in the wide $A=0$ interval $f$ has a small slope, so consecutive inputs round to the same output; dropping $k$ LSBs only affects the $k$ rightmost intervals, since elsewhere the shifter injected zeros there anyway. Treat $w_Y$ as a parameter, start at $w_X-1$, decrement until accuracy breaks.
2. **Degenerate leftmost intervals.** The two leftmost are single points, where the polynomial collapses to $C_0$ alone. That is how logarithmic segmentation absorbs a **discontinuity** or an infinite derivative at $0$ — its main reason to exist.
3. **No knob.** Nothing corresponds to $\alpha$. Fix that and the non-power-of-two table size at once by **saturating** the leading-bit count at $\alpha=\lceil\log_2 w_X\rceil$ or less, merging the leftmost intervals into one.

Variants: leading-*one* count for discontinuities on the right, leading-*sign* count for both sides.

### Hierarchical segmentation

Stack levels [Lee+03]; experience [Lee+09] says only two combinations pay (p. 551). **UU** — uniform inside uniform: a small level-2 parameter table indexed by $A_1$ (the top $\alpha_1$ bits) yields $\alpha_2(A_1)$ and an offset $\Delta(A_1)$ pointing at the first polynomial of that block; since $\alpha_2$ varies, a shifter splits $Y_1$ into $(A_2,Y_2)$ and the address is $\Delta(A_1)+A_2$ (Fig. 18.17, p. 552). **LU** — logarithmic first (capturing the discontinuity), uniform inside each resulting subinterval; this is the generic way to give logarithmic segmentation the memory/arithmetic knob it lacks, because the values $\alpha_2(A_1)$ *are* the knob. Some functions prefer UU, some LU — but those experiments used equal-size multipliers, strictly worse than computing just right, so a careful reimplementation is still owed.

### Arbitrary dichotomy-based segmentation

Drop the structure entirely: partition $I$ so that, at fixed degree, the approximation error is *balanced* across subintervals [SNB05; CK12; Tho15; KL14]. Best possible for approximation, worst for architecture — finding $A$ now needs a dichotomic search: a comparator tree ([[2.2) Comparators]]), a chain of table lookups, or a polynomial threaded through the splitting points [KL14]. Restricting the search to the top $\alpha$ bits of $X$ makes it cheap, at the price of errors that are only *comparable* rather than equal — fine within a factor of 2. The dichotomy also emits the midpoint $M_A$, so $Y=X-M_A$ costs one subtraction and $w_X-\alpha<w_Y<w_X$ (Fig. 18.18, p. 553).

### Which one

A **universal** approximator needs all four: for each there is a function it wins on, and they differ in the latency/area and memory/logic mix. For a **known** function, use its algebra first (p. 553): $\sqrt{2^{2p}x}=2^{p}\sqrt x$ turns a fixed-point input into a quasi-floating-point $X=2^{2p}Y$ via an LZC and shifter, after which two polynomials cover the two rightmost segments — a specialised hierarchical scheme, far better than anything generic. Functions with no usable identity (most inverse cumulative distributions [Che+07]) are exactly what the generic machinery is for.

## Horner Evaluation

Factor the developed form by repeatedly applying **distributivity of $\times$ over $+$ in reverse** — pulling the common factor $Y$ out of every term above the constant:

$$\begin{aligned}p(Y)&=C_0+C_1Y+C_2Y^2+\dots+C_dY^d\\ &=C_0+Y\left(C_1+C_2Y+\dots+C_dY^{d-1}\right)\\ &=C_0+Y\left(C_1+Y\left(C_2+\dots+Y\,C_d\right)\right).\end{aligned}\tag{18.19}$$

$d$ multiplications and $d$ additions — provably minimal in multiplications for a generic degree-$d$ polynomial, against $d(d+1)/2$ for the naive developed form. The price is that the factorisation is a **chain**: stage $i$ cannot start before stage $i+1$ finishes, so latency is $\Theta(d)$ multiply-add delays.

As a recurrence (18.20, p. 555):

$$S_i=C_i+Y\,S_{i+1}\ \ (i<d),\qquad S_d=C_d,\qquad p(Y)=S_0 .$$

### Why every stage gets its own widths

Computing $S_i$ exactly needs a growing format, which is exactly what "computing just right" forbids. Introduce two per-stage parameters: $\ell_i^{S}$, the LSB to which $\widetilde S_i$ is quantized, and $\ell_i^{Y}$, the LSB to which the multiplier's $Y$ input is truncated (18.21):

$$\widetilde S_i=\left[\,C_i+\widetilde Y_i\times\widetilde S_{i+1}\,\right]_{\ell_i^{S}} .$$

Decompose the cumulated error $\delta_{\text{eval},i}=\widetilde S_i-S_i$ by inserting and cancelling intermediates (the standard trick of [[1.2) Error Accuracy]]) — 18.23 becomes

$$\delta_{\text{eval},i}=\underbrace{\left[C_i+\widetilde Y_i\widetilde S_{i+1}\right]_{\ell_i^{S}}-\left(C_i+\widetilde Y_i\widetilde S_{i+1}\right)}_{\delta_{\text{multadd},i}}\;+\;\underbrace{(\widetilde Y_i-Y)}_{\delta_{Y,i}}\times\widetilde S_{i+1}\;+\;Y\cdot\delta_{\text{eval},i+1}.\tag{18.24}$$

Three facts fall out.

**(i) The addition is exact.** $C_i$ already has LSB $\ell_i\ge\ell_i^{S}$ — otherwise the table is storing bits nobody uses — so $\delta_{\text{multadd},i}$ is purely the multiplier's rounding: $\bar\delta_{\text{multadd},i}=2^{\ell_i^{S}}$ for truncation, $2^{\ell_i^{S}-1}$ for rounding (18.26).

**(ii) The recursion does not amplify.** $|Y|\le1$ by normalization, so the third term is bounded by $\bar\delta_{\text{eval},i+1}$ and, with $\bar\delta_{\text{eval},d}=0$ since $\widetilde S_d=C_d$ exactly, the triangle inequality unrolls to a plain **sum of $2d$ terms** (18.28–18.29):

$$\bar\delta_{\text{eval}}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\left(\bar\delta_{\text{multadd},i}+\bar S_{i+1}\,\bar\delta_{Y,i}\right).$$

**(iii) The $Y$ truncation is *amplified by $\bar S_{i+1}$, not by 1*** — and that is the whole game. $\bar S_{i+1}$ is a bound on the partial Horner sum, which by $|Y|\le1$ is $\bar S_{i}\le|C_i|+\bar S_{i+1}$, i.e. dominated by $|C_i|$. But after a range reduction of parameter $\alpha$, $C_i\approx\frac{f^{(i)}}{i!}2^{-i\alpha}$: **the coefficients decay by about $\alpha$ bits per degree.** Balancing the two terms of each summand, $\bar S_{i+1}\bar\delta_{Y,i}\approx\bar\delta_{\text{multadd},i}$, gives Algorithm 18.2's line

$$\ell_i^{Y}=\max\!\left(\ell^{S}-m_{\bar S_{i+1}},\ \ell_Y\right)\approx\ell^{S}+(i+1)\alpha .$$

So the deeper stages see *fewer bits of $Y$*, and their accumulators are narrower on the MSB side too ($m_i\approx m_0-i\alpha$). Both multiplier operands shrink by roughly $\alpha$ bits per level. Since $\alpha(d+1)\approx w_R$, the topmost stage handles operands of about $2\alpha$ bits while the last stage — where $\widetilde Y_0=Y$ exactly, no truncation, and anything else means the evaluator is fed bits it never uses — is full width. Measured for $\log_2(1+X)$, $w_X=w_R=24$, $d=3$, $\alpha=5$:

| stage $i$ | 2 | 1 | 0 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $\widetilde S_i$ width | 19 | 25 | 29 |
| bits of $Y$ used | 12 | 18 | 19 (all) |
| $\approx$ multiplier area (bit$^2$) | 228 | 450 | 551 |

$1229$ against $3\times551=1653$ for an evaluator with uniform-width multipliers — **26% of the multiplier area saved by truncation alone**, and the saving grows with $d$. This, not the multiplication count, is why Horner is cheap.

The sizing loop (Algorithm 18.2, p. 558) is a search on a single $\ell_S$: start at $\ell_p$, evaluate the $2d$-term sum, decrement until $\bar\delta_{\text{eval}}<\bar\delta^{\text{target}}_{\text{eval}}$. Sharing one $\ell_S$ across all stages is a heuristic — a bit is usually recoverable per stage by relaxing it. For a piecewise approximation, run it per subinterval and take the worst case (max of MSBs, min of LSBs). Note that Algorithm 18.2 uses $\bar S_i$ where (18.29) has $\bar S_{i+1}$; the algorithm is the conservative one.

## Parallel Evaluation

Evaluate the developed form instead (18.30). In software that is $d(d+1)/2$ multiplications — worse. In hardware it is better, because *depth*, not operation count, is the currency: compute $Y^{2^k}$ in sequence for $2^k\le d$, combine by the binary decomposition of each $i$ to get all $Y^i$ in $O(\log d)$, and sum the $d+1$ products in an adder tree of depth $O(\log d)$. **$O(\log d)$ against Horner's $O(d)$, at the cost of more multipliers.** Estrin's scheme is the recursive version of the odd/even split $p(x)=p_e(x^2)+x\,p_o(x^2)$.

Three hardware observations make it more than a latency trick (p. 559):

- $|C_i|$ decays with $i$, so $\widetilde Y_i^{\,i}$ may be quantized to the same width as $C_i$ — the powering is cheap exactly where it is used most.
- For large $i$, $\widetilde Y_i$ is narrow enough that **tabulating** $\widetilde Y_i^{\,i}$ is attractive, and it reads in parallel with the coefficient table that is already there.
- Every addition merges into one **bit heap** ([[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]]): expand $C_iY^i=\left(\sum_j2^jc_{i,j}\right)\left(\sum_k2^ky_k\right)^i$ at the bit level and discard everything whose weighted sum stays under $2^{\ell_R}$. Fig. 18.21 (p. 561) gives three realisations of each $\left[C_ix^i\right]$ box: faithful powering unit plus truncated multiplier, tabulated rounded power plus truncated multiplier, or a fused partial-product generator ([[2.4) Multipliers]], [[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]]). Which is best is open. Free bonus: $x^i$ has the parity of $i$, so for even $i$ the sign bit of $Y$ is ignorable and for odd $i$ negation is XOR-based, as in multipartite.

Error analysis is *simpler* than Horner's — no recurrence, one parameter (the bit-heap LSB) — but architecture-dependent, and the book leaves it as an exercise. All published parallel evaluators target 24 bits with uniform segmentation and stop at degree 3: [PBM01] (degree 2, truncated squarer, merged compressor tree, for $X^p$); [OS05] (degree 2, multifunction, not faithful for all); HOTBM [DD05] (any degree — $P_i=\left[Y^i\right]$ split into subwords $P_{k,i}$, products $C_i(A)P_{k,i}$ tabulated against a *truncated* $A$, which works only because of the coefficient smoothness above and costs an extra error term); [Che15] (degree 3 Taylor, $Y^3$ tabulated, $Y^2$ by truncated squarer — [[3.3) Squarers and Integer Powers]]); [De+17] (ILP, degrees 1–2, correctly rounded).

## Other Evaluation Techniques

Refactoring can push a degree-$\ge3$ polynomial below $d$ multiplications [Mul16 §3.8; Rev09 §5.2], but it *changes the coefficients*, which injects a new error term, may demand more accurate coefficients to start with, and has unclear interaction with range reduction (p. 562).

The one that survives contact with hardware is **SquareRich** [XFM14]: complete the square,

$$C_0+x(C_1+xC_2)=C_2\left((x+m)^2+n\right),\quad m=\frac{C_1}{2C_2},\ n=\frac{C_0}{C_2}-\frac{C_1^2}{4C_2^2}\tag{18.31}$$

trading one multiplier for a squarer, which is cheaper ([[3.3) Squarers and Integer Powers]]). The catch is that after a uniform reduction $C_2\ll C_1$, so $m=C_1/2C_2\gg x$: the squarer is on a much wider word than the multiplier it replaced. Gains are still reported up to degree 6.

## Putting It All Together

### The error budget

$$\delta_{\text{total}}(X)=R(X)-f(X)=\underbrace{R-R_{\text{ext}}}_{\delta_{\text{final round}}}+\underbrace{R_{\text{ext}}-p_A(Y)}_{\delta_{\text{eval}}}+\underbrace{p_A(Y)-f(X)}_{\delta_{\text{approx}}}\tag{18.33}$$

with **no range-reduction term**, because (18.3)–(18.6) are exact, and **no coefficient-quantization term**, because it was folded into $\delta_{\text{approx}}$ above. Faithful rounding demands $\bar\delta_{\text{total}}<2^{\ell_R}$, i.e. by the triangle inequality

$$\bar\delta_{\text{final round}}+\bar\delta_{\text{eval}}+\bar\delta_{\text{approx}}<2^{\ell_R}.$$

The architecture computes $R_{\text{ext}}$ slightly wider than $\ell_R$ and truncates, so $\bar\delta_{\text{final round}}=2^{\ell_R-1}$ and it is **free**: pre-add a half-ulp into $C_0$ in the table and truncation *is* round-to-nearest. The a-priori split is therefore $2^{\ell_R-1}$ / $2^{\ell_R-2}$ / $2^{\ell_R-2}$, and once Algorithm 18.1 reports the *actual* $\bar\delta_{\text{approx}}$ the evaluator gets the slack back:

$$\bar\delta^{\text{target}}_{\text{eval}}=2^{\ell_R-1}-\bar\delta_{\text{approx}}.\tag{18.36}$$

That number is the input to Algorithm 18.2 — the one equation joining the chapter's three halves.

### Two generators, and one ILP

**Without range reduction** (Fig. 18.22, p. 564): no choice at all. Find the minimal degree reaching $\ell_R$ over the whole interval (Sollya `guessdegree`), call `BasicPolyApprox`, compute (18.36), hand it to `FixHornerEvaluator`. FloPoCo's `FixFunctionBySimplePoly`.

**With uniform piecewise reduction** (Fig. 18.23, p. 565): $d$ is the user's knob, $\alpha$ is found by trial and error upward, and each trial is quickly rejected by testing only the two **extremal** subintervals ($A=0$ and $A=2^{\alpha}-1$), where the error is almost always maximal. Then one shared $\ell_p$, then per-subinterval evaluation budgets, then worst-case formats. FloPoCo's `FixFunctionByPiecewisePoly`.

**Jointly, by ILP** [De+17] (pp. 566–568). Separating approximation from evaluation costs bits, because each stage is dimensioned against a *worst case* that rarely occurs. Make the coefficient bits themselves the variables, $C_i=\sum_{j}2^jc_{i,j}$, so

$$R_{\text{ext}}(Y)=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\sum_{j}2^{j}Y^{i}c_{i,j}\tag{18.38}$$

is **linear in the unknowns** for each fixed $Y$. Correct rounding is then two linear constraints per input value,

$$\lfloor f(X)\rfloor_{\ell_R}\le R_{\text{ext}}(Y)<\lfloor f(X)\rfloor_{\ell_R}+2^{\ell_R},\tag{18.39}$$

scaled to integers by a power of two — one independent ILP per subinterval, $2\cdot2^{w_X-\alpha}$ constraints each. Truncated products come for free: $Y^i$ is a *constant* in each constraint, so $\widetilde{C_iP_i}=\sum_j2^j\lfloor P_i\rfloor_{\ell-j}c_{i,j}$ is still linear, and any hardware approximation to $Y^i$ can be modelled exactly as long as it is reported faithfully. The objective introduces $\mu_{i,k}=1$ iff $c_{i,j}=0$ for all $j\in\{j_{\min,i},\dots,j_{\min,i}+k\}$ and maximizes $\sum\mu_{i,k}$ — "force low bits to zero", weightable by hardware cost. Two passes ($C_1$ then $C_2$) beat one, for the same reason the shared-$\ell_p$ trick works. Result: **correct** rounding rather than faithful, at up to 24 bits with $\alpha=7$, and a few bits shaved off each coefficient versus the worst-case generator ([[7.2) ILP for Arithmetic Design]]).

## Floating-Point Architectures Built on Fixed-Point Polynomials

Every efficient floating-point elementary function [PBM01; OS05; LP13; Che15; JP20] is a fixed-point polynomial core plus exponent handling ([[2.7) Floating-Point Operators]], [[3.4) Floating-Point Specialization]]). Two structural gifts (p. 568):

- A floating-point input hands you a **free logarithmic segmentation**: its exponent *is* a crude $\log_2X$. Refine it by feeding a few leading significand bits into a tiny LUT — this is how the probit function is done on FPGAs [JP20].
- [Tho15]'s universal approximator splits the floating-point domain so that neither the input nor its image changes exponent within a subdomain — which **reduces the problem to fixed point**. Subdomains are split further until $f$ is monotone and faithfully approximable by degree $d$; the table stores the coefficients *and* the output exponent; the segmentation is arbitrary and located by dichotomy; one Horner evaluator serves all polynomials. $d$ is again the memory/arithmetic knob.

## On FPGAs

The resource classes map cleanly, which is why this family suits FPGAs ([[1.3) FPGAs]]):

- **Coefficient table → block RAM.** $2^{\alpha}$ words of $\sum_i(m_i-\ell_p)$ bits is a wide, shallow ROM — the natural BRAM shape, and a degree-2 or -3 design at 24 bits fits in one or two. It is the *only* exponential resource, so $\alpha$ is chosen to fit the BRAM you have.
- **Multipliers → DSP blocks.** $d$ of them, of *decreasing* width, so the mapping is asymmetric: deep stages fit in LUT logic while the last one takes the DSP ([[2.4) Multipliers]]). Truncated multipliers are the natural fit — the error analysis above already assumes truncation.
- **Additions → bit heap.** Horner's additions, the LDTC compression adders, and (in parallel evaluation) the whole summation collapse into one compressor tree ([[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]]).
- **Logarithmic segmentation** additionally needs an LZC and a barrel shifter ([[2.6) Shifters]]) — cheap in LUTs, and the reason LU hierarchies are practical.

This is the comparison Chap. 17 deferred. A degree-1 evaluator is *exactly* multipartite's equal on table bits ($7936$ vs $7808$) — no surprise, since **multipartite is a degree-1 polynomial evaluator in which the product $C_1(A)\cdot Y$ has been replaced by tables of its partial products**. Both are stuck in the $2^{w/2}$ regime. Degree 2 is what breaks out of it: $5.5\times$ less memory than the best multipartite, for two multipliers.

**Pick multipartite** when $w_X\lesssim16$ and multipliers are precious — multiplier-free, shallow, airtight error analysis.
**Pick degree 2–3 with uniform segmentation** for $w_X$ between 16 and 32: the table collapses to a single BRAM and DSP blocks are usually idle. This is the default.
**Pick logarithmic or LU segmentation** only when the function misbehaves at an endpoint — discontinuity, infinite derivative, or a derivative varying by orders of magnitude across $I$; you pay an LZC and a shifter and lose the clean $w_Y=w_X-\alpha$.
**Pick dichotomy-based segmentation** when the generator must swallow arbitrary user functions and you can afford the search hardware. **Pick parallel evaluation over Horner** only when latency binds; $d\le3$ in practice, and merge everything into one bit heap.

## Worked Example

$f(X)=\log_2(1+X)$, $w_X=w_R=12$, $\ell_R=-12$, faithful. Budget: $2^{-13}$ final rounding, $2^{-14}$ approximation, remainder to evaluation.

**1. Segmentation.** Law 2 with $d=2$: $\alpha=2$ gives $2^{-12.55}$ (fails $2^{-14}$), $\alpha=3$ gives $2^{-15.31}$. Take $\alpha=3$, $d=2$, so $Y$ is $w_Y=12-3=9$ bits.

**2. Coefficient formats.** Over the 8 subintervals, $\max_A|C_0|=0.9069$, $\max|C_1|=0.1799$, $\max|C_2|=0.01001$ $\Rightarrow m=(-1,-3,-7)$. All $C_2$ are negative — constant sign, so the sign bit is hardwired, not stored. Algorithm 18.1 starts at $\ell_p=\lceil-14\rceil+2=-12$ and decrements to $\ell_p=-15$, where the worst quantized error is $2^{-14.86}<2^{-14}$. Table: $8\times(15+13+9)=8\times37=\mathbf{296\text{ bits}}$.

**3. One subinterval,** $A=1$, i.e. $X\in[\tfrac18,\tfrac14)$. Remez gives $C=(0.169942573,\ 0.159974587,\ -0.008006636)$; rounded to $\ell_p=-15$ (integers $5569$, $5242$, $-262$):

$$\begin{aligned}\widetilde C_0&=\texttt{0.001010111000001}_2=0.169952393\\ \widetilde C_1&=\texttt{0.001010001111010}_2=0.159973145\\ \widetilde C_2&=-\texttt{0.000000100000110}_2=-0.007995605\end{aligned}$$

**4. Evaluator sizing.** $\bar S_2=0.01001\Rightarrow m_{\bar S_2}=-7$; $\bar S_1=0.18989\Rightarrow m_{\bar S_1}=-3$. Algorithm 18.2 with $\bar\delta^{\text{target}}_{\text{eval}}=2^{-13}-2^{-14.86}=2^{-13.47}$ lands on $\ell^{S}=-16$ and $\ell_1^{Y}=\max(-16+7,-9)=-9$: at 12 bits and degree 2 the $Y$ truncation is already saturated at full width. Bound: $\bar\delta_{\text{eval}}=2\cdot2^{-16}+\bar S_2\cdot0=2^{-15}$. ✓

**5. Evaluate** at $Y=\texttt{101101001}_2=0.705078125$, i.e. $X=\texttt{.001101101001}_2=0.213134766$.

$$\begin{aligned}\widetilde S_2&=\widetilde C_2=-0.007995605\\ \widetilde Y_1\times\widetilde S_2&=0.705078125\times(-0.007995605)=-0.005637443\\ \widetilde S_1&=\left[0.159973145-0.005637443\right]_{-16}=\left[0.154335618\right]_{-16}=0.154327393\\ Y\times\widetilde S_1&=0.108810424\\ \widetilde S_0=R_{\text{ext}}&=\left[0.169952393+0.108810424\right]_{-16}=0.278762817\end{aligned}$$

**6. Round.** The half-ulp $2^{-13}$ was pre-added into $\widetilde C_0$, so truncating $R_{\text{ext}}$ to $\ell_R=-12$ *is* round-to-nearest:

$$R=\texttt{.010001110110}_2=0.278808594,\qquad f(X)=0.278739827 .$$

**7. Error tally,** in ulp ($1\,\text{ulp}=2^{-12}$):

| contribution | this point | bound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\delta_{\text{approx}}$ (incl. coefficient quantization) | $+0.128$ | $0.138$ |
| $\delta_{\text{eval}}$ (two truncations to $\ell^{S}=-16$) | $-0.034$ | $0.125$ |
| $\delta_{\text{final round}}$ | $+0.188$ | $0.500$ |
| **total** | $\mathbf{+0.282}$ | $\mathbf{0.763<1}$ |

Faithful, with $0.24$ ulp of slack — which is what you would spend by dropping to $\ell_p=-14$ or $\ell^{S}=-15$ and re-checking, exactly the loop Algorithms 18.1 and 18.2 automate.
