## Specification

**Inputs:** $n$ level-sensitive lines $x$, or a bundled $k$-bit datum with a $\{\text{req},\text{ack}\}$ pair.
**Outputs:** $m$ lines $z$, plus $s$ *secondary* variables $y$ that are fed back into the same combinational network.
**Correctness promise (this is not arithmetic — there is no ulp):** every allowed input change drives the circuit to the *unique* next state named by the flow table, for **arbitrary positive gate and wire delays**. That means: no static hazard on any excitation, no critical race, no essential hazard.
**Optimised for:** average-case latency and dynamic energy — the circuit finishes when it is done, not when a clock edge says it may be done. Paid for in area, EMI predictability, and design effort.

Every choice below traces back to that one correctness promise. In [[0.4) Synchronous Sequential Logic]] the clock buys it for you: the period is set to the worst-case path, every glitch has decayed before the edge, and the only question left is setup/hold. Remove the clock and *all* of that becomes your problem.

*The Huffman model — the standard textbook drawing of a feedback sequential circuit.*

The delay boxes $\Delta_i$ are **not components you place**; they are the lumped propagation delay already present in the loop. Drawing them explicitly is what makes the analysis possible.

The circuit's behaviour is one fixed-point equation:

$$Y = f(x,y),\qquad y_i(t+\Delta_i)=Y_i(t)$$

A **total state** is the pair $(x,y)$. It is **stable** iff

$$y = f(x,y)$$

and **unstable** otherwise, in which case the $y_i$ with $y_i\ne Y_i$ start moving. Since the $\Delta_i$ are unknown and unequal, the *order* in which they arrive is unknown. Every pathology in this note is a consequence of that single sentence.

The state count sets a floor on $s$:

$$s \ \ge\ \lceil \log_2 k\rceil \quad\text{for } k \text{ flow-table rows}$$

but as [[#Races and critical races]] shows, that floor is usually not achievable — race freedom is a *graph-embedding* constraint, not a counting one.

## Why anyone gives up a clock

A synchronous stage costs $T_{\text{clk}} \ge t_{pd,\max} + t_{\text{setup}} + t_{\text{skew}}$ every cycle, whatever the data. Three things follow.

**Latency tracks actual delay, not worst case.** A ripple-carry adder's carry chain is $O(n)$ deep only for the operand pairs that actually propagate a carry the full width; the average carry propagation length is $O(\log n)$. A self-timed adder finishes in $\sum_i t_i$ instead of $N\,t_{\max}$:

$$T_{\text{sync}} = N\,t_{\max}, \qquad \mathbb{E}\!\left[T_{\text{async}}\right] = N\,\mathbb{E}[t]$$

> [!important] The average-case win evaporates at every join
> The $N\,\mathbb{E}[t]$ figure holds for a *chain*. Put $N$ independent units in parallel and wait for all of them and you get $\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_i t_i\right]$, which converges to $t_{\max}$ as $N$ grows. Any design with wide parallelism and frequent synchronisation barriers — which is most of them — recovers almost none of the average-case advantage. This is the honest reason async never displaced synchronous in datapaths, and it is a statistics result, not an engineering failure.

**No clock distribution power.** The clock tree in a large synchronous chip is a full-swing, full-fanout net toggling every cycle; it routinely accounts for 20–40% of dynamic power. An async circuit has no such net, and quiescent blocks draw only leakage without needing clock gating logic. This is why the technique survives in duty-cycled sensor front ends.

**No skew.** Skew is a *global* constraint: it must be met between every pair of registers in a domain, which is why clock-tree synthesis is its own tool. Async replaces it with *local* constraints — one matched delay per stage, or none at all.

Secondary but real: no clock means no sharp spectral line at $f_{\text{clk}}$ and its harmonics. Async switching noise is spread, which matters both for EMI compliance and for resistance to power-analysis side-channel attacks.

## Fundamental mode

The whole classical theory rests on one restriction:

> **Fundamental-mode assumption.** Inputs change **one variable at a time**, and no further input change occurs until the circuit has settled into a stable total state.

Two separate promises hidden in one sentence:

1. **Single-input-change (SIC).** Never analyse two simultaneous input changes. Without this, the target state is genuinely ambiguous — a machine that goes $A\to B$ on $x_1$ and $A\to C$ on $x_2$ has no defined answer for both at once, and the physical circuit will pick by delay.
2. **Settle before the next change.** This is what bounds the input rate. It is the async analogue of the clock period, except it is a constraint on the *environment*, not on the circuit.

SIC is why hazard analysis in [[0.2) Combinational Logic]] is tractable at all: the static-hazard taxonomy there is defined per single-variable transition. Under multiple-input change you must additionally worry about *function hazards*, which no cover can remove.

Relaxing fundamental mode gives **input–output mode** (the environment must only wait for the *outputs* to respond, not the internal state) and eventually the fully unrestricted **burst mode** used by real async synthesis tools. Each relaxation costs another layer of analysis.

## Flow tables

A **flow table** is a state table with input *combinations* as columns instead of a clock edge. Each cell holds a next state and an output; a cell whose next state equals its row is **stable** and is conventionally circled (bold here).

A **primitive flow table** has **exactly one stable state per row**. You build it directly from the word specification, giving every distinct (input, history) situation its own row, without any cleverness. It is always correct and always far larger than necessary.

Reduction proceeds by merging rows, and the merging rule is *not* the synchronous equivalence rule:

- Two rows are **compatible** if, in every column, their entries do not conflict — same next state, or at least one unspecified. Outputs must also not conflict wherever both are specified.
- Compatibility is **not transitive**, so you cannot just partition. You build a **merger diagram** (vertices = rows, edges = compatible pairs), find the **maximal compatibles** (maximal cliques), and then choose a **minimal closed cover**: a set of compatibles that covers every row *and* is closed, i.e. the implied next-state pairs of every chosen compatible are themselves contained in some chosen compatible.

The closure condition is the part that has no synchronous analogue and the part everyone forgets. Merging without checking closure produces a table that is one merge short of consistent and fails only on a specific input sequence.

Unspecified entries are the real currency here: the more don't-cares the primitive table has (states the environment promises never to reach), the more rows merge. That is why the primitive table is written from the spec *including* the "cannot happen" cases marked as such.

## Races and critical races

A **race** occurs when an input change makes two or more secondary variables unstable at once. Formally, from stable $(x,y)$, let $x$ change to $x'$ and let

$$D=\{\,i \;:\; y_i \ne f_i(x',y)\,\}$$

If $\lvert D\rvert \ge 2$, the variables in $D$ change in an order set by the $\Delta_i$, and the circuit passes through an intermediate code that was never in the flow table.

- **Non-critical race:** every possible order converges on the same final stable state. Harmless, though it may add transitions and glitch the output.
- **Critical race:** at least two orders end in *different* stable states. The circuit's function now depends on manufacturing spread and temperature. This is a hard functional bug, not a timing margin.

The cure is structural. A transition $S_i \to S_j$ is race-free iff

$$d_H\big(\text{code}(S_i),\,\text{code}(S_j)\big)=1$$

so a race-free assignment exists exactly when the flow table's **transition graph embeds as a subgraph of the $s$-cube $Q_s$**. That is a much stronger demand than $2^s \ge k$: $Q_s$ has only $s\,2^{s-1}$ edges and is bipartite, so any odd cycle in the transition graph is immediately unembeddable at that $s$.

When the graph does not embed, three standard escapes:

1. **Add state variables.** More dimensions, more edges. Universal schemes exist (Huffman's multiple-row, Liu's shared-row, Tracey's single-transition-time assignment); they trade $s$ for guaranteed race freedom on any table.
2. **Route through unused codes** — a **cycle**, below. Costs transition time, not variables.
3. **Duplicate a row** so one logical state has two codes, each adjacent to a different neighbour.

> [!note] Gray-coded FIFO pointers are this exact theorem
> The one place every RTL engineer meets race-free assignment is the clock-domain-crossing FIFO. The read/write pointers are Gray coded so that a pointer sampled *while it is changing* differs from a valid value in at most one bit — the receiver either sees the old count or the new one, never a code that was never written. That is $d_H = 1$ doing the same job as it does in a flow table.

## Essential hazards

An essential hazard is not a hazard of the *circuit* — it is a property of the *flow table*, and no amount of logic can remove it.

> **Unger's test.** A flow table has an essential hazard at stable state $S$ for input variable $x_i$ if the state reached after **one** change of $x_i$ differs from the state reached after **three** successive changes of $x_i$.

The physical mechanism: the input $x_i$ reaches different gates by paths of different length (very commonly, $x_i$ arrives directly at one gate and through an inverter at another). A secondary variable $y_j$ can change and propagate to a gate *before* the late copy of $x_i$ gets there. That gate then sees the pair (new $y$, old $x$) — exactly the combination that a *second* change of $x_i$ would produce. The circuit runs ahead through the sequence, ending where three changes would have taken it.

Why logic cannot fix it: static and dynamic hazards live in the *cover* — a consensus term from [[0.1) Boolean Algebra]] closes the gap between two adjacent implicants and the glitch is gone. An essential hazard is present in **every** implementation of the table, minimal or not, because it is derived from the table's transition sequence alone. The only cure is **delay**:

$$\Delta_{\text{feedback}} \;>\; \max_{\text{gates } g}\big(t_{x_i \to g}\big) - \min_{\text{gates } g}\big(t_{x_i \to g}\big)$$

Put a deliberate delay element in the feedback path large enough that no state change can outrun the slowest copy of the input. This is the one place in classical async design where you are *required* to build a delay, and it is why an async design's correctness ends up written into placement constraints rather than into RTL.

The canonical carrier is the toggle element:

| present | $x=0$ | $x=1$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **1** | 2 |
| 2 | 3 | **2** |
| 3 | **3** | 4 |
| 4 | 1 | **4** |

From stable 1 with $x{:}\,0\to1$: one change lands in **2**; three changes land in **4**. Different — essential hazard. Every edge-triggered flip-flop contains one, which is exactly why flip-flop cells carry internal delay constraints and are never re-synthesised from their own truth tables. See [[0.3) Introduction to Flip-Flops & Latches]] and [[2.8) Counters#Asynchronous Counters]].

## Cycles

A **cycle** is a deliberately chained sequence of unstable states inside a single input column: $S_a \to S_b \to S_c$, all in column $x$, ending at a stable $S_c$. Each step is unit-distance, so the whole multi-bit move happens with no race at all — the cost is that the transition takes several gate delays instead of one, and intermediate outputs appear on $z$ unless the output assignment is chosen to keep them constant along the cycle.

Cycles are the standard tool for escape route 2 above: route a distance-2 transition through an unused code.

The failure mode is a cycle with **no stable state** in it. The circuit then oscillates at the loop's natural frequency for as long as that input holds — a ring oscillator you did not ask for. Detect it by checking that every column's unstable-entry graph is acyclic. (Occasionally you *want* it: that is how a clock-less pulse generator is built.)

*Each C-element takes the request from its left neighbour and the inverted state of its right neighbour; request and acknowledge are 1 bit each, data is $k$ bits wide.*

**Bundled data.** Keep an ordinary single-rail $k$-bit datapath, add two 1-bit control wires. The protocol's timing assumption is a **matched delay line** in the request path:

$$t_{\text{matched}} \;\ge\; t_{pd,\max}(\text{datapath}) + t_{\text{setup}}$$

which is literally the synchronous period constraint made *local* to one stage. It is the same worst-case number — the win is that only *this* stage pays it, and only when this stage is used.

- **Four-phase (return-to-zero, level signalling):** `req↑ → ack↑ → req↓ → ack↓`. Wires are level-sensitive, so latch control is a wire and the logic is cheap. Cost: two round trips of wire delay per datum, and the return-to-zero half does no useful work.
- **Two-phase (transition signalling, NRZ):** every *transition* on `req` or `ack`, either direction, is an event. Half the transitions, so half the wire energy — attractive on long links. Cost: storage must be *event*-sensitive (capture-pass latches, double-edge-triggered elements, XOR-based toggle logic), which is materially larger and slower than a level-sensitive latch. Two-phase wins when the wire dominates and loses when the logic does.

**Dual-rail.** Drop the timing assumption entirely by encoding validity into the data. Each bit $b$ gets two wires:

| $b.0\,b.1$ | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| $00$ | spacer / empty |
| $10$ | logic 0 |
| $01$ | logic 1 |
| $11$ | illegal |

Data validity is now self-announcing, so **completion detection** is a function of the data alone:

$$\text{done} \;=\; \bigwedge_{i=0}^{k-1}\big(b_{i.0}\vee b_{i.1}\big)$$

built as an OR per bit into a tree. In four-phase dual-rail the tree is built from **C-elements** rather than ANDs, so that the "all empty" detection is symmetric and the return-to-spacer phase is also acknowledged. Costs: $2k$ wires, roughly double the datapath area, and the return-to-spacer phase means every bit toggles on every datum regardless of the data — dual-rail's switching activity is *data-independent*, which is bad for power and excellent for side-channel resistance. **1-of-4 encoding** carries 2 bits on 4 wires with exactly one wire toggling per 2 bits, halving transitions at the same wire count.

## Delay-insensitive and QDI

Order the timing-assumption hierarchy from strongest guarantee to weakest:

| Model | Assumes | Practical status |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **DI** (delay-insensitive) | nothing — arbitrary finite gate *and* wire delays | provably almost useless |
| **QDI** (quasi-delay-insensitive) | DI plus **isochronic forks** | the real target |
| **SI** (speed-independent) | arbitrary gate delay, **zero** wire delay | Muller's original model |
| **bundled data** | a matched delay bound per stage | what gets built |

Martin's result is the pivot: a circuit built from single-output gates and correct under arbitrary gate *and wire* delays can compute essentially nothing beyond what C-elements and inverters give you. Pure DI is a dead end. **QDI** buys back the whole design space with the weakest known repair — the **isochronic fork**: when a wire forks, the delay difference between the branches is assumed negligible compared to gate delays. Acknowledge one branch and you have acknowledged both. That single assumption is the entire timing-closure burden of a QDI design.

**The Muller C-element** is the primitive that makes any of this work. It is the rendezvous operator: output goes to 1 when *all* inputs are 1, goes to 0 when *all* are 0, and **holds** otherwise. For two inputs,

$$Y \;=\; ab + (a+b)\,y \;=\; \mathrm{maj}(a,b,y)$$

Expanding by distributivity of AND over OR gives $Y = ab + ay + by$ — the **complete sum**, all three prime implicants. That is not an accident of algebra: it is precisely the consensus-completed cover from [[0.1) Boolean Algebra]], and it is what makes the C-element free of the static-1 hazard that $ab + ay$ alone would have. Drop $by$ to save a gate and the element glitches on the $a{:}1\to0$ transition while $b=y=1$. Every synthesis tool wants to drop it.

A **Muller pipeline** is nothing but a chain of these: stage $i$'s C-element takes the output of stage $i-1$ and the *inverted* output of stage $i+1$. A stage may advance only when its predecessor has data and its successor has emptied. The chain is self-initialising, deadlock-free, and correct for any gate delays — no matched delays anywhere. Its occupancy is the price: in four-phase, a full-then-empty alternation means $2N$ stages hold $N$ tokens, so half the pipeline is bubbles at peak throughput. Hang latches off the C-element outputs and you have Sutherland's micropipeline; put dual-rail function blocks between them and you have a QDI datapath.

## FPGA mapping

Async is materially harder on FPGA than on ASIC, and for reasons that are all tooling rather than physics.

- **Combinational loops are a first-class error.** The feedback path of the Huffman model and the C-element's self-loop are both combinational cycles. Static timing analysis cannot traverse a loop, so the tools break it, warn, and then report timing that is meaningless. Vivado needs `set_property ALLOW_COMBINATORIAL_LOOPS TRUE` on the net plus a `set_disable_timing`; Quartus needs an equivalent loop cut. You are now hand-certifying the timing the tool refused to.
- **Consensus terms get optimised away.** The hazard-free cover is by construction *not* minimal. Every synthesiser's logic optimiser will remove the redundant product term, reintroducing the exact glitch you added it to remove. The fix is to stop synthesising and instantiate: an explicit `LUT3` primitive with the majority INIT mask, wrapped in `DONT_TOUCH`/`KEEP_HIERARCHY`. A C-element on a 6-LUT is one LUT and one loop.
- **Delay elements are not synthesisable.** A chain of inverters gets collapsed to a wire. What survives is either a carry-chain (`CARRY4`) delay line with a location constraint, or a LUT chain with `DONT_TOUCH` on every net. Either way the delay is fully PVT-dependent and unverifiable by the standard flow.
- **Isochronic forks are unenforceable.** FPGA routing delay dominates logic delay and is set by the router, not by you. There is no mechanism to constrain two branches of a fork to match. QDI's one timing assumption is therefore the one assumption FPGA routing will not honour, which is why FPGA async work is almost entirely bundled-data with a matched carry-chain delay and a `set_max_delay` constraint on the datapath.
- **What FPGAs actually run:** the CDC FIFO. Gray-coded pointers, two-flop synchronisers, and a full-/empty- flag comparison — a race-free unit-distance state assignment plus a metastability filter, and nothing else from this note. That is the honest scope of async on FPGA.

## Trade-offs

> [!warning] Not covered in the source — needs outside research
> This note has no assigned book source, so the table below is **relative and qualitative**. No LUT counts, gate delays, or measured energy figures are quoted, because inventing them would be worse than omitting them. Treat every cell as an ordering, not a measurement.

| | Synchronous | Bundled-data async | Dual-rail QDI |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Average latency** | worst case, always | actual per-stage delay | actual, plus completion-tree delay |
| **Worst-case latency** | $N\,t_{\max}$ | $\approx N\,t_{\max}$ + handshake overhead | worst, completion detection is on the critical path |
| **Area** | baseline | +control, +matched delay lines | ~2× datapath, + completion trees |
| **Dynamic power** | clock tree always toggles | no clock; idle blocks are free | no clock, but return-to-spacer forces full activity every datum |
| **EMI / spectrum** | sharp lines at $f_{\text{clk}}$ and harmonics | spread | spread, and data-independent (side-channel resistant) |
| **Timing closure** | one global STA run, mature | one matched-delay constraint per stage, manual | isochronic forks only; nothing else to close |
| **Testability** | scan + stuck-at, ~complete | scan is broken by the loops | very hard; hazards are not stuck-at faults |
| **Tool support** | the entire industry | fragmentary | research (Balsa, Haste/Tangram, Petrify, ACT) |

**Why synchronous won anyway.** Not speed, and not power — three things that never appear in a latency comparison:

1. **Static timing analysis.** One tool, one constraint set, one answer, no vectors. Async correctness depends on relative delays across paths that STA has no vocabulary for, so verification falls back to simulation with delay models — which is sampling, not proof.
2. **Testability.** Scan chains plus a stuck-at model give near-complete coverage for a synchronous design almost for free. Async has state-holding combinational loops that cannot be scanned, and its characteristic failures (a critical race, an essential hazard) are *not stuck-at faults* — they are delay-dependent behaviours that a passing stuck-at test set will not catch.
3. **Determinism.** A synchronous simulation is reproducible. An async one is not: hazards, races, and arbiter metastability make the result depend on the delay model. Every downstream methodology — regression, formal equivalence, coverage — assumes the determinism async does not have.

**Pick which, when.** Take **synchronous** unless you have a specific reason not to; the tooling advantage swamps everything else. Take **bundled-data async** when a block is duty-cycled hard and clock-tree power dominates, or when a stage's data-dependent delay spread is large enough that worst-case timing is genuinely wasteful. Take **dual-rail QDI** only when robustness to voltage/temperature is the requirement itself — near-threshold and subthreshold operation, where $t_{pd}$ varies by orders of magnitude and no matched delay line can track it — or when data-independent switching activity is a security requirement.

**Where async actually lives today.** Low-power sensor and RFID front ends, where the circuit is event-driven and idle almost always. **GALS** interfaces: any two independently clocked domains meet through an asynchronous FIFO, a mutex, or a pausible clock, because clock-domain crossing is *intrinsically* an async problem and no synchronous technique makes it go away. And **NoC links**, where a long inter-router wire is exactly the case that favours async — the link's latency is data- and distance-dependent, routers on either end need not share a clock, and two-phase signalling halves the transitions on the longest wires in the chip. See [[6.3) Network-On-Chip]].

## Worked example: a critical race and its repair

Four states $A,B,C,D$, two inputs $x_1x_2$, one output $z$. Stable entries in **bold**.

| present | $00$ | $01$ | $11$ | $10$ | $z$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $A$ | **A** | $B$ | $D$ | **A** | 0 |
| $B$ | $A$ | **B** | **B** | $C$ | 0 |
| $C$ | $B$ | $D$ | **C** | **C** | 1 |
| $D$ | $A$ | **D** | **D** | $C$ | 1 |

The transitions the table demands are $A\!\leftrightarrow\!B$, $B\!\to\!C$, $C\!\to\!D$, $C\!\to\!B$, $D\!\to\!C$, $D\!\to\!A$ — an adjacency graph that is the 4-cycle $A\!-\!B\!-\!C\!-\!D\!-\!A$.

### The bad assignment

Take the obvious binary count $A{=}00$, $B{=}01$, $C{=}10$, $D{=}11$. Two required edges are now distance 2: $B\!-\!C$ ($01\to10$) and $D\!-\!A$ ($11\to00$).

**Trace $B \to C$.** Start stable in $B$ in column $x_1x_2=11$, so $y_1y_2=01$. Let $x_2$ fall — a legal single input change — putting us in column $10$, where the table says $B\to C$. Excitation is $Y_1Y_2 = \text{code}(C) = 10$ against present $y_1y_2 = 01$: **both** variables are unstable.

| order | intermediate code | row entered | column $10$ entry | outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $y_1$ first | $11$ | $D$ | $C=10$ | $y_2$ falls → $10=C$ ✓ |
| $y_2$ first | $00$ | $A$ | **$A$ (stable)** | machine stops at $A$ ✗ |
| simultaneous | $10$ | $C$ | — | $C$ ✓ |

If $\Delta_2 < \Delta_1$ the circuit lands in $A$, which is **stable in column 10** — it does not recover when the delays settle, and it emits $z=0$ where the specification demands $z=1$. Two orders, two different final states: a **critical race**.

**Contrast, $D \to A$ in column $00$.** From $y_1y_2=11$ to $00$, also distance 2:

- $y_2$ first → $10 = C$; row $C$, column $00$ → $B = 01$; row $B$, column $00$ → $A = 00$. Settles at $A$.
- $y_1$ first → $01 = B$; row $B$, column $00$ → $A = 00$. Settles at $A$.

Both orders converge on $A$. This is a **non-critical race** — and the first path is a three-step **cycle** $D\to C\to B\to A$, all within column $00$. It is functionally correct but glitches $z$ from 1 to 0 through an intermediate that was never in the specification.

### The repair

Assign along the 4-cycle instead — Gray-adjacent:

$$A=00,\quad B=01,\quad C=11,\quad D=10$$

Now every required edge is an edge of $Q_2$:

| transition | codes | $d_H$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $A\to B$ | $00\to01$ | 1 |
| $B\to C$ | $01\to11$ | 1 |
| $C\to D$ | $11\to10$ | 1 |
| $D\to A$ | $10\to00$ | 1 |
| $C\to B$ | $11\to01$ | 1 |
| $D\to C$ | $10\to11$ | 1 |

**Re-trace $B\to C$.** Stable in $B$, $y_1y_2=01$, column $11$. $x_2$ falls to column $10$; excitation $Y_1Y_2 = \text{code}(C) = 11$. Only $y_1$ is unstable, so $\lvert D\rvert = 1$: no race by definition. $y_1$ rises after $\Delta_1$, the code becomes $11 = C$, and $C$ is stable in column $10$. One variable, one gate delay, one possible outcome — for any $\Delta_1,\Delta_2 > 0$.

**Re-trace $D\to A$.** $10 \to 00$: only $y_1$ changes. The three-step cycle is gone too, and with it the glitch on $z$ (both $D$ and the path now emit consistently).

### The excitation is not automatically hazard-free

Race freedom fixes the *state* assignment; the *cover* is a separate obligation. Mapping the table under the Gray assignment gives

$$Y_1 \;=\; y_1x_1 \;+\; y_1x_2 \;+\; \overline{y_2}\,x_1x_2 \;+\; y_2\,x_1\overline{x_2}$$

Check every pair of adjacent 1-cells: each such pair is covered by a *common* product term, so no single-variable transition passes through a moment where all terms are 0. This cover happens to be static-1-hazard-free. That is luck, not method — the general procedure is to include every prime implicant needed to cover each adjacent 1-pair, i.e. add consensus terms per [[0.1) Boolean Algebra]] until the cover is complete on all $1\!-\!1$ adjacencies, using the taxonomy of [[0.2) Combinational Logic]] to decide which transitions need checking. Then note that no logic-level fix exists for essential hazards, and go back and run Unger's three-change test on the table.
