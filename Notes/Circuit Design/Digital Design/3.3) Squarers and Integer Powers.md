**Specification.** Input $X \in \mathrm{ufix}(m,\ell)$ (or $\mathrm{sfix}(m,\ell)$), a single operand — there is no second input. Output $X^p$ for a positive integer $p$; exactly, the value fits in $\mathrm{ufix}\big(p(m+1),\,p\ell\big)$ for unsigned $X$. Accuracy is either **exact** or **faithful** ($<1$ ulp, see [[1.2) Error Accuracy]]) when the output is truncated. The quantity being minimised is **area**, measured as bits in the bit heap for ASIC and as LUTs + DSP blocks for FPGA; latency follows from heap height and is not the driver here.

Everything in this note falls out of one fact: because the two operands are literally the same signal, the partial-product array is symmetric, and a symmetric array can be folded.

## The symmetry collapse

Write $X$ as an $n$-bit integer (the fixed-point case is the same with a scale factor, so no generality is lost):

$$X=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{i}x_i,\qquad x_i\in\{0,1\}$$

Square it and expand the product of sums by **distributivity**:

$$X^{2}=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{i}x_i\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}2^{j}x_j\right)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}2^{i+j}\,x_ix_j$$

That double sum is exactly the $n^2$-entry partial-product array of a general $n\times n$ multiplier ([[2.4) Multipliers]]). Now **split the index set** into the diagonal and the two open triangles:

$$X^{2}=\underbrace{\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{2i}x_ix_i}_{\text{diagonal}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{0\le j<i<n}2^{i+j}x_ix_j}_{\text{below}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{0\le i<j<n}2^{i+j}x_ix_j}_{\text{above}}$$

Two Boolean facts finish it.

**Commutativity of AND**: $x_ix_j = x_jx_i$. The term above the diagonal at position $(i,j)$ and the term below at $(j,i)$ are the *same bit* and, since $i+j$ is symmetric in $i,j$, sit at the *same weight* $2^{i+j}$. So the two triangles are termwise equal and merge into one triangle with a factor of two:

$$\sum_{0\le j<i<n}2^{i+j}x_ix_j+\sum_{0\le i<j<n}2^{i+j}x_ix_j=2\!\!\sum_{0\le j<i<n}\!\!2^{i+j}x_ix_j=\!\!\sum_{0\le j<i<n}\!\!2^{i+j+1}x_ix_j$$

**Idempotence of AND**: $x_ix_i = x_i$. A diagonal partial product is not a gate at all, it is a wire — the bit $x_i$ dropped into column $2i$ of the heap.

$$\boxed{\;X^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}2^{2i}x_i\;+\!\!\sum_{0\le j<i<n}\!\!2^{i+j+1}\,x_ix_j\;}\tag{14.1}$$

> [!important] This is the entire justification for a dedicated squarer
> A general $n\times n$ multiplier needs $n^{2}$ AND gates and drops $n^{2}$ bits into the heap. Equation (14.1) needs
> $$\binom{n}{2}=\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\ \text{AND gates},\qquad n+\frac{n(n-1)}{2}=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\ \text{heap bits}$$
> Gate count $\to \tfrac12$ and heap size $\to \tfrac12$ as $n$ grows. Halving the heap halves the compressor tree in front of the final adder ([[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]]) and removes roughly one level from its depth, since compressor-tree depth is $\Theta(\log h)$ in the column height $h$. Building $X^2$ by feeding $X$ into both ports of a general multiplier throws all of that away.

The factor 2 that merges the triangles costs nothing: it is absorbed into the *weight* of the bit, i.e. into which heap column the bit is dropped in. There is no shifter and no adder — the same trick that makes constant-power-of-two multiplication free in [[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]].

## Generalities: formats

Before any architecture, fix the output format from the input format ([[1.1) Number Formats]]).

For $X\in\mathrm{ufix}(m,\ell)$ the largest value is $2^{m+1}-2^{\ell}$ and the smallest nonzero value is $2^{\ell}$, so $X^p$ has MSB weight $p(m+1)$ and LSB weight $p\ell$: the exact result lives in $\mathrm{ufix}\big(p(m+1),p\ell\big)$.

For signed $X\in\mathrm{sfix}(m,\ell)$ we have $X\in[-2^m,\,2^m-2^\ell]$. The LSB is always $p\ell$, but the *signedness* of the output depends on the parity of $p$:

| $p$ | range of $X^p$ | output format |
| --- | --- | --- |
| even | $[0,\;2^{mp}]$ — always positive | $\mathrm{ufix}(pm,\,p\ell)$ |
| odd | $[-2^{mp},\;(2^m-2^\ell)^p]$ — sign of $X$ | $\mathrm{sfix}(pm,\,p\ell)$ |

An even power is unsigned by construction, which is one small saving over a general multiplier that must handle two independent signs. All of the above is the exact value; most applications truncate the LSB end well above $p\ell$.

## Squarers: basics

Equation (14.1) is the whole architecture: emit $n$ diagonal wires into columns $0,2,4,\dots,2n-2$, emit $\binom{n}{2}$ AND gates into columns $i+j+1$, hand the heap to a compressor tree ([[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits#Compressor Trees]]), finish with one carry-propagate adder. For $n=12$ that is a heap of $12+66=78$ bits against $144$ for the multiplier — the shape drawn in Fig. 14.2, p. 445.

Note that column heights are *uneven* in a way a multiplier's are not: columns of even weight carry a diagonal bit, odd columns do not, and the triangle is skewed. This is not a problem for a bit-heap compressor, which does not care about shape, but it does mean the "rows of partial products" mental model from [[2.4) Multipliers]] no longer applies.

## Squarers: high-radix square

Split $X$ into $k$-bit subwords, i.e. read it in radix $2^k$. The same symmetry appears one level up, between *subwords* instead of bits. Two-way split:

$$X^{2}=(2^{k}X_1+X_0)^{2}=2^{2k}X_1^{2}+2\cdot 2^{k}X_1X_0+X_0^{2}\tag{14.2}$$

Three-way:

$$(2^{2k}X_2+2^{k}X_1+X_0)^{2}=2^{4k}X_2^{2}+2^{2k}X_1^{2}+X_0^{2}+2\cdot2^{3k}X_2X_1+2\cdot2^{2k}X_2X_0+2\cdot2^{k}X_1X_0\tag{14.3}$$

Each square or cross product is one multiplier — one DSP block on FPGA ([[1.3) FPGAs#DSP Blocks]]). A general two-way-split multiplier needs 4 DSPs, (14.2) needs **3**; a three-way split goes from 9 down to **6** (p. 445). Crucially this reduction comes at **no arithmetic overhead**, unlike [[2.4) Multipliers#Karatsuba]], which buys its multiplier savings with extra additions. Here the savings are pure: $X_1X_0$ appears twice in the expansion and we simply reuse it, exactly as $x_ix_j$ was reused at bit level.

> [!note] The catch is DSP chaining, not the algebra
> AMD DSP blocks let you cascade a partial sum from one DSP to the next, but the chain input is shifted by exactly **17 bits**. With $k=17$ the powers of two appearing in (14.2) are $0, 18, 34$ — not multiples of 17, so the chain cannot be used directly. In (14.3) they are $0,18,34,35,42,64$, worse. The fix, valid for integers of at most 33 bits, is to rewrite (14.2) as
> $$(2^{17}X_1+X_0)^{2}=2^{34}X_1^{2}+2^{17}(2X_1)X_0+X_0^{2}\tag{14.4}$$
> and pre-shift $X_1$ by one bit before it enters its DSP. That spare bit exists only if $X_1$ is at most 16 bits wide, i.e. $X$ is at most 33 bits — which covers the important 32-bit case. Intel devices need different shift values: Stratix V cascading inputs support 18-bit and 27-bit shifts (pp. 445–446).

## Squarers: Booth recoding for squarers

[[2.4) Multipliers#Booth's Algorithm]] nearly halves a *multiplier's* heap, so it is natural to try it here. Recode

$$X=\sum_i 4^{i}Z_i,\qquad Z_i\in\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}\tag{14.5}$$

and apply the identical split-the-index-set argument, now over recoded digits rather than bits:

$$X^{2}=\sum_i 4^{2i}Z_i^{2}+\sum_{i>j}4^{i+j+1}Z_iZ_j\tag{14.6}$$

Same structure as (14.1) — diagonal plus one triangle at bumped weight — because the derivation only used commutativity, which holds for the $Z_i$ too. The accounting:

- $Z_i^{2}\in\{0,1,4\}$, so it is **2 bits** in the heap. It replaces 3 bits of the radix-2 squarer (two diagonal bits $x_i^2, x_{i+1}^2$ and one off-diagonal $x_ix_{i+1}$).
- $Z_iZ_j\in\{-4,-2,-1,0,1,2,4\}$, so it is **3 sign-extended bits**, replacing a $2\times2$ block of 4 off-diagonal bits.

Expected heap reduction factor $\approx 3/4$ (p. 446).

That is a much weaker win than Booth gives a multiplier, and it is not free: you pay the recoder, plus a Boolean function computing $Z_iZ_j$ where the plain approach needed 4 AND gates. The book's verdict is that a Booth-recoded squarer is **probably less efficient** than a plain squarer built from (14.1). The reason is structural — Booth's leverage on a multiplier comes from killing whole *rows* of partial products, and (14.1) has already killed half the array by a cheaper mechanism.

## Squarers: truncated squarers

There is nothing squarer-specific in the construction: the bit-heap truncation machinery of [[2.4) Multipliers#Truncated Multipliers]] (Sect. 7.2.4, p. 167) applies verbatim. Delete every heap column below a chosen weight, bound the discarded sum, and add a compensation constant so the residual error is faithful ($<1$ ulp, [[1.2) Error Accuracy#Rounding]]). Refinements exist that improve the *statistical* distribution of the error with a linear compensation function [Gar+10], but the generic heap-truncation heuristic is already optimal with respect to **worst-case** error.

> [!important] Rule of thumb: a squarer truncates one column further than a multiplier
> Column $w$ of a squarer heap holds about half as many bits as column $w$ of the same-size multiplier heap — that is the same folding, read column-wise. The worst-case truncation error of a column is (height) $\times$ (weight), so each dropped column contributes **half** the error it would in a multiplier. Since the columns grow geometrically in weight, half the error buys exactly one more column of truncation before the faithfulness budget is spent (p. 447).

The book's illustration (Fig. 14.3, p. 447) is a faithful squarer with input and output both in $\mathrm{ufix}(-1,-12)$, i.e. operands in $[0,1)$: the exact 12-bit heap of Fig. 14.2 truncated to be faithful on its 12 most significant bits by Algorithm 7.1 (p. 172), giving $\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}=\ell-3$ and $t=3$ — three guard columns kept internally — with **3 constant ones** injected into the three rightmost surviving columns as the compensation term.

## FPGA mapping: squarer-specific tiling

The radix-$2^k$ decomposition above is just the special case of [[2.4) Multipliers#Multiplier Tiling]] in which every tile is square. Once you accept rectangular and irregular tiles, better coverings exist [BKD22]. Only the region on and above the diagonal has to be covered — the rest is folded away.

![Examples of tiles in a 12-bit squarer: M0 rectangular, M1 straddling the diagonal with double-covered square S1, T1 squarer-specific](assets/squarer-tiling-01.png)

*Fig. 14.4, p. 448, cropped from the source: the tiling map is too dense to redraw faithfully. Blue = off-diagonal partial products $x_ix_j$, $i>j$; red = diagonal bits.*

- **M0** — an ordinary rectangular multiplier tile, imported unchanged from the multiplier tile library. Its 6 input bits ($x_0,x_1,x_9,x_{10},x_{11}$ and neighbours) are disjoint between the horizontal and vertical axes, so it shares nothing with the diagonal. The only difference from a multiplier is that *one* physical multiplier realises **both** M0 tiles in the figure, its output shifted left by 1 bit — the merge of (14.1) applied at tile granularity.
- **M1** — also rectangular but deliberately placed across the diagonal. Consequence: a square of bits, marked **S1**, is covered both by M1 and by M1's mirror image, so it is computed **twice**; S1 must be recomputed separately and *subtracted* from the heap to get a correct result. Worth it only when M1 is a DSP block that otherwise covers a large area efficiently. Because M1 shares two bits ($x_7,x_8$) between its axes, it is a function of only 6 inputs and could instead be tabulated in a single LUT6.
- **T1** — the genuinely squarer-specific tile: a staircase covering both diagonal and off-diagonal products, shaped so it maps onto LUT6s that fracture into two LUT5s. It covers **15 partial products** and emits **10 bits** into the heap using **10 LUT5 = 5 LUT6**. Efficiency $15/(5+10\times 0.55)\approx 1.43$ partial products per LUT, better than any LUT-based multiplier in Table 8.3, p. 229. T1 can equivalently be drawn as a square on the upper-right corner, in which case a DSP block in square configuration ($w_X=w_Y$) computes it.

Fig. 14.5, p. 449, gives the optimal tiling of a **53-bit squarer with 4 DSP blocks** — the significand squarer of a double-precision floating-point square. The ILP tiling formulation of Chap. 8 extends to squarers, exact and truncated, by requiring only the upper-left region to be covered and constraining squarer-specific tiles to lie along the diagonal [BKD22].

## Cubes and higher powers: direct algebraic expression

Cube the same expansion. The multinomial theorem gives three families of terms — all-equal indices, two-equal, all-distinct — and the multiplicities $3$ and $6=3\cdot 2$ are the multinomial coefficients:

$$X^{3}=\left(\sum_{i=\ell}^{m}2^{i}x_i\right)^{3}=\sum_{\ell\le i\le m}2^{3i}x_i\;+\!\!\sum_{\ell\le i<j\le m}\!\!3\cdot(2^{i+2j}+2^{2i+j})x_ix_j\;+\!\!\sum_{\ell\le i<j<k\le m}\!\!3\cdot 2^{i+j+k+1}x_ix_jx_k\tag{14.8}$$

Idempotence again collapses $x_i^3 \to x_i$ on the first sum and $x_i^2x_j \to x_ix_j$ on the second. The factor $2$ in the last term is the fold ($6 = 3\cdot 2$, absorbed into the weight as before), but the **factor 3 does not fold** — $3x = 2x + x$ merely *replicates* the bit at two adjacent weights in the heap. So the cube pays where the square did not, and the heap grows as the **cube of the input width** for an exact result. The book's own assessment: "this formulation is not very interesting."

Truncation rescues it when the application permits: the techniques of Sect. 7.2.4 can dramatically shrink a truncated cube's heap (Fig. 7.18, p. 173), but by how much is entirely application-dependent — it depends on how many bits of the cube are actually consumed downstream.

Beyond $p=3$, expanding the binary representation is hopeless. $X^4$ is better computed as $(X^2)^2$ with two squarers.

## Cubes and higher powers: computing powers by squaring and multiplying

The generic recursion, exactly binary exponentiation:

- $p=2q$ even: $X^{p}=(X^{q})^{2}$
- $p=2q+1$ odd: $X^{p}=X\cdot(X^{q})^{2}$

$$X^{34}=\big(X^{17}\big)^{2}=\big(X\cdot X^{16}\big)^{2}=\Big(X\cdot\big(X^{8}\big)^{2}\Big)^{2}=\dots=\left(X\cdot\left(\big(\big(X^{2}\big)^{2}\big)^{2}\right)^{2}\right)^{2}\tag{14.10}$$

Cost: $\lfloor\log_2 p\rfloor$ **squarings** plus $k-1$ **multiplications**, where $k$ is the population count of $p$ in binary. For $p=34=100010_2$, $k=2$: five squarings and one multiplication. As a chain, with the operand width on every wire:

> [!warning] The exact version blows up
> For an exact result, every squarer outputs **twice as many bits as it inputs**. Chaining five of them from an $n$-bit input gives a $32n$-bit final squarer. The operator count is logarithmic in $p$ but the operator *widths* are exponential in the depth, so "this solution can become quite expensive" (p. 450).

## Cubes and higher powers: approximate fixed-point powers

The exponential width growth is only a problem if you insist on the exact value. When $X$ arrives in $\mathrm{ufix}(-1,\ell)$ or $\mathrm{sfix}(0,\ell)$ we have $|X|<1$, hence $|X^{p}|<1$, and the output almost always uses the same format — so it must be truncated or rounded anyway.

Correct rounding would still require the exact $X^{p}$, because you cannot decide the last bit without the tail ([[1.2) Error Accuracy#Rounding]]). A **faithful** result is far cheaper: run the square-and-multiply recursion with every intermediate clamped to the output width plus a few guard bits, using only truncated squarers and truncated multipliers. The widths then stay constant down the chain instead of doubling. Sizing rule of thumb: **one guard bit per squaring or multiplication** (p. 451). The book gives no detailed error analysis for this.

> [!warning] Not covered in the source — needs outside research
> The detailed error analysis of a chain of truncated squarers and multipliers is explicitly skipped by the source ("we do not provide a detailed error analysis here", p. 451). The one-guard-bit-per-operation figure is stated without derivation.

For very small precisions (8 bits and below, p. 443) plain tabulation of $X^p$ beats all of the above and delivers correct rounding for free — see [[4.2) Table-Based Function Evaluation]]. Alternatively use the generic approximation machinery of [[4.1) Fixed-Point Function Approximation]] and treat $X^p$ as just another smooth function [PBM01]. Floating-point $X^k$ belongs to [[3.4) Floating-Point Specialization]].

> This chapter reports **no measured LUT counts or delays** for squarers, unlike the constant-multiplier and divider chapters. The DSP counts, the T1 efficiency figure, and the $3/4$ Booth factor are the only hard numbers; latency comparisons above are structural, not measured.

**Picking one.** Use the plain squarer of (14.1) whenever the target is LUT logic and the width is modest — it is the default and it is nearly always right. Move to the high-radix decomposition (14.2)/(14.3) as soon as DSP blocks are the binding resource, and to (14.4) if the design depends on DSP cascading and $X$ fits in 33 bits. Truncate the moment the application tolerates a faithful result — the squarer gives you a free extra column over a multiplier. Reach for ILP tiling only for large widths (the 53-bit floating-point significand is the canonical case), where hand-tiling stops being tractable. For $p>2$, never expand (14.8) unless the result is heavily truncated; square-and-multiply with truncated intermediates is the default. Skip all of it and tabulate below 8 bits.

## Worked example: a 6-bit squarer

Take $n=6$ and $X = 45 = 101101_2$, so $x_5x_4x_3x_2x_1x_0 = 1,0,1,1,0,1$. The exact result must fit in 12 bits.

**Raw array.** The general multiplier builds all $6\times6 = 36$ products $x_ix_j$ at weight $2^{i+j}$.

**Fold.** Apply (14.1). Six diagonal terms become free wires at even weights; the 15 pairs $j<i$ become one AND each, at weight $2^{i+j+1}$. Heap size $21$ instead of $36$ — a $42\%$ cut. The resulting heap, column by column:

| weight $w$ | diagonal bit | merged cross terms | height |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $x_0$ | — | 1 |
| $1$ | — | — | 0 |
| $2$ | $x_1$ | $x_1x_0$ | 2 |
| $3$ | — | $x_2x_0$ | 1 |
| $4$ | $x_2$ | $x_3x_0,\;x_2x_1$ | 3 |
| $5$ | — | $x_4x_0,\;x_3x_1$ | 2 |
| $6$ | $x_3$ | $x_5x_0,\;x_4x_1,\;x_3x_2$ | 4 |
| $7$ | — | $x_5x_1,\;x_4x_2$ | 2 |
| $8$ | $x_4$ | $x_5x_2,\;x_4x_3$ | 3 |
| $9$ | — | $x_5x_3$ | 1 |
| $10$ | $x_5$ | $x_5x_4$ | 2 |
| | $6$ | $15$ | **21** |

Peak column height is 4, against 6 for the multiplier heap — hence the shallower compressor tree.

**Substitute $X=101101_2$.** Set bits are $\{0,2,3,5\}$; a cross term is 1 iff both its indices are set. Reading the table above column by column with those values gives the heap in stage (a) below.

*Own construction; the heap of stage (a) is the $n=6$ case of Fig. 14.2, p. 445.*

Column sums, read as a weighted sum:

$$X^{2}=1\!\cdot\!2^{0}+1\!\cdot\!2^{3}+2\!\cdot\!2^{4}+3\!\cdot\!2^{6}+1\!\cdot\!2^{8}+1\!\cdot\!2^{9}+1\!\cdot\!2^{10}$$
$$=1+8+32+192+256+512+1024=2025$$

**Compress.** Two columns are still over height 1. A half adder on column 4 ($1+1$) emits sum $0$, carry $1$ into column 5. A full adder on column 6 ($1+1+1$) emits sum $1$, carry $1$ into column 7:

$$\text{columns }(10\dots 0)=1,1,1,0,3,0,2,1,0,0,1\;\longrightarrow\;1,1,1,1,1,1,0,1,0,0,1$$

$$X^{2}=11111101001_2 = 1024+512+256+128+64+32+8+1 = 2025 = 45^{2}\quad\checkmark$$

**Now truncate.** Keep only columns $w\ge t$ and drop the rest. The worst-case discarded value is $\sum_{w<t}c_w2^{w}$ with $c_w$ the column heights above, versus the multiplier's own column heights $(1,2,3,4,5,6,\dots)$ at the same positions:

| $t$ | squarer $\sum_{w<t}c_w2^{w}$ | in ulp of $2^{t}$ | multiplier bound | in ulp of $2^{t}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $2$ | $1\!\cdot\!1+0\!\cdot\!2=1$ | $0.25$ — faithful | $1+2\!\cdot\!2=5$ | $1.25$ — **not** faithful |
| $3$ | $1+0+2\!\cdot\!4=9$ | $1.125$ — needs compensation | $1+4+3\!\cdot\!4=17$ | $2.125$ |

*Own construction, the $n=6$ instance of the one-extra-column rule of p. 447 (cf. Fig. 14.3).*

At $t=2$ the squarer is faithful ($<1$ ulp, [[1.2) Error Accuracy]]) and the multiplier is not — the "one extra column" rule made concrete. At $t=3$ the raw squarer bound is $1.125$ ulp, just over budget; adding the compensation constant of Algorithm 7.1 recentres the error and recovers faithfulness, which is precisely why the book's 12-bit example carries 3 constant ones.
