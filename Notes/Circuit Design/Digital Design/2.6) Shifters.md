**Specification.** Input a bit vector $X$ on $w_X$ bits and an unsigned shift distance $S$ on $w_S=\lceil\log_2(S_{\max}+1)\rceil$ bits; output $R$ on $w_R$ bits equal to $X$ shifted by $S$ in a direction *fixed at generation time*, optionally with a **sticky bit** $\sigma=\bigvee(\text{bits shifted out})$. An **exact full shifter** ($w_R=w_X+S_{\max}$) loses nothing: $0$ ulp. A **truncated shifter** ($w_R<w_X+S_{\max}$) drops low bits, so truncation alone costs up to $1$ ulp of $R$; $\sigma$ plus a round bit brings that back to $\le\tfrac12$ ulp under round-to-nearest ([[1.2) Error Accuracy]]).
**What is optimized:** latency first — a shifter is almost never the answer, it is a step on somebody else's critical path (see [[2.7) Floating-Point Operators]]) — then area, then the fanout of $S$.

## Derivation: a shift is a binary power of one operator

Let $\sigma$ denote shift-by-one. Shifting is a monoid homomorphism from $(\mathbb{N},+)$ into function composition:

$$\sigma^{a}\circ\sigma^{b}=\sigma^{a+b}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad (X\gg a)\gg b=X\gg(a+b)$$

Expand the shift distance in binary — this is the only "law" in the whole construction:

$$S=\sum_{i=0}^{w_S-1}s_i2^i,\qquad s_i\in\{0,1\}$$

Substitute into the homomorphism and re-bracket by **associativity of composition**:

$$X\gg S \;=\; \sigma^{\sum_i s_i2^i}(X)\;=\;\Big(\sigma^{s_{w_S-1}2^{w_S-1}}\circ\cdots\circ\sigma^{s_12}\circ\sigma^{s_0}\Big)(X)$$

Each factor $\sigma^{s_i2^i}$ is a two-way choice: identity when $s_i=0$, hardwired shift by $2^i$ when $s_i=1$. A hardwired shift is free (it is routing), so the factor costs exactly **one row of 1-bit 2:1 multiplexers** controlled by $s_i$. Hence

$$\text{depth}=w_S=\lceil\log_2(S_{\max}+1)\rceil\ \text{mux levels},\qquad \text{area}=O(w_X\log S_{\max})$$

**Commutativity of $(\mathbb{N},+)$** means the stages can be permuted freely; the result is identical whatever the order. That freedom is spent below on latency ([[#Architecture of an Exact Full Shifter]]) and on sticky-bit timing ([[#Barrel Shifter with Early Sticky-Bit Computation]]).

> [!important] This is the same trick as the prefix trees
> Kogge–Stone in [[2.1) Adders]] gets $\log n$ depth because the carry operator $\circ$ is associative, so stage $i$ can pre-compose it over a span of $2^i$ bits. Here the associative operator is shift-composition and stage $i$ applies it over a distance of $2^i$. Both are *binary powering of an associative operator*: decompose an index into bits, spend one logic level per bit. Every structure in this note — barrel shifter, prefix-OR, the LZC tree — is that same statement wearing a different operator.

## Avoiding Bidirectional Shifters

A bidirectional shifter takes a direction bit (or, equivalently, a signed $S$ as sign+magnitude) and shifts either way. **Do not build one.** The right move is to bias the problem so the data is only ever shifted *left*, starting from its rightmost possible position. The overhead is one constant offset added to the signed $S$ — the largest possible right shift — converting it to an unsigned distance.

That addition is usually free. In a floating-point context $S$ comes from an exponent difference, which already carries a constant bias ([[1.1) Number Formats]]), so the offset merges into the existing bias subtraction. And $w_S$ is tiny ($w_S\le6$ covers shifts up to 64), so even a standalone adder for it is cheap. Reducing every shift to a unidirectional one buys a strictly better architecture, especially when the maximum left and right distances differ (p. 308–309).

Throughout, direction is a **generator** parameter, not a runtime input. A right shifter is the mirror image of a left shifter: exchange the two inputs of every multiplexer, or equivalently complement every $s_i$ (Fig. 10.4 caption, p. 312).

## Parameters of a Generic Shifter Generator

| Parameter | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| direction | left or right, fixed at generation |
| $w_X$ | input width |
| $S_{\max}$ | maximum shift distance — **not** $w_S$ |
| $w_R$ | output width |
| $(p_\text{left},p_\text{right})$ | pad bits: $0$ = pad with zeroes, $1$ = pad with ones, $-1$ = pad with a third input port, sampled at runtime |

$p=-1$ is what makes an *arithmetic* right shift: left-pad with the sign bit of $X$, i.e. divide a two's-complement number by $2^S$ ([[1.1) Number Formats]]). This is the ASR/LSR split every ISA exposes.

$S_{\max}$, not $w_S$, is the parameter, because real requirements are not powers of two minus one. An IEEE-754 adder needs shifts of $w_F+2$ bits, i.e. 12, 25 or 54 for the standard formats — **none** of which has the form $2^{w_S}-1$ (p. 311). $w_S$ is then derived: $w_S=\lceil\log_2(S_{\max}+1)\rceil$.

Two useful classes sit at the extremes, plus the useless region in between:

- **exact full shifter**: $w_R=w_X+S_{\max}$; no bit of $X$ is ever discarded.
- **truncated full shifter**: $S_{\max}=w_R$; $X$ can be shifted out completely.
- **barrel (rectangular) shifter**: $w_X=w_R$ and $S_{\max}=w_X$; necessarily truncated. This is the ALU shifter of a microprocessor.

Everything useful obeys one constraint (Eq. 10.1, p. 311):

$$S_{\max}\;\le\;\max(w_R,w_X)\;\le\;\min(w_R,w_X)+S_{\max}$$

Read it as: an output bit that no input bit can reach is dead silicon, and a shift so large that the whole input is gone is a constant.

## Architecture of an Exact Full Shifter

Stack the $w_S$ mux rows of the derivation, growing the word as you go. With $S_{\max}=2^{w_S}-1$ the intermediate width after stage $i$ is $w_X+2^{i+1}-1$, since the pad bits accumulate (Fig. 10.4, p. 312: $10\to11\to13\to17\to25$ for $w_X=10$, $S_{\max}=15$).

**Consume the bits of $S$ LSB-first.** Two independent reasons, both from p. 313:

1. It minimizes the summed multiplexer width. For $w_X=8$, $w_S=4$, LSB-first costs $(w_X+1)+(w_X+3)+(w_X+7)+(w_X+15)=58$ 1-bit muxes; MSB-first costs $(w_X+8)+(w_X+12)+(w_X+14)+(w_X+15)=81$. LSB-first keeps the narrow stages early, where the word is still short.
2. $S$ usually comes out of an adder or subtractor, and carry propagation produces the **low** bits first. LSB-first lets stage 0 start while the adder is still resolving the high bits of $S$ — a free latency overlap, and it means the adder computing $S$ need not be a fast one.

When $S_{\max}\ne2^{w_S}-1$ the architecture is unchanged except in the last stage, which is narrowed. It remains *exact*: the bits the last stage appears to discard can only be zeroes introduced by earlier stages, because $S>S_{\max}$ never occurs (Fig. 10.5, p. 313).

Padding with zeroes collapses the affected muxes to AND gates, since one input is a constant.

## Barrel Shifters

A barrel shifter is the case $w_X=w_R$: the word never grows, so every stage is the same width and every $s_i$ has the same fanout. All $w_S$ rows are $w_X$ muxes wide:

$$\text{muxes}=w_X\lceil\log_2(S_{\max}+1)\rceil,\qquad \text{depth}=\lceil\log_2(S_{\max}+1)\rceil$$

For the 16-bit, $S_{\max}=15$ shifter of Fig. 10.6 (p. 315) that is $16\times4=64$ 1-bit muxes at depth 4.

Because the stages are now interchangeable in cost as well as in function, stage order is a pure timing decision. The LSB-first order of Fig. 10.4 is still the default, for reason (2) above: overlap with the adder computing $S$.

The alternative — one $(S_{\max}{+}1)$:1 mux per output bit, driven by a one-hot decode of $S$ — has the same asymptotic *depth* (one AND level plus a $\log_2$ OR tree) but quadratic area, $w_X\cdot S_{\max}$ 2:1-equivalents, and a fanout of $S_{\max}$ on every bit of $X$. Never competitive above trivial widths. See the [[#Trade-offs]] table.

## Barrel Shifter with Early Sticky-Bit Computation

In floating-point addition the smaller significand is right-shifted to align with the larger; the adder then only consumes the upper bits. The lower bits must not simply vanish — round-to-nearest-even needs to know whether they were *all zero* (a tie) or not. That one bit of information is the **sticky bit**:

$$\sigma=\bigvee_{i\in\mathcal{D}}x_i,\qquad \mathcal{D}=\{\text{bits of }X\text{ discarded by the shift}\}$$

The naive architecture is an exact full shifter followed by a wide OR over the low bits. It pays twice: once for the extra width that keeps the doomed bits alive, and once for a wide OR that is entirely serial after the shift.

Both costs come off using **associativity and idempotence of OR**. Let $\mathcal{D}_j$ be the set of bits stage $j$ throws away. The $\mathcal{D}_j$ are disjoint and $\bigcup_j\mathcal{D}_j=\mathcal{D}$ — the same binary decomposition as the shift itself — so

$$\sigma=\bigvee_{j=0}^{w_S-1}t_j,\qquad t_j=s_j\wedge\!\!\bigvee_{i\in\mathcal{D}_j}\!\!x_i$$

Stage $j$ discards at most $2^j$ bits, and only when $s_j=1$; so $t_j$ is a $2^j$-wide OR gated by $s_j$, computed *inside* the stage from bits that are present there anyway. The word never has to grow: the exact full shifter with $w_X=w_F+1$, $S_{\max}=w_F+3$ collapses to a barrel shifter with $w_X=w_R=w_F+3$ (p. 316).

Now spend the stage-ordering freedom. With LSB-first ordering the **last** stage discards the most bits, so the widest OR starts last and lands squarely on the critical path. Reverse the order — largest shift first — and the widest OR starts at $t=0$ and folds into the OR accumulation chain while the remaining mux stages are still resolving.

> [!note] Why "early" is the whole point
> The sticky bit is not optional decoration — without it round-to-nearest-even cannot distinguish an exact tie from a value just above it, and the operator loses its $\tfrac12$ ulp guarantee ([[1.2) Error Accuracy]], [[Rounding]]). The naive placement computes it *after* the shift, so its wide OR is pure added latency in series with the whole shifter. Computing it *incrementally inside* the stages costs the same gates but hides them under mux delay that was going to be paid anyway. Measured on a single-precision floating-point adder, the reversed-order incremental version cut **20% of the area and 5% of the delay** versus an exact full shifter plus a wide OR (p. 316); comparable savings were reported for posit addition. Area from not growing the word, delay from not serialising the OR.

## Leading Zero Counters

An LZC takes $X$ on $w_X$ bits and returns, as a binary integer $C$ on $w_C$ bits, the position of the leftmost non-zero bit. Trivial variants: the **leading one counter** (LOC) counts ones; the **leading bit counter** (LBC) takes the bit value $b$ to count as an input. The LBC generalizes both but pays for the fanout of $b$, so the three are kept distinct (p. 316).

### The recurrence

Take $w_X=2^k-1$ so that $C\in[0,w_X]$ fits exactly on $w_C=k$ bits. Split $X$ into its upper $2^{k-1}$ bits $X_H$ and lower $2^{k-1}-1$ bits $X_L$. Then

$$c_{k-1}=1\iff C\ge2^{k-1}\iff X_H=0\iff c_{k-1}=\mathrm{NOR}(X_H)$$

and the count splits by cases:

$$\mathrm{LZC}_k(X)=\begin{cases}2^{k-1}+\mathrm{LZC}_{k-1}(X_L) & X_H=0\\[2pt] \mathrm{LZC}_{k-1}(X_H) & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

The addition of $2^{k-1}$ is **free**: it is exactly the concatenation of $c_{k-1}=1$ in front of a $(k-1)$-bit field, since $\mathrm{LZC}_{k-1}<2^{k-1}$ carries no bit into that column. So one recursion level is one wide NOR plus one $(2^{k-1}-1)$-wide 1-bit multiplexer, feeding an $\mathrm{LZC}(k-1)$. The middle bit of $X$ can even be dropped: whichever branch is taken, it is already accounted for.

If $w_X$ is not of the form $2^k-1$, set $w_C=\lceil\log_2(w_X+1)\rceil$ and pad $X$ on the right with **ones** to width $2^{w_C}-1$. Padding with ones makes $C$ saturate at $w_X$ for $X=0$ rather than run off (p. 318–319).

### Naive LZC architecture

Chaining those levels gives (Fig. 10.10, p. 319, for $w_X=15$, $w_C=4$):

$$\text{1-bit muxes}=\sum_{k=1}^{w_C-1}(2^k-1)=2^{w_C}-1-w_C=w_X-w_C$$

and the same count of 2-input NOR gates. **Area is linear in $w_X$**, like a ripple-carry adder.

Delay is the problem. The NOR at level $k$ is a binary tree over $2^{k-1}$ bits, depth $k-1$, plus 1 for the mux, and levels are strictly serial:

$$d_k=k+d_{k-1}\;\Longrightarrow\;d_k=\frac{k(k+1)}{2}=O(\log^2 w_X)$$

Quadratic in the logarithm, not logarithmic. Keep this architecture anyway on FPGAs — see [[#FPGA mapping]].

### Logarithmic-time LZC architectures

**Why it can be logarithmic at all.** Summarise a chunk of bits by the pair $(z,C)$: $z=1$ iff the chunk is all zeroes, $C$ = its leading-zero count (meaningful only when $z=0$). Carrying the chunk width $n$, define

$$(z_L,C_L,n_L)\bullet(z_R,C_R,n_R)=\big(z_L\wedge z_R,\;\; z_L\,?\,(n_L+C_R):C_L,\;\; n_L+n_R\big)$$

$\bullet$ is **associative**, and the proof is one line: whatever the bracketing, the result is the offset of the leftmost 1 in the concatenated chunk, and that offset is defined without reference to any bracketing. Its identity is the empty chunk $(1,\cdot,0)$. So the LZC is a reduction under an associative operator over $w_X$ leaves — exactly the situation of [[2.1) Adders#Parallel Prefix Adders]] — and *therefore* a balanced binary tree evaluates it in $\lceil\log_2 w_X\rceil$ levels. The naive architecture is the fully unbalanced (linear) association of the same operator; that is the whole difference.

**Oklobdzija's recursive construction** (p. 320) is that balanced tree, specialised to equal power-of-two halves $w_X=2^{k+1}$, where $n_L=2^k$ and $n_L+C_R$ degenerates to a concatenation:

$$z=z_L\wedge z_R \tag{10.2}$$
$$C=0C_L\ \text{when}\ z_L=0,\quad \text{else}\ 1C_R \tag{10.3}$$

Counting bit-level multiplexers, $m_{k+1}=k+1+2m_k$ with $m_1=1$, giving $m_k=2^{k+1}-k-2$ — slightly under $2w_X$, roughly twice the naive count — plus a tree of $w_X$ AND gates matching the naive architecture's NORs. A wide multiplexer is one gate delay (its bit slices run in parallel), so each level adds one: total $O(\log_2 w_X)$. The catch is **fanout**: the select signal $z_L$ drives an entire $k$-bit-wide multiplexer, and at large $w_X$ that fanout must be charged to the delay.

**LZC by monotonic string conversion** (p. 321–322) fixes the fanout, and is more general. Convert $X$ to a monotone string $Y$ with the same leading one but all ones to its left:

$$y_i=\bigvee_{j\ge i}x_j$$

which is a **prefix-OR** — again an associative reduction, so it is a [[Kogge-Stone Tree]] with fanout limited to 2, in $\log_2 w_X$ levels ([[2.1) Adders#Parallel Prefix Adders]]). $Y$ is a thermometer code: $n$ leading ones encode the count $n$. Isolate the transition by AND-ing $Y$ with its own negated one-bit shift,

$$Z=Y\wedge\overline{Y\gg1}$$

which is one-hot at the leading-one position. Each bit of $C$ then falls out of a $w_X/2$-wide OR over the positions whose index has that bit set:

$$c_j=\bigvee\{\,z_p\;:\;\lfloor p/2^{j}\rfloor \text{ odd}\,\}$$

i.e. $c_0$ ORs every other bit of $Z$, $c_{w_C-1}$ ORs the whole lower half, and intermediate $c_j$ OR consecutive chunks of $2^j$. Worked on Fig. 10.12's example, $X=0000010011011001$ gives $Y=0000011111111111$, $Z=0000010000000000$, and the four ORs read out $c_3c_2c_1c_0=0101=5$ — the leading one sits at position 5. Correct.

This form adapts directly to LOC and LBC, and it is the basis of **leading-zero anticipation**: predict the LZC of a sum from the addends *in parallel with* the addition, removing the LZC from the floating-point adder's critical path entirely.

## Normalizers

A normalizer is an LZC fused with a left shifter: count the leading zeroes of $X$, shift them out so the leading one lands on the MSB, and output both $R$ (the normalized word, $w_R$ bits) and $C$ (the count, $w_C$ bits). It is how a fixed-point number becomes a floating-point one — $R$ is the significand, $C$ becomes the exponent up to a constant (p. 323).

Variants: count zeroes for sign/magnitude formats; count the **leading sign bit** for two's-complement significands, as [[Floating-Point Formats]]-adjacent formats like posits require, which is why the generator's fourth parameter is *what to count* (0s, 1s, or the sign bit $s$). When $w_R<w_X$ the discarded low bits need a sticky bit again, for the same ties-to-even reason as above.

**Why fusing helps.** The obvious pipelinable construction is LZC then shifter, with $C$ wired to $S$ — two full latencies in series. The two logarithmic LZCs of the previous section produce *all* count bits simultaneously, so there is nothing to overlap. The naive LZC does not: its bits arrive **MSB-first**, $c_{k-1}$ first. That is exactly the order a left shifter wants to consume them if its stages are ordered largest-shift-first. So each level can do its NOR, emit its count bit, *and* perform its own conditional shift, all in one pass (Fig. 10.15, p. 324):

- level $j$ takes the current word, NORs its top $2^j$ bits to get $c_j$, and muxes between the word and the word shifted left by $2^j$ under $c_j$.

Area grows as $O(w_X\log w_X)$ and delay as $O(\log^2 w_X)$, but on FPGAs the practical delay is close to $\log_2 w_X$ because the wide NORs ride the fast carry logic (p. 325).

> [!warning] Not for IEEE-754
> A fused normalizer is *not* appropriate inside an IEEE floating-point adder or FMA: subnormal support requires arithmetic on the count itself, which the fused form has no place for. It is for application-specific formats without subnormals — FloPoCo's Nfloat, posits, and the internals of elementary function evaluators (p. 324).

The cheapest normalizer of all is a while-loop shifting one bit per cycle while the MSB is 0. Tiny, but variable-latency and unpipelinable — fine for a state machine, useless in a datapath.

## FPGA mapping

- **Two mux stages per LUT level.** A 6-input [[Lookup-Tables|LUT]] implements a 4:1 multiplexer (4 data + 2 select), so two successive rows of 2:1 muxes merge into one row of 6-LUTs. Halve both the LUT count and the logic depth versus the 2:1 picture. Back-end tools find this themselves; [[FloPoCo]] emits plain 2:1 muxes. **But**: when pipelining a shifter, place registers every *other* level, or you defeat the merge (p. 314).
- **Wide ORs are not OR trees.** A wide OR/NOR maps onto the fast carry chain, not a tree of gates. Synthesis handles it; the designer only needs to know that wide-OR delay on FPGA is far below the $\log$ of its width (p. 315).
- **Prefer the naive LZC on FPGA.** Its $O(\log^2)$ asymptotic is misleading: its wide NORs are precisely what wide LUTs plus fast carry do best, and it is the only variant that merges cleanly with a shifter into a normalizer (p. 320).
- **Shift on a DSP block.** A left shift is a multiplication by $2^S$, so one-hot-encode $S$ in logic and feed a [[DSP Blocks|DSP]] multiplier. Some AMD floating-point adder cores do exactly this; it is faster and cheaper than a full logic shifter when a DSP is free (p. 325).
- **Sporadic shifters.** When only a discrete subset of shift distances is needed, the multiplexer organization can be optimized against that set rather than against $[0,S_{\max}]$ (p. 325).
- **Constant $X$.** Tabulate all shifted values in a [[Block Memory|block RAM]] addressed by the shift value; in polynomial evaluation, shift the *coefficients* in the table instead of the datapath (p. 325).

## Trade-offs

Shifters, $w_X=w_R=n$, $S_{\max}=n-1$:

| Variant | 1-bit muxes | Depth | $n=16$ | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Naive mux-per-position (one $n$:1 mux per output bit) | $n(n-1)$ | 1 AND + $\log_2 n$ OR | 240 cells, fanout 15 on each $x_i$ | derived, not in the book |
| Barrel shifter | $n\log_2 n$ | $\log_2 n$ | 64 muxes, depth 4 | Fig. 10.6, p. 315 |
| Barrel shifter on 6-LUTs | $\tfrac12 n\log_2 n$ LUTs | $\tfrac12\log_2 n$ | 32 LUTs, depth 2 | p. 314 |
| Exact full shifter, LSB-first ($w_X{=}8$, $w_S{=}4$) | 58 | 4 | — | p. 313 |
| Exact full shifter, MSB-first (same) | 81 | 4 | — | p. 313 |
| Exact full shifter + wide OR vs. reversed barrel + early sticky | — | — | **+20% area, +5% delay** for the naive one | p. 316 |

Leading zero counters, $w_X$ bits, $w_C=\lceil\log_2(w_X+1)\rceil$:

| Variant | Muxes | Other gates | Delay | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Naive | $w_X-w_C$ | $w_X-w_C$ 2-input NORs | $d_k=k(k+1)/2$, $O(\log^2 w_X)$ | p. 319–320 |
| Oklobdzija | $2^{k+1}-k-2\approx 2w_X$ | $w_X$ ANDs | $O(\log_2 w_X)$, high fanout on $z_L$ | p. 320–321 |
| Monotonic string | — | Kogge–Stone prefix-OR + $w_X$ ANDs + $w_C$ wide ORs | $O(\log_2 w_X)$, fanout $\le2$ | p. 321–322 |
| Fused normalizer (naive LZC + barrel) | $O(w_X\log w_X)$ area | — | $O(\log^2 w_X)$; $\approx\log_2 w_X$ on FPGA | p. 325 |

Pick the **barrel shifter** unless you specifically need every bit preserved, in which case pay for the exact full shifter and consume $S$ LSB-first. Pick **early sticky in reversed stage order** whenever the shifter feeds a rounded operator — it is strictly cheaper *and* strictly faster than shift-then-OR. Pick the **naive LZC** on FPGA and whenever it can be fused into a normalizer; pick a **logarithmic LZC** on ASIC at large $w_X$, and among those, **monotonic string conversion** when the Oklobdzija fanout starts showing up in timing reports.

## Worked example

8-bit right barrel shifter, $w_X=w_R=8$, $S_{\max}=7$, $w_S=3$, with early sticky in reversed stage order.

$$X=\underline{101}\underline{10101}_2=181,\qquad S=5=s_2s_1s_0=101_2$$

| Stage | $s_j$ | Action | Bits discarded $\mathcal{D}_j$ | $t_j$ | Word after stage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| — | — | — | — | — | $10110101$ |
| $j=2$ | 1 | $\gg 4$ | $0101$ | $1\wedge(0{\vee}1{\vee}0{\vee}1)=1$ | $00001011$ |
| $j=1$ | 0 | pass | $\varnothing$ | $0\wedge(1{\vee}1)=0$ | $00001011$ |
| $j=0$ | 1 | $\gg 1$ | $1$ | $1\wedge 1=1$ | $00000101$ |

$$R=00000101_2=5,\qquad \sigma=t_2\vee t_1\vee t_0=1$$

The disjoint discarded sets reassemble to $\mathcal{D}=\{0101\}\cup\varnothing\cup\{1\}=x_4x_3x_2x_1x_0=10101$, the low 5 bits of $X$ — as the derivation promised.

Run the same operands LSB-first to check order-independence: $\gg1\to01011010$ (discards $1$, $t_0=1$); pass; $\gg4\to00000101$ (discards $1010$, $t_2=1$). Identical $R$, identical $\sigma$. Commutativity of $(\mathbb{N},+)$, as claimed.

**Error.** Exactly, $181/2^5=5.65625$. Truncation gives $R=5$, an error of $0.65625$ ulp of $R$ — above $\tfrac12$ ulp, so truncation alone is only *faithful*, not correctly rounded. Split $\mathcal{D}$ into round bit $r=x_4=1$ and sticky $\sigma=x_3{\vee}x_2{\vee}x_1{\vee}x_0=1$: $r=1$ and $\sigma=1$ means "strictly above the midpoint", so round-to-nearest increments to $6$, error $6-5.65625=0.34375$ ulp $\le\tfrac12$ ulp. Had $\sigma$ been 0 with $r=1$ the value would have been an exact tie and ties-to-even would have kept $R=5$ ([[Rounding]]). **Without $\sigma$ those two cases are indistinguishable** — that single bit is the entire difference between a faithful and a correctly-rounded shift.
