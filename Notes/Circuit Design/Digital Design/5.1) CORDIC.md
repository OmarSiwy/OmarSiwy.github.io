**Input.** One binary angle $X$ in $\mathrm{sfix}(0,\ell)$, i.e. two's complement, $X\in[-1,1)$, $-\ell$ fractional bits. The argument of the trigonometric function is $\pi X$, so $X$ spans exactly one period.
**Output.** The pair $(C,S)\approx(\cos\pi X,\sin\pi X)$, both in $\mathrm{sfix}(0,\ell)$ — a *fused* sin/cos operator, because most methods compute both anyway and most callers (rotations, DDS) want both.
**Accuracy.** Last-bit accurate: $|\delta_\text{total}|<1\ \mathrm{ulp}=2^{\ell}$, with respect to the **scaled** functions $f_{\sin}(x)=(1-2^{\ell})\sin\pi x$ and $f_{\cos}(x)=(1-2^{\ell})\cos\pi x$ (see [[1.2) Error Accuracy]]).
**Optimized for.** Throughput per area, at fixed accuracy: everything below is a fully pipelined (unrolled) architecture, and the question the chapter answers is which one buys that throughput for the fewest LUTs, DSPs and pipeline stages.

This is a case study, not a technique. One specification, three architectures, and the interesting part is the comparison at the end.

## Mathematical background

Four identities off the unit circle generate the whole chapter; each architecture leans on a different one.

| # | Identity | Used by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| (20.1) | $\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1$ | modulus bookkeeping, the CORDIC scale factor |
| (20.2)–(20.11) | periodicity $\theta+2\pi$, symmetries about $x$, $y$, the origin, the diagonal | [[#Argument reduction]] |
| (20.14) | $\cos(a+b)=\cos a\cos b-\sin a\sin b$, $\ \sin(a+b)=\sin a\cos b+\cos a\sin b$ | CORDIC's micro-rotations, the multiplier shortcut, the table-and-multiplier split |
| (20.15)/(20.17) | $\sin x=x-\frac{x^3}{3!}+\dots$, $\ \cos x=1-\frac{x^2}{2!}+\dots$ | the small-angle polynomials |

(20.14) is the real projection of $e^{j(a+b)}=e^{ja}e^{jb}$ (20.13): *rotating by $a+b$ is rotating by $a$ then by $b$.* Every method here splits $\theta$ into pieces whose sine and cosine are cheap and re-composes with (20.14). CORDIC splits it into $-\ell$ tiny pieces; the table architecture splits it into exactly two.

## Fixed-point specification

### Binary angles

Radians are the wrong unit for hardware. The period $2\pi$ is irrational, so a radian argument can never tile the fixed-point grid and the wrap-around alone costs a systematic rounding error. The chapter therefore computes $\sin(\pi X)$ and $\cos(\pi X)$ with $X\in[-1,1)$ in $\mathrm{sfix}(0,-\ell)$ — see [[1.1) Number Formats]] for the format, and [[Fixed-Point Representations]] for two's complement.

The payoff is that **the modulo-$2\pi$ periodicity of the function becomes the modulo-$2$ wrap of two's complement**, which is free. The input $-1$ maps to $+1$ by the wrap, and in angle terms $-\pi\mapsto\pi$: identical points on the circle, so $[-1,1)$ is exactly one period. Consequently an operator specified on $[-1,1)$ evaluates *any* wider argument at zero cost — give it $k$ integer bits and simply drop them, since they weigh integer multiples of the period. That is precisely how direct digital synthesis (DDS) phase accumulators work.

### Scaled output

The value $1$ is not representable in $\mathrm{sfix}(0,\ell)$, whose range is $[-1,1)$. Rounding $\cos 0=1$ up would wrap it to $-1$ — a catastrophic modulo error, not a small one. This chapter picks scaling:

$$f_{\sin}(x)=(1-2^{\ell})\sin(\pi x),\qquad f_{\cos}(x)=(1-2^{\ell})\cos(\pi x) \tag{20.19–20.20}$$

so the true supremum becomes $1-2^{\ell}$, the largest representable value, and the last-bit accuracy claim is *against $f_{\sin},f_{\cos}$, not against $\sin,\cos$*. Three reasons this is not a cheat: signal-processing callers care about shape rather than absolute amplitude; the systematic $2^{\ell}$ relative error is removed by asking for one more bit of $\ell$; and for every architecture below the factor $(1-2^{\ell})$ is absorbed into a constant that had to exist anyway, so it costs nothing. Read it as a rounding mode: $(1-2^{\ell})$ is the "round toward zero" answer to the range problem, chosen for the same reason truncation is chosen inside a datapath.

### The three algorithms compared

1. **CORDIC** (Volder, 1959): decompose the angle into micro-rotations chosen so that (20.14) needs only additions and shifts. Sect. 20.4.
2. **Table and multipliers**: a coarser split $\theta=\theta_h+\theta_l$, where $\theta_h$ is the $k$ leading bits, so $\theta_l<2^{-k}$. Then $\sin\theta_h,\cos\theta_h$ come from a table and $\sin\theta_l,\cos\theta_l$ from a low-degree Taylor polynomial, recombined by (20.14). This uses the block RAM and DSP blocks that CORDIC leaves idle. Sect. 20.5, and see [[4.2) Table-Based Function Evaluation]].
3. **Generic polynomial evaluator**: apply the machinery of [[4.3) Polynomial Approximation]] to sine directly. Sect. 20.6.

A fourth point on the line, "reduced iterations CORDIC" [THH89], rotates with CORDIC only until the residual $\theta_r$ is small enough that $\sin\theta_r\approx\theta_r$ and $\cos\theta_r\approx1$, then finishes with two small multiplications — that is Sect. 20.4.6 below, and it is really a hybrid of 1 and 2.

## Argument reduction

Shared by all three architectures, and exact — every step is a symmetry of the circle applied to a *binary* angle, i.e. a bit permutation or a negation. Name the three leading bits of $X$ (Fig. 20.5, p. 605): $X_0=s$ (sign), $X_{-1}=q$ (quadrant), $X_{-2}=o$ (octant). Let $Y\in\left[0,\frac14\right)$ be what remains, and define

$$Y'=\begin{cases}Y & \text{if }o=0\\[2pt] \frac14-Y & \text{if }o=1\end{cases}\tag{20.21}$$

so $Y'\in\left[0,\frac14\right)$, i.e. an angle $\pi Y'\in[0,\pi/4)$ — one octant. The reflection when $o=1$ is symmetry about the main diagonal, identities (20.10)–(20.11). The eight $sqo$ patterns then reconstruct the full-circle result by swapping $\sin\leftrightarrow\cos$ and negating (Fig. 20.6, p. 606): $\cos$ and $\sin$ of $\pi X$ are always $\pm\sin(\pi Y')$ and $\pm\cos(\pi Y')$ in some order.

Once guard bits exist, both the subtraction $\frac14-Y$ and the final negation are better done as **bitwise complement** — a row of XORs — rather than as true two's-complement negation ($-x=\bar x+1$), because the $+1$ costs a carry propagation on the critical path. Each such shortcut costs exactly one ulp on the *guard-bit* datapath, which is usually absorbed without even changing $g$ (p. 613).

## CORDIC computing just right

### Description of the algorithm

A rotation by $\theta$ is the matrix

$$\begin{pmatrix}x'\\y'\end{pmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}}_{R}\begin{pmatrix}x\\y\end{pmatrix}\tag{20.22}$$

**Factor out $\cos\theta$** — legitimate because $\cos\theta\ne0$ on the reduced range — using $\tan\theta=\sin\theta/\cos\theta$:

$$R=\cos\theta\begin{pmatrix}1&-\tan\theta\\ \tan\theta&1\end{pmatrix}\tag{20.23}$$

Now decompose the angle as a sum of micro-rotations, $\theta=\sum_{i}\alpha_i$, which is exactly (20.14)/(20.13): composition of rotations. Choose

$$\alpha_i=\pm\arctan 2^{-i}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tan\alpha_i=\pm2^{-i}$$

and (20.23) becomes

$$R'=\cos\alpha_i\begin{pmatrix}1&\mp2^{-i}\\ \pm2^{-i}&1\end{pmatrix}\tag{20.25}$$

> [!important] The matrix has no multiplier in it
> $\arctan2^{-i}$ is a horrible constant, but $\tan(\arctan 2^{-i})=2^{-i}$ is a **right shift**. Multiplying by $\begin{pmatrix}1&-2^{-i}\\2^{-i}&1\end{pmatrix}$ is therefore two shifts and two additions and nothing else. That is the whole of CORDIC: it buys a transcendental function with the hardware budget of an adder. In an *unrolled* pipeline the shift amount $i$ is fixed per stage, so the shifters are literally wiring — zero logic — and no [[2.6) Shifters]] hardware is instantiated at all. (In an *iterative* implementation $i$ varies, and you pay for two real barrel shifters, which is why the iterative variant is not simply "the same thing, slower".)

**Where the scale factor comes from.** Dropping $\cos\alpha_i$ from (20.25) is what makes the step multiplier-free, and it is not a rotation any more: it scales the modulus. By Pythagoras (20.1),

$$\cos\alpha_i=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tan^2\alpha_i}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+2^{-2i}}}$$

so each shift-and-add step magnifies the vector length by $\sqrt{1+2^{-2i}}$. Crucially **this factor does not depend on the sign $\pm$**, hence not on the data. The total growth over $n$ steps is the known constant

$$K=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{1+2^{-2i}}$$

and it is cancelled for free by initializing the vector at $1/K$ instead of $1$ — the initial vector is $\binom{1}{0}$, so a single hardwired constant absorbs it. The output scaling $(1-2^{\ell})$ of (20.19) rides along in the same constant, which is why it costs nothing.

**Why it converges.** The reachable set is $\left|\theta\right|\le\sum_i\arctan2^{-i}$; starting at $i=0$ this sum is $1.7433$ rad $=99.88^\circ$ (p. 607). Representability of every angle in that range needs the tail to dominate each term, $\arctan 2^{-i}\le\sum_{j>i}\arctan 2^{-j}$. Bound both sides with the alternating Taylor series of $\arctan$, whose terms decrease, so partial sums bracket:

$$
\arctan 2^{-i}\le 2^{-i}-\tfrac13 2^{-3i}+\tfrac15 2^{-5i},\qquad
\sum_{j>i}\arctan 2^{-j}\ \ge\ \sum_{j>i}\left(2^{-j}-\tfrac13 2^{-3j}\right)=2^{-i}-\tfrac{1}{21}2^{-3i}
$$

using $\sum_{j>i}2^{-j}=2^{-i}$ (geometric series) and $\sum_{j>i}2^{-3j}=\frac{8}{7}2^{-3i-3}=\frac{1}{7}2^{-3i}$. The condition reduces to $\left(\tfrac13-\tfrac1{21}\right)2^{-3i}\ge\tfrac15 2^{-5i}$, i.e. $0.286\ge0.2\cdot2^{-2i}$, true for all $i\ge0$ with room to spare. So a greedy sign choice drives the residual to zero, and $|Z_{i+1}|\le\sum_{j>i}\alpha_j\approx\alpha_i$.

Argument reduction has already brought us to $\pi/4=45^\circ$, and the reach from $i=1$ is $1.7433-0.7854=0.958$ rad $=54.9^\circ$. **The iteration can start at $i=1$**, saving the $45^\circ$ stage; textbooks that start at $i=0$ (for the full range) use a different $C_1$.

The same statement as an interval: entering stage $i$, the residual is trapped in $\pm\sum_{j\ge i}\alpha_j$, and that bracket halves per stage — which is simultaneously the convergence proof and the reason the $Z$ adder loses one MSB per stage (Sect. 20.4.5 below).

Three values are iterated in parallel: an angle $Z_i$ initialized to $Y'$ and driven to $0$, and a vector $(C_i,S_i)$ starting on the $x$ axis and rotated by the same amounts in the opposite direction. As $Z_i\to0$, $(C_i,S_i)\to(\cos\pi Y',\sin\pi Y')$:

$$
\begin{cases}
C_1=\dfrac{1}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}\sqrt{1+2^{-2i}}}\\ S_1=0\\ Z_1=Y'
\end{cases}
\qquad\forall i\ge1\ \begin{cases}
d_i=+1\ \text{if}\ Z_i>0,\ \text{else}\ -1\\
C_{i+1}=C_i-2^{-i}d_iS_i\\
S_{i+1}=S_i+2^{-i}d_iC_i\\
Z_{i+1}=Z_i-d_i\arctan(2^{-i})
\end{cases}\tag{20.26–20.27}
$$

Because $Z$ is a *binary* angle, every constant $\arctan(2^{-i})$ is pre-divided by $\pi$ — a change of constant, no hardware.

The algebra above is one picture: the vector $(C_i,S_i)$ walking around the circle in steps of $\pm\arctan2^{-i}$, overshooting and correcting, growing by $\sqrt{1+2^{-2i}}$ each step. ### Overall error budget

With $C_i,S_i$ the infinitely precise values of (20.26)–(20.27), $\widetilde C_i,\widetilde S_i$ what the architecture computes on a $g$-guard-bit datapath, and $S$ the final rounded output, split the total error into three (the standard decomposition of [[1.2) Error Accuracy]], by inserting and cancelling intermediate terms):

$$\delta_\text{total}=S-\sin\pi Y'=\underbrace{(S-\widetilde S_n)}_{\delta_\text{final round}}+\underbrace{(\widetilde S_n-S_n)}_{\delta_\text{round}}+\underbrace{(S_n-\sin\pi Y')}_{\delta_\text{approx}}\tag{20.28–20.29}$$

The final rounding is one half-ulp, $\delta_\text{final round}\le2^{\ell-1}$. Last-bit accuracy ($|\delta_\text{total}|<2^{\ell}$) therefore demands

$$\delta_\text{round}+\delta_\text{approx}<2^{\ell-1}$$

and the design splits that budget in half: $2^{\ell-2}$ for stopping the iteration, $2^{\ell-2}$ for all the rounding. **The whole architecture is derived from this one line** — the left half fixes $n$, the right half fixes $g$.

### Approximation error and the number of iterations

For large $i$, $\arctan 2^{-i}\approx2^{-i}$ (Taylor), so iteration $i$ removes from $Z_i$ a quantity of order $2^{-i}$, and adds to $C_i,S_i$ a quantity smaller than $2^{-i}$ since $|C_i|,|S_i|<1$. **Each iteration buys at least one bit.** Hence choosing

$$n=-\ell+2$$

gives $\delta_\text{approx}\le2^{\ell-2}$: exactly the quarter-ulp half of the budget. The pipeline depth of an unrolled CORDIC is thus *linear in the precision* — the fact that later kills it on delay.

### Rounding errors

Let $u=2^{\ell-g}$ be the ulp of the internal datapath.

**$Z$ datapath.** Each stage adds a correctly rounded $\arctan 2^{-i}$, absolute error $\le u/2$, and the errors merely accumulate: after $i$ stages, $\le i\cdot u/2$.

**$C,S$ datapath.** The choice is truncate or round-to-nearest (Sect. 3.1.4, [[Rounding]]). Truncation computes

$$
\widetilde S_{i+1}=\widetilde S_i+\left\lfloor2^{-i}d_i\widetilde C_i\right\rfloor_{\ell-g}
\qquad\text{with}\qquad\left|\delta_i^{(\text{add})}\right|<2^{\ell-g}=1u\tag{20.32–20.33}
$$

Round-to-nearest adds a half-ulp before truncating (20.34) — but that involves *two* roundings of $u/2$ each (the truncation of $2^{-i}\widetilde C_i$ to $2^{\ell-g-1}$, then the rounding of the sum back to $2^{\ell-g}$), so it also totals $1u$ while making every adder one bit wider. **Truncate.** Same accuracy, less hardware.

Errors compound rather than merely add, because each stage feeds a *wrong* $\widetilde C_i$ into the next shift. Subtracting the exact recurrence from the computed one and regrouping,

$$\delta_{\text{round},i+1}=\underbrace{\left\lfloor2^{-i}d_i\widetilde C_i\right\rfloor_{\ell-g}-2^{-i}d_i\widetilde C_i}_{\text{new truncation, }\le1u}+\underbrace{(\widetilde S_i-S_i)}_{\text{inherited}}+\underbrace{2^{-i}d_i(\widetilde C_i-C_i)}_{\text{inherited, shifted}}\tag{20.35–20.38}$$

Assuming by symmetry the same bound $\bar\delta_{\text{round},i}$ on both datapaths, in ulps:

$$\boxed{\ \bar\delta_{\text{round},i+1}=1+(2^{-i}+1)\,\bar\delta_{\text{round},i}\ }\tag{20.39}$$

Initialization: $\widetilde S_1=S_1=0$ exactly, and $\bar\delta_{\text{round},1}=\widetilde C_1-C_1\le u/2$, i.e. half an ulp, from rounding the constant $C_1$ into the working format. Evaluate (20.39) in software up to $\bar\delta_\text{round}=\bar\delta_{\text{round},n}$; the growth is close to $2\times$ per iteration for small $i$ and settles to $+1$ per iteration.

Then impose the budget, $\bar\delta_\text{round}\cdot2^{\ell-g}<2^{\ell-2}$, which gives

$$g=2+\left\lfloor\log_2(\bar\delta_\text{round})\right\rfloor\tag{20.41–20.42}$$

> [!warning] The $d_i$ decisions can diverge, and you must not try to prevent it
> If $Z_i$ is close to $0$, rounding may make the real architecture choose a different $d_i$ from the ideal sequence, and the two computations part company. Forcing agreement would require prohibitive accuracy. The correct move is to redefine the reference: for the purpose of the error analysis, take as "ideal" the infinitely precise sequence that makes the *same decisions* as the architecture. Both branches converge to the same limit anyway, since either sign choice at a near-zero residual leaves a residual the tail can still absorb (p. 612).

### Reduced $Z_i$ datapath

$|Z_{i+1}|\le\sum_{j>i}\alpha_j\approx\alpha_i<2^{-i}$, so **the MSB of $Z_i$ is $-i$**: one leading bit dies every iteration. In an unrolled implementation the width of stage $i$'s $Z$ adder can therefore be $-\ell+g-i+1$ rather than $-\ell+g+1$. Summing the triangle instead of the rectangle nearly halves the $Z$ datapath, and since $Z$ is one of three equal datapaths, cuts total area by about $1/6$ (p. 613).

On FPGAs the $C_i$ and $S_i$ lines of (20.27) each pack into **one LUT per bit** while still using the fast carry logic ([[Lookup-Tables]]), which makes the cost formula almost exact:

$$\text{LUTs}\approx\underbrace{2(-\ell+g)(-\ell+2)}_{C\ \text{and}\ S,\ n\ \text{stages}}+\underbrace{\tfrac12(-\ell+g)^2}_{\text{triangular }Z}\ \approx\ 2.5\,(-\ell+g)^2$$

**Quadratic in the precision.** The optimization also shortens the critical path in the early iterations: the loop-carried dependency is $Z_i\to d_i\to Z_{i+1}$, and a shorter $Z_i$ makes $d_i$ available before $C_{i+1},S_{i+1}$ finish, letting two successive carry propagations overlap. The gain shrinks in later iterations — unfortunately, since that is where $d_i$ is fastest.

### Replacing half the CORDIC iterations with a small multiplier

Stop iterating as soon as the *remaining* rotation by the residual binary angle $Z_i$ can be applied directly. Apply (20.14) with the Taylor approximations $\cos(\pi Z_i)\approx1$ and $\sin(\pi Z_i)\approx\pi Z_i$:

$$\begin{cases}C_\text{final}=C_i-\pi Z_i\,S_i\\ S_\text{final}=S_i+\pi Z_i\,C_i\end{cases}\tag{20.43}$$

This introduces a *new* approximation term, the Taylor remainders, of size at most $\frac{Z_i^2}{2}$. Rule of thumb: it is below $2^{\ell-3}$ as soon as $Z_i<2^{\ell/2-1}$, i.e. at

$$i=\left\lfloor-\ell/2\right\rfloor+1$$

so **half the iterations disappear**. What replaces them: $Z_i$ is $\lfloor-\ell/2\rfloor+1+g$ bits, multiplied by $\pi$ with a `FixRealCM` constant multiplier ([[Multiplication by Constants]]) accurate to one ulp, then two multiplications by $C_i$ and $S_i$. Truncating $C_i$ and $S_i$ to the same width as $\pi Z_i$ costs one more ulp and halves the multiplier area, leaving **two multipliers slightly wider than $-\ell/2$ bits**. In practice the signed $18\times18$ multipliers standard in FPGA [[DSP Blocks]] cover $\ell$ down to $-24$.

The initial scaling constant $C_1$ must be recomputed for the shorter product, and the error analysis regrown around the two new multiplications; the book leaves this as an exercise and points at the FloPoCo source.

## An architecture based on tables and multipliers

Designed to use the block memories and embedded multipliers that CORDIC leaves untouched. Interestingly, even when synthesized *in logic only*, its LUT cost is comparable to CORDIC's while its timing is much better [DIS13] — because it exposes bit-level parallelism instead of a chain of $-\ell+2$ dependent additions.

### Algorithm

A **second argument reduction** splits the octant angle $Y'$ into its $\alpha$ most significant bits $A$ and the rest $Y_\text{red}\in[0,2^{-2-\alpha})$:

$$Y'=.\,00\;\underbrace{A}_{\alpha\ \text{bits}}\;\underbrace{Y_\text{red}}_{-\ell-2-\alpha\ \text{bits}}\tag{Fig. 20.10}$$

$A$ addresses a table of $\sin(\pi A)$ and $\cos(\pi A)$. For the small part, set $Z=\pi Y_\text{red}$ and take Taylor series. Recombine with (20.14):

$$\sin(\pi Y')=\sin(\pi A)\cos Z+\cos(\pi A)\sin Z,\qquad \cos(\pi Y')=\cos(\pi A)\cos Z-\sin(\pi A)\sin Z\tag{20.44–20.45}$$

Taylor beats the generic minimax polynomial of [[4.3) Polynomial Approximation]] here for two reasons: the sine series is odd and the cosine series is even, so a given degree costs **half the multiplications**; and up to degree 4 the coefficients are powers of two (free) or powers of two times $1/3$ (also cheap, Chap. 13). Keep two terms each:

$$\sin Z\approx Z-\frac{Z^3}{6},\qquad \cos Z\approx1-\frac{Z^2}{2}\tag{20.46–20.47}$$

Now the key rewriting: $\cos Z$ is very close to $1$, so instead of a full-width product $\sin(\pi A)\times\cos Z$, write $\sin(\pi A)\times\left(1-\frac{Z^2}{2}\right)=\sin(\pi A)-\sin(\pi A)\times\frac{Z^2}{2}$ — **a large multiplier becomes a much smaller one plus an addition**, because $\frac{Z^2}{2}$ has its MSB at $-2\alpha-1$. Hence

$$\sin(\pi Y')\approx\sin(\pi A)-\sin(\pi A)\frac{Z^2}{2}+\cos(\pi A)\left(Z-\frac{Z^3}{6}\right)\tag{20.48}$$
$$\cos(\pi Y')\approx\cos(\pi A)-\cos(\pi A)\frac{Z^2}{2}-\sin(\pi A)\left(Z-\frac{Z^3}{6}\right)\tag{20.49}$$

Four multipliers, two tables, and everything else is a bit heap ([[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]]).

**Constraint on $\alpha$ from the approximation error.** The truncated Taylor remainders are

$$\delta_{\text{Taylor}(\cos)}=\cos Z-\left(1-\tfrac{Z^2}{2}\right)\approx\tfrac{Z^4}{24},\qquad \delta_{\text{Taylor}(\sin)}=\sin Z-\left(Z-\tfrac{Z^3}{6}\right)\approx\tfrac{Z^5}{120}$$

With $Z=\pi Y_\text{red}<3.15\cdot2^{-2-\alpha}<2^{-\alpha}$: $\ \delta_{\text{Taylor}(\cos)}<2^{-4\alpha}/24<2^{-4\alpha-4}$ and $\delta_{\text{Taylor}(\sin)}<2^{-5\alpha}/120<2^{-5\alpha-6}$. In (20.44)–(20.45) these are multiplied by sines and cosines bounded by $1$, so they are **not amplified**. Requiring the dominant one below half an ulp, $2^{-4\alpha-4}<2^{\ell}$:

$$\alpha>-\frac{\ell}{4}-1\tag{20.52}$$

The table is addressed by a quarter of the input bits — an exponentially smaller table than a plain tabulation.

### Rounding error analysis and implementation details

Assume an intermediate precision $2^{\ell-g}$ as usual. The principle of computing just right is to **balance** the error terms: no part of the architecture should be dramatically more accurate than the rest, so every truncation is chosen so that its contribution to the final error is of order $2^{\ell-g}$.

Worked on one multiplier, $\sin(\pi A)\times\frac{Z^2}{2}$: both inputs are already approximate, so by the truncated-multiplier analysis of Sect. 8.1.3 ([[Truncated Multipliers]]) they should be truncated to the *same absolute* precision. With $X=\sin(\pi A)$ ($m_X=0$) and $Y=\frac{Z^2}{2}$ ($m_Y=-2\alpha-1$), constraint (8.10) reads

$$\ell-g\ \approx\ -2\alpha-1+\ell_X\ \approx\ 0+\ell_Y\tag{20.53}$$

so $\sin(\pi A)$ is truncated to $\ell_X=\ell-g+2\alpha+1$ and $\frac{Z^2}{2}$ to $\ell_Y=\ell-g$; the product's MSB is $-2\alpha-1$. The *same* $\sin(\pi A)$ multiplied by $Z-\frac{Z^3}{6}$ is truncated differently ($\ell_X=\ell-g+\alpha$), and when finally summed into the sine bit heap it must be full precision $\ell-g$. Each quantity is computed once at its highest required precision and truncated per use.

### Architectural details

- $\frac{Z^2}{2}$: either a truncated squarer ([[3.3) Squarers and Integer Powers]]) or a plain tabulation. **Tabulation is more accurate** — a half-ulp bound instead of a full ulp — so it wins whenever the table fits.
- $\frac{Z^3}{6}$ is tiny: its width is $-3\alpha-2-\ell+g$ bits, and with $\alpha>-\ell/4-1$ that is roughly a quarter of the input size plus the guard bits. Tabulate it for most practical $\ell$.
- For large sizes, an ad hoc architecture computes $Z-\frac{Z^3}{6}$ inside a **single bit heap**; only a few bits need dividing by 3, using the divide-by-3 variant of Chap. 13 ([[3.2) Division by Constants]]).

### Computing the number of guard bits

Count the error sources: one **half**-ulp per table, one ulp per truncation box, one ulp per truncated multiplier or squarer, one ulp for the XOR-based computation of $\frac14-y$, and one ulp lumping the higher-order terms and the scaled-down error contributions. Composed carefully, the architecture loses **up to 15 ulps**, hence

$$g=4$$

and — the striking part — **$g$ does not depend on $\ell$.** Contrast CORDIC, where $\bar\delta_\text{round}$ grows with the iteration count and so $g$ grows like $\log_2$ of the precision. The reason is structural: CORDIC's errors are fed back through $-\ell+2$ dependent stages and compound (20.39), while here they are a fixed, bounded number of independent terms summed once. Favourable variants replace a few ulps with half-ulps, but never enough to win a guard bit back.

### Special cases for small sizes

FloPoCo degrades gracefully as $\ell$ shrinks, in order of increasing input size: (1) plain table over the whole domain; (2) plain table over an octant, after the first argument reduction; (3) second argument reduction with first-order $\sin Z\approx Z$, $\cos Z\approx1$; (4) second order, $\sin Z\approx Z$, $\cos Z\approx1-\frac{Z^2}{2}$; (5) third order, the full architecture above. $g$ is adapted for each. Below some size, "computing just right" means *not building an architecture at all* — just tabulate.

## An architecture using a generic polynomial evaluator

The lazy option: wrap the generic table-and-DSP polynomial evaluator of Chap. 18 ([[4.3) Polynomial Approximation]]) in the argument reduction of Sect. 20.3. Two differences from the others. First, it takes an extra input bit $\sin/\overline{\cos}$ selecting *which* function to compute — a `FixSinOrCos`, not a fused `FixSinCos`, so an application needing both must instantiate it twice. Second, the reduction only goes down to a **quadrant**, $Y'\in[0,1/2)$, not an octant: the evaluator computes $\sin(\pi Y')$ on $[0,1/2)$ and the output is reconstructed from it and the bits $s$, $q$, $\sin/\overline{\cos}$. That trades a wider approximation interval (more segments or higher degree) for less reduction logic.

## FPGA mapping

The three architectures use three disjoint parts of the FPGA, which is the real reason the comparison is interesting.

- **CORDIC uses only the configurable logic.** The $C$/$S$ update packs one LUT per bit on the fast carry chain; the shifts are routing; the constants are hardwired. Zero block RAM, zero DSP. Fast carry logic genuinely favours CORDIC (p. 621), and it is why CORDIC has been in FPGA vendor core generators from the start [And98], [VKP02].
- **Table-and-multiplier uses block RAM and DSPs.** Multipliers go in logic for small sizes, as truncated DSPs for larger ones, or a mix. And because block memory comes in fixed granules, **a larger $\alpha$ than (20.52) requires is often free** — which shrinks the multipliers, and may even allow an order-1 architecture that drops the $\frac{Z^2}{2}$ term entirely. FloPoCo exploits this.
- **Iterative CORDIC costs more logic than Fig. 20.8 suggests.** With $i$ varying at runtime you need two real [[2.6) Shifters]] (barrel shifters) plus a table of the $\arctan(2^{-i})$ constants — more logic than the three adders they serve — and the reduced-$Z$ trick of Sect. 20.4.5 is no longer available. **Unrolled CORDIC is more efficient in throughput per area than iterative CORDIC**; iterative wins only on absolute area, when the throughput is not needed.

The numbers above are the cost *formulas* the text states, plus the one measured comparison it cites [DIS13]: with all tables and multipliers forced into FPGA logic, unrolled CORDIC and the table-and-multiplier architecture consume **very comparable resources**, but CORDIC is slower by a factor 2 (16 bits) to a factor 3 (32 bits) in logic delay. Device-level numbers need outside research. A proper VLSI comparison against redundant-arithmetic CORDIC variants [Meh+09] "remains to be done" (p. 614, footnote).

**Pick the table-and-multiplier architecture** for a pipelined FPGA sin/cos: same area, 2–3× the speed, a constant $g=4$, and it spends the block RAM and DSPs that would otherwise idle. **Pick unrolled CORDIC** only where multipliers and memories are genuinely unavailable or already saturated — the "no multiplier at all" property is its whole argument, and on a modern FPGA that argument has largely stopped applying. **Pick iterative CORDIC** when the throughput requirement is low: several cycles per result, far fewer resources, and the only variant here that is small rather than fast. **Pick the generic polynomial evaluator** when you need one function, not both, or when you want one code path for many functions rather than a hand-tuned operator.

The ASIC picture is explicitly open (p. 621): the FPGA verdict leans on fast carry logic (pro-CORDIC) *and* on tables and small multipliers mapping efficiently into LUTs (pro-table), and neither argument transfers cleanly. Faster CORDIC using redundant number systems ([[Redundant Number Systems]]) could close the delay gap at higher area cost; the chapter does not study it.

## Worked example: four CORDIC iterations, $\ell=-8$

Input $X=0.00110100_2=0.203125$. The leading bits are $s=q=o=0$, so $sqo=000$: no reconstruction, and $Y'=Y=X=0.203125$. The target angle is $\pi Y'=0.63813$ rad $=36.56^\circ$. (For legibility the $(1-2^{\ell})$ output scaling is dropped; folding it into $C_1$ changes nothing structurally.)

Constants, in the binary-angle scale $\frac1\pi\arctan2^{-i}$, correctly rounded to 8 fractional bits:

| $i$ | $\frac1\pi\arctan2^{-i}$ | rounded | binary |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $0.1475836$ | $38/256$ | $0.00100110$ |
| 2 | $0.0779770$ | $20/256$ | $0.00010100$ |
| 3 | $0.0395815$ | $10/256$ | $0.00001010$ |
| 4 | $0.0198668$ | $5/256$ | $0.00000101$ |

Scale factor for $n=4$: $K=\prod_{i=1}^{4}\sqrt{1+2^{-2i}}=1.1636771$, so $C_1=1/K=0.8593755\to220/256=0.11011100_2$.

Initial state: $C_1=0.11011100$, $S_1=0.00000000$, $Z_1=0.00110100$.

| $i$ | $d_i$ | $\lfloor2^{-i}C_i\rfloor$ | $\lfloor2^{-i}S_i\rfloor$ | $C_{i+1}$ | $S_{i+1}$ | $Z_{i+1}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $+$ | $110/256$ | $0$ | $\mathtt{0.11011100}$ | $\mathtt{0.01101110}$ | $\mathtt{0.00001110}$ |
| 2 | $+$ | $55/256$ | $27/256$ | $\mathtt{0.11000001}$ | $\mathtt{0.10100101}$ | $-6/256$ |
| 3 | $-$ | $24/256$ | $20/256$ | $\mathtt{0.11010101}$ | $\mathtt{0.10001101}$ | $\mathtt{0.00000100}$ |
| 4 | $+$ | $13/256$ | $8/256$ | $\mathtt{0.11001101}$ | $\mathtt{0.10011010}$ | $-1/256$ |

Reading the residual column: $52\to14\to-6\to4\to-1$ (in units of $2^{-8}$). **The angle residual shrinks by about one bit per iteration and alternates in sign** — that is the greedy sign choice of (20.27) working, and it is the concrete form of the convergence proof above.

Tracing one row in full, $i=3$ with $d_3=-1$ (because $Z_3=-6/256<0$):

$$C_4=C_3+\left\lfloor2^{-3}S_3\right\rfloor=\mathtt{0.11000001}+\left\lfloor\tfrac{165}{8}\right\rfloor=193+20=213 = \mathtt{0.11010101}_2$$
$$S_4=S_3-\left\lfloor2^{-3}C_3\right\rfloor=\mathtt{0.10100101}-\left\lfloor\tfrac{193}{8}\right\rfloor=165-24=141=\mathtt{0.10001101}_2$$
$$Z_4=Z_3+\tfrac1\pi\arctan2^{-3}=-6+10=4=\mathtt{0.00000100}_2$$

Note $\lfloor165/8\rfloor=20$, discarding $0.625$ ulp: that is the truncation of (20.32), bounded by $1u$, exactly as the error analysis assumes.

**Final error.** $C_5=205/256=0.80078125$ and $S_5=154/256=0.6015625$, against $\cos(0.63813)=0.803213$ and $\sin(0.63813)=0.595695$:

$$\delta_C=-0.62\ \mathrm{ulp},\qquad \delta_S=+1.50\ \mathrm{ulp}\qquad(\mathrm{ulp}=2^{-8})$$

$S$ misses last-bit accuracy, and the budget says exactly why. Stopping at $n=4$ leaves $|Z_5|=1$ ulp of *angle*, i.e. an unrotated $\pi\cdot2^{-8}$ rad, worth $\approx2.5$ ulp on $S$ — the specification demands $n=-\ell+2=10$ iterations, not 4. Rounding pulls the other way by $\approx1$ ulp, within the recurrence's bound $\bar\delta_{\text{round},5}=5.9$ ulp from (20.39) with $\bar\delta_{\text{round},1}=0.5$. **Both halves of the error budget are visible in a four-row table**, and both were sized by the same $2^{\ell-1}$ constraint.
