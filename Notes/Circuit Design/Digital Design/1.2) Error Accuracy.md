An application-specific operator has no standard to hide behind: you write its accuracy specification yourself, and every architectural choice inside it is then a search for the cheapest way to meet that number. This note fixes the vocabulary that every other arithmetic note in this vault uses to state its accuracy.

## Specification

The template that every operator note here follows:

- **Input** $X$ in a fixed-point format $\mathrm{ufix}(m_X,\ell_X)$ or $\mathrm{sfix}(m_X,\ell_X)$ — see [[1.1) Number Formats]].
- **Output** $R$ in $\mathrm{ufix}(m_R,\ell_R)$, whose quantum is $u=\mathrm{ulp}=2^{\ell_R}$.
- **Accuracy**: $\delta=\max_X|R(X)-f(X)| < u$ (*faithful*), unless a section says otherwise.
- **Optimized**: area / latency / energy *at fixed accuracy* — accuracy is a constraint, not an objective.

Fixing the output format fixes $u$, and fixing $u$ fixes the accuracy target. That is the whole discipline: **the format is the specification.**

> [!important] The last bit is a shared budget
> The final rounding to the output format costs $u/2$ and that cost cannot be negotiated away. So an operator specified to $\delta<u$ has exactly $u/2$ left over, and **the approximation error and every internal rounding error must be paid out of that same $u/2$**. Spend it all on guard bits and the approximation must be near-exact; spend it all on a fancier approximation and you cannot afford any internal truncation. "Computing just right" is the statement that this budget should be *spent*, not left on the table: a sub-component that is more accurate than its share of the budget is wasted area.

## Absolute and Relative Error

> **Definition 3.1 (absolute error).** $\;\delta(X)=\widehat{f}(X)-f(X)$, where $\widehat{f}$ is the function the hardware actually computes and $f$ the ideal one.
> **Definition 3.2 (relative error).** $\;\varepsilon(X)=\dfrac{\widehat{f}(X)-f(X)}{f(X)}$.

The error is a **function of $X$**, not a number; it can be plotted. What gets quoted is the worst case over the input domain,

$$\delta=\max_{\forall X}|\delta(X)|,\qquad \varepsilon=\max_{\forall X}|\varepsilon(X)|$$

Worst-case bounds are chosen over average error or SNR because they give the strongest guarantee and, crucially, because they **compose** — see [Error Analysis](#error-analysis). Absolute error is the natural currency for fixed point (where the quantum is constant); relative error is the natural currency for floating point (where it is not).

> [!note] A terminology trap the book apologises for
> "Absolute" carries two meanings in the same breath: *absolute* error as opposed to *relative* error, and the *absolute value* in $\max|\cdot|$. An operator can have a worst-case bound on its absolute relative error. Read the qualifier, not the word. (p. 67, footnote 1)

Discretizing the input is never the problem — a discrete $X$ is still a real number, so $f(X)$ is well defined. The whole subject is the discretization of the *output* value $f(X)$.

## Unit in the Last Place (ulp)

> **Definition 3.3 (ulp, fixed point).** For a $\mathrm{ufix}(m,\ell)$ or $\mathrm{sfix}(m,\ell)$ format, $\;\mathrm{ulp}=2^{\ell}$ — the weight of the LSB.

Often written $u$. It is a **constant of the format**: the ulp of an integer format is $1$. This is what lets a specification be format-relative: an operator whose output is $\mathrm{ufix}(m,\ell)$ with $\delta<2^{\ell}$ is *accurate to 1 ulp*, and you need not repeat the format to say so.

In floating point the ulp is no longer constant. For $\mathrm{Nfloat}(w_F,w_E)$ a number $X$ with exponent $E_X$ has $\mathrm{ulp}(X)=2^{E_X-w_F}$, constant on each **binade** $[2^k,2^{k+1})$ and jumping at binade boundaries. Extending $\mathrm{ulp}(\cdot)$ to all of $\mathbb{R}$ — needed so that $\mathrm{ulp}(f(X))$ makes sense for the *exact* value — forces a choice at exactly those boundaries. The book adopts what Muller concludes is the best one:

> **Definition 3.4 (ulp, floating-point context).** If $x$ lies strictly between two consecutive finite floating-point numbers $a$ and $b$, then $\mathrm{ulp}(x)=|b-a|$. **Otherwise**, $\mathrm{ulp}(x)$ is the distance between the two finite floating-point numbers nearest $x$. And $\mathrm{ulp}(\mathrm{NaN})=\mathrm{NaN}$.

The "otherwise" is doing real work: it covers $x$ that *is itself* a representable number (in particular the binade boundaries $2^k$, where the two neighbours are unequally spaced) and $x$ larger in magnitude than the largest representable number. Get this clause wrong and half-ulp claims stop being true near powers of two. With Definition 3.4, round-to-nearest is a half-ulp operation everywhere except on overflow to infinity.

## Rounding

Write $\lfloor Y\rceil_\ell$ for $Y$ rounded to the nearest multiple of $2^\ell$. In fixed point the worst-case absolute error is $\delta=\tfrac12\,\mathrm{ulp}$, and unlike truncation's bound **it is attained**, at the *ties* — values exactly halfway between two representable numbers. With $\ell=0$ (integers, $u=1$): $1.17\to1$ with $\delta=0.17$; $1.53\to2$ with $\delta=0.47$; $1.49\to1$ with $\delta=0.49$; and $1.5\to1$ or $2$, either way $\delta=0.5=\tfrac12\mathrm{ulp}$. On $\mathrm{ufix}(-1,-12)$ the bound $2^{-13}$ is attained at e.g. $0.25+2^{-13}$.

Ties must be resolved to make rounding a function. IEEE 754-1985 chose *ties to even* (LSB of the significand forced to 0), which is unbiased but **expensive**: the tie case has to be detected and handled specially. For application-specific fixed point the book uses the cheap alternative

$$\lfloor Y\rceil_\ell \;=\; \big\lfloor\, Y + 2^{\ell-1} \,\big\rfloor_\ell \tag{3.1}$$

**Why (3.1) is round-to-nearest-ties-up.** Write $Y=q2^{\ell}+r$ with $0\le r<2^{\ell}$. Then $\lfloor Y+2^{\ell-1}\rfloor_\ell = q2^\ell$ iff $r+2^{\ell-1}<2^{\ell}$, i.e. iff $r<2^{\ell-1}$ — round down when strictly below the midpoint, round up at or above it. The floor $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor_\ell$ is *free* (drop every bit of weight $<2^\ell$); the whole cost is the single added bit of weight one half ulp.

The catch is that (3.1) rounds ties **up**, always — including for negative numbers in two's complement, so $-1.5\to-1$. Symmetry around zero is lost, which ties-to-even preserves (pp. 69–70).

**The first trade-off.** Suppose $f(X)$ is computed exactly but on too many bits.

| Option | Error bound | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| truncate to $\ell$ | $<1\,\mathrm{ulp}$, never attained | free (drop bits) |
| round to nearest at $\ell$ via (3.1) | $\le\tfrac12\,\mathrm{ulp}$, attained | one addition, i.e. a carry propagation across the output |
| truncate to $\ell-1$ | $\le\tfrac12\cdot 2^{\ell}$ | free, but the result is 1 bit wider |

The last two rows both deliver an error bound of $2^{\ell-1}$; you are choosing between one adder and one extra output bit that every downstream consumer must carry. **The escape hatch**: if the rounding half-ulp $2^{\ell-1}$ can be injected *for free* somewhere earlier in the datapath — as a constant column in a [[2.3.1) Bit Heaps|bit heap]], or on an unused carry-in of an adder that is already there — then round-to-nearest costs exactly what truncation costs and wins without discussion. This trick recurs throughout the book (p. 70).

**Bias** (a direct consequence of the definitions, not a separate result). Truncation gives $\lfloor Y\rfloor_\ell \le Y$ always, so $\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}}\in(-u,0]$: the error is one-signed, with mean $-u/2$ for $Y$ uniform. Over $N$ accumulated truncations the *bound* grows like $N u$ but the *expected* error grows like $-Nu/2$ — a systematic drift, not noise. Round-to-nearest gives $\delta\in(-u/2,+u/2]$, symmetric apart from the tie rule, so its mean error is $O(u\cdot\Pr[\text{tie}])$ rather than $O(u)$. This is exactly why IEEE 754 preferred ties-to-even for "large computations" (p. 69).

## Rounding and Overflow in Fixed-Point Libraries

`fixed_pkg` for VHDL and `ap_fixed` / `ac_fixed` for C++ HLS always offer at least round-to-nearest and round-toward-$-\infty$ (truncation), and the trade-off above is precisely what the mode switch buys you.

They also offer **directed** modes — round toward zero, round away from zero, round toward $+\infty$ — which in fixed point are *not* implementable by truncation. They carry the same 1-ulp worst-case error as truncation yet cost **more than round-to-nearest-ties-up**: on top of the rounding addition they must detect the case where all the discarded bits are zero ($17.01$ rounds up to $18$, but $17.00$ must stay $17$). So a directed mode is simultaneously less accurate and more expensive; reach for one only for a real reason, such as computing safe interval bounds (p. 71).

**Overflow** has its own switch: *wrap* (truncate the overflowing bits — free in hardware, numerically catastrophic) or *saturation* / clipping (map out-of-range values to the extreme representable one — numerically much better, especially in signal processing, but costs at least one multiplexer per operation). In an ASIC or FPGA you also have a third option unavailable in software: run a **wider, overflow-free internal datapath and saturate only once at the end**. The book's own stance is to use range and error analysis to make overflow impossible by construction, so the issue rarely surfaces (p. 71).

## Half-Unit Biased (HUB) Format

HUB adds an **implicit constant half-ulp** to a fixed-point representation: the stored code is a $\mathrm{fix}(m,\ell)$ bit vector, and the value it denotes is that code plus $2^{\ell-1}$, i.e. an infinite-precision trailing $1$ at position $\ell-1$. Formalised by Hormigo and Villalba.

| HUB code | represented binary value | decimal |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `0.11` | `0.111` | $0.875$ |
| `0.10` | `0.101` | $0.625$ |
| `0.01` | `0.011` | $0.375$ |
| `0.00` | `0.001` | $0.125$ |
| `1.11` | `1.111` | $-0.125$ |
| `1.10` | `1.101` | $-0.375$ |
| `1.01` | `1.011` | $-0.625$ |
| `1.00` | `1.001` | $-0.875$ |

*signed $\mathrm{HUB}(1,-2)$, after Table 3.1, p. 72. The third fractional bit is the implicit half-ulp — it is not stored.*

One drawback, two payoffs.

- **Drawback**: zero is not representable.
- **Payoff 1**: the representable set is symmetric about zero, so **negation is bitwise inversion** — no carry propagation, unlike two's complement. Read it off the table: invert `0.11` to get `1.00`, and $0.875\mapsto-0.875$.
- **Payoff 2**: rounding *any* binary number to the nearest HUB number is **plain truncation**, hence free. Rounding $0.1000000\ldots$, $0.100001$, $0.101111$ and $0.1011111\ldots$ to $\mathrm{HUB}(1,-2)$ all truncate to code `0.10` $=0.625$, with errors $-2^{-3}$, $-2^{-3}+2^{-6}$, $2^{-3}-2^{-6}$, $2^{-3}-\epsilon$ respectively — all within the half-ulp bound $2^{-3}$ (Table 3.2, p. 72).

So HUB dissolves the trade-off of the previous section: half-ulp accuracy at truncation cost. Better still, rounding a $\mathrm{HUB}(m,\ell_1)$ value to $\mathrm{HUB}(m,\ell_2)$ with $\ell_2>\ell_1$ **never attains** the half-ulp bound; by the symmetry of the representable set the exact worst case is

$$\delta \;=\; 2^{\ell_2-1}-2^{\ell_1-1}$$

which is the strict inequality faithfulness needs, for free. The book does not build whole systems in HUB — it uses HUB as an **internal** format when it improves the cost/accuracy trade-off, e.g. in FIR filters, [[5.1) CORDIC]] rotators, and [[4.2.1) Multipartite Methods|multipartite]] tables (p. 73).

## Rounding to the Nearest When an Approximation Is Involved

Take $f(X)=\pi X$ with $X\in\mathrm{ufix}(-1,\ell)$ so $0\le X<1$, and output $\mathrm{ufix}(1,\ell)$ since $\pi<4$. The binary expansion of $\pi$ is infinite, so "compute exactly, then round" is not an option — it would take unbounded work. You must approximate, and that creates a **second** error term beside the rounding.

Use $\widetilde{\pi}=\lfloor\pi\rceil_\ell$, so

$$\delta_\pi=\widetilde{\pi}-\pi,\qquad |\delta_\pi|\le 2^{\ell-1} \tag{3.2, 3.3}$$

An exact multiplier on $X$ and $\widetilde\pi$ then commits an approximation error

$$
\delta_{\mathrm{approx}}(X)=\widetilde{\pi}X-\pi X=(\widetilde{\pi}-\pi)X,\qquad
|\delta_{\mathrm{approx}}(X)|=|\widetilde\pi-\pi|\cdot|X|\le 2^{\ell-1} \tag{3.4}
$$

using $|X|<1$. Its output sits on $\mathrm{ufix}(1,2\ell)$ — the products of the two LSBs land at weight $2^{2\ell}$ — so a **second**, final rounding is needed, with $\delta_{\mathrm{round}}(X)=|R(X)-\widetilde\pi X|<2^{\ell-1}$. Now decompose the total error by inserting and cancelling the intermediate value $\widetilde\pi X$:

$$\delta(X)=R(X)-\pi X=\underbrace{\big(R(X)-\widetilde{\pi}X\big)}_{\delta_{\mathrm{round}}}+\underbrace{\big(\widetilde{\pi}X-\pi X\big)}_{\delta_{\mathrm{approx}}}$$

and by the triangle inequality $|\delta(X)|<2^{\ell-1}+2^{\ell-1}=2^{\ell}=1\,\mathrm{ulp}$. **Faithful, but not round-to-nearest**, which would require $|\delta(X)|\le 2^{\ell-1}$. Two half-ulps do not fit in one half-ulp.

A finite correctly-rounded architecture *does* exist here — tabulate all $2^{w_X}$ correctly-rounded products — and the table-filling algorithm terminates precisely because $\pi$ is transcendental: no $\pi X$ is rational, while every tie $\tfrac{n}{2^{\ell-1}}$ is, so no product is ever exactly a tie. To get there with the multiplier architecture you must widen $\widetilde\pi$ to at least the precision that table-filling ever needed, which buys you a bigger multiplier. See [[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]].

> [!note] The general statement
> As soon as a computation involves *any* approximation, a 1-ulp bound is far easier than round-to-nearest. Every internal error term can be driven arbitrarily small by internal precision — but the **final rounding alone contributes one half ulp**, which is the entire round-to-nearest budget. (p. 75)

This is why IEEE 754 can mandate round-to-nearest for $+,-,\times,\div,\sqrt{\;}$ and nothing else: for those, $\delta_{\mathrm{approx}}$ simply *does not exist*. A finite $\times$ finite product is exact before rounding. A quotient like $1/3$ has an infinite but **periodic** expansion, and the paper-and-pencil remainder decides the rounding in finite time ([[2.5) Dividers]]). For general special functions no such number-theoretic result exists — that is the **Table Maker's Dilemma**, and current implementations are either not correctly rounded or not proven to terminate (p. 76).

## Faithful Rounding

> **Definition 3.5 (last-bit accuracy).** An operator is **faithful**, equivalently **last-bit accurate**, when its absolute error bound is *strictly* smaller than the ulp of its output: $\;\delta<u$.

The output format already forces $\delta\ge u/2$ on any operator, so $\delta<u$ is only a factor of two off optimal — a tight specification, not a loose one. Two consequences:

- A faithful operator returns **one of the two representable numbers bracketing** the exact $f(X)$.
- If $f(X)$ *is* representable, a faithful operator returns it **exactly**. This is where the strictness of $\delta<u$ earns its keep: at $\delta=u$ exactly, the neighbouring value would also be admissible.

Purists object to the name "faithful **rounding**": a rounding ought to be a *function*, and as the upper panel shows, a faithful operator may legitimately return either of two values for the same input. It is an accuracy *constraint*, not a rounding mode. The book uses the term anyway, and so does this vault.

## Last-Bit Accuracy vs. Rounding to the Nearest

Given an application-level accuracy target $\delta_{\mathrm{target}}=2^{k}$, there are two ways to hit it:

1. a **faithful** operator on a format with $u=2^{k}$, or
2. a **correctly rounded** operator on a format with $u=2^{k+1}$ — one bit narrower.

Both deliver $2^k$. Option 1 is usually cheaper — faithful truncated multipliers ([[2.4.3) Truncated Multipliers]]) save substantial resources exactly by exploiting this slack. But option 1's output is one bit wider, and that bit propagates into every downstream consumer. Which wins is genuinely contextual (p. 77).

## Using the Format to Specify the Accuracy

Is $1$ ulp special? Honestly, no: $0.75$ ulp would be slightly more accurate and slightly more expensive with no change of technique, and there are threshold-effect situations where a $1.5$-ulp operator is the better choice — provided that $1.5$ ulp is carried into the global error analysis. The bound that is **fundamentally** harder is $0.5$ ulp, for the reason derived above.

The reason to standardise on $1$ ulp anyway is interface economy: once the user has given the output format, the ulp is determined, hence the accuracy is determined, and an operator generator needs **no accuracy argument at all**. Most [[7.1) FloPoCo]] operators follow this convention. It removes a degree of freedom that was mostly a burden, and it makes design-space exploration meaningful — you can only compare architectures that are numerically equivalent (pp. 77–78).

## Parametric Designs

Parameters come in two fuzzy kinds. **External** parameters expose the operator to its environment: input and output formats, plus knobs for cost-versus-performance trade-offs. **Internal** parameters are *deduced* from the external ones — in a floating-point adder parameterised by significand width $w_F$, internal signals of width $w_F+1$, $w_F+3$, $\lceil\log_2(w_F+2)\rceil$.

The advice is aggressive: **when you face an architectural design choice, do not make it — capture it as a new internal parameter.** The single cost is slightly more complex generator code. The returns:

- a much finer understanding of how design choices interact;
- clean separation between *generating* an architecture (a hardware problem) and *choosing* one (a software and algorithmic problem);
- design-space exploration, hence optimisation, becomes possible at all;
- future-proofing — a choice can be reassessed when the target technology moves.

A published architecture is usually one point in a space of possible operators. Adding the missing parameters guarantees a result at least as good as the publication, since the parameter space contains it (pp. 78–80).

## Error Analysis

> **First principle.** An error is a **difference between a less accurate value and a more accurate value**. There is no such thing as "the error of a term". (p. 80)

The overall error $\delta(X)=\widehat f(X)-f(X)$ obeys it, and so does every equation of the $\pi$ example. The values appearing in the analysis may be **virtual** — objects that exist only in the proof. For $f(X)=\log(1+X)$ approximated by a Taylor series truncated at degree $d$, define

$$p(X)=X-\frac{X^2}{2}+\frac{X^3}{3}-\dots+(-1)^{d+1}\frac{X^d}{d}$$

$p(X)$ appears nowhere in the hardware — its binary expansion is infinite, thanks to the $1/3$ — but it lets the approximation error be defined at all.

**Composition.** If $A$ approximates $B$ and $B$ approximates $C$, then inserting and cancelling $B$ (telescoping),

$$A-C=(A-B)+(B-C)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \delta_{AC}=\delta_{AB}+\delta_{BC} \tag{3.8, 3.10}$$

For relative errors, divide by $C$ and then multiply-and-divide the first term by $B$, using $B/C=1+\varepsilon_{BC}$:

$$
\frac{A-C}{C}=\frac{A-B}{B}\cdot\frac{B}{C}+\frac{B-C}{C}
=\varepsilon_{AB}(1+\varepsilon_{BC})+\varepsilon_{BC}
$$

$$\Longrightarrow\qquad \varepsilon_{AC}=\varepsilon_{AB}+\varepsilon_{BC}+\varepsilon_{AB}\cdot\varepsilon_{BC} \tag{3.9, 3.11}$$

The cross term is what makes relative errors compose *almost*, but not quite, additively.

**Building the chain.** Split until each piece is boundable. First isolate the mathematical approximation:

$$\delta(X)=\big[\widehat f(X)-p(X)\big]+\big[\underbrace{p(X)-\log(1+X)}_{\text{Taylor remainder}}\big]$$

The second bracket is bounded by Taylor's theorem; forget it. The first is still everything the hardware does, so split again by promoting one concrete signal. Let $T_2(X)$ be the bit vector the architecture actually computes for $X^2/2$, with LSB $\ell_2$, and define the virtual

$$p_2(X)=X-T_2(X)+\frac{X^3}{3}-\dots+(-1)^{d+1}\frac{X^d}{d}$$

If $T_2$ is a faithful operator, $\left|T_2(X)-\tfrac{X^2}{2}\right|<2^{\ell_2}$, and since $p_2$ and $p$ differ in that one term only,

$$p_2(X)-p(X)=T_2(X)-\frac{X^2}{2}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad |p_2(X)-p(X)|<2^{\ell_2}$$

giving the longer chain

$$\delta(X)=\big[\widehat f(X)-p_2(X)\big]+\underbrace{\big[p_2(X)-p(X)\big]}_{<\,2^{\ell_2}}+\underbrace{\big[p(X)-\log(1+X)\big]}_{\text{Taylor}}$$

Repeat until only boundable terms remain, then apply the triangle inequality once at the very end:

$$\delta(X)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\delta_i(X)\;\Longrightarrow\;|\delta(X)|\le\sum_i|\delta_i(X)|\;\Longrightarrow\;\delta\le\delta_1+\dots+\delta_k$$

Note $\ell_2$ was never given a value — it stays a parameter, which is the point of [Parametric Designs](#parametric-designs).

**The five rules** (p. 82): define an error as a difference between a less accurate and a more accurate value; introduce as many virtual values as needed; introduce all the concrete values that appear in the architecture; introduce architecture parameters in the process; and **bound the errors as late as possible**, so the algebra can still simplify.

Products are the one case where naive term-by-term addition fails: bounding the error of $A\cdot B$ requires the *ranges* of $A$ and $B$, not only their error terms. The [Gappa](https://gappa.gitlabpages.inria.fr) proof assistant automates the bounding safely for fixed and floating point; its weakness is that a script describes one *instance*, so parametric designs need the script generated per parameter set.

## Error Budget and Design-Space Exploration

Return to the architecture at the top. Insert and cancel $R_{\mathrm{ext}}$:

$$R-f(X)=\underbrace{\big[R-R_{\mathrm{ext}}\big]}_{\delta_{\mathrm{final\;round}}}+\underbrace{\big[R_{\mathrm{ext}}-f(X)\big]}_{\delta_{\mathrm{datapath}}} \tag{3.12}$$

$R=\lfloor R_{\mathrm{ext}}\rceil_{\ell_R}$ gives $|\delta_{\mathrm{final}}|\le u/2$, **attained**, and it is *incompressible* — no choice of internal precision touches it. So faithfulness, $|\delta|<u$, follows from

$$\boxed{\;|\delta_{\mathrm{datapath}}| < u/2\;}$$

Now expand the datapath term by the chain method: $n$ internal roundings at LSB $\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}=\ell_R-g$ plus an approximation error $A(\theta)$ controlled by an approximation parameter $\theta$,

$$|\delta_{\mathrm{datapath}}| \;\le\; n\,2^{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}} + A(\theta) \;=\; n\,u\,2^{-g} + A(\theta) \;\overset{!}{<}\; \frac{u}{2}$$

Both terms are knobs, and both go to zero:

- **rounding errors** shrink like $2^{-g}$, where $g=\ell_R-\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}$ is the number of **guard bits** — extra LSBs carried internally;
- **approximation error** $A(\theta)$ shrinks with the approximation parameter: bits of $\pi$, Taylor degree $d$, iteration count for a digit-recurrence.

Finding *some* $(g,\theta)$ satisfying the inequality is easy. Finding the **optimal** one is an art, because the cost (area, delay, power) depends on the parameters in a non-trivial way. Sometimes it is expressible as an integer linear program ([[7.2) ILP for Arithmetic Design]]); sometimes exhaustive enumeration works because the parameters are few.

> [!note] Why correct rounding does not yield to guard bits
> Correct rounding demands $R=\lfloor f(X)\rceil$, not merely $|\delta|\le u/2$. Let $m$ be the midpoint of the output format nearest $f(X)$. If $f(X)$ and $R_{\mathrm{ext}}$ straddle $m$, the final rounding goes the wrong way and $|\delta|$ jumps to $u/2+|f(X)-m|$. Avoiding that requires $|\delta_{\mathrm{datapath}}| < \min_X|f(X)-m(X)|$ — a *hardest-to-round distance*. Any $g$ is defeated by an input whose $f(X)$ is closer to $m$ than $2^{-g}$, so no amount of guard bits helps unless that minimum can be lower-bounded by number theory. That bound is exactly what the Table Maker's Dilemma denies for most functions. Faithful rounding is cheap because $u/2>0$ is a budget you can *buy* with $g$; correct rounding is expensive because its budget is $0$.
>
> HUB weakens even the incompressible term: using HUB internally reduces the final rounding error to $u/2-2^{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}-1}$ (p. 83, footnote 5).

Because faithfulness composes, this method is **divide-and-conquer**: the $\log$ example simply *assumed* a parametric faithful operator for $X^2/2$, and building that sub-component uses the same method one level down. The book's deepest instance is a parametric faithful floating-point exponential ([[5.3) Floating-Point Exponential]]) assembled from faithful constant multipliers, a faithful polynomial approximator, faithful truncated multipliers, and tables (p. 84).

## On FPGAs

> [!warning] Not covered in the source — needs outside research
> Chapter 3 is deliberately technology-neutral and contains **no** LUT, slice, DSP or delay figures. The FPGA-specific costs below are the technology-independent statements of the chapter, pointed at the notes that do carry numbers; do not quote area figures from here.

Two chapter-3 facts have outsized weight on FPGA:

- **The rounding half-ulp is often free.** On an FPGA the LSB half of a datapath is usually a compressor tree feeding one final adder ([[2.3.1) Bit Heaps]], [[2.3.2) Compressor Trees]]). A constant $2^{\ell-1}$ is one more column entry in that heap, or the carry-in of an adder that already exists — so round-to-nearest costs nothing over truncation. Take this route whenever the architecture provides it (p. 70).
- **Guard bits are the cheap knob, until they are not.** Adding $g$ guard bits widens every internal signal by $g$, which on FPGA is $g$ more LUT/carry rows per operator and $g$ more bits of routing and register. The width thresholds that matter — hard-macro adder chains, DSP block port widths, block-RAM aspect ratios — live in [[1.3) FPGAs]], [[1.3.2) DSP Blocks]] and [[1.3.3) Block Memory]], and they make the cost of $g$ a **step function**, not a linear one. That is why the choice of $g$ belongs in design-space exploration rather than in a formula.

## Trade-offs

Rounding modes and accuracy specifications side by side. The first five rows are *rounding modes* (what one truncation site does); the last two are *operator specifications* (what the whole block promises).

| Variant | Hardware cost | Worst-case error | Attained? | Bias | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Truncation (round $\to-\infty$) | free — drop bits | $<1\;\mathrm{ulp}$ | no | one-signed, mean $-u/2$; accumulates linearly | p. 69–70 |
| Round to nearest, ties up, (3.1) | one addition (free if the half-ulp is absorbed upstream) | $\le\tfrac12\;\mathrm{ulp}$ | yes, at ties | small, from ties only; breaks symmetry about $0$ ($-1.5\to-1$) | p. 69–70 |
| Round to nearest, ties to even | addition **+ tie detection and special handling** | $\le\tfrac12\;\mathrm{ulp}$ | yes, at ties | unbiased; preserves symmetry about $0$ | p. 69–70 |
| Directed (to zero / away / $\to+\infty$) | addition **+ all-discarded-bits-zero detection** — dearer than ties-up | $<1\;\mathrm{ulp}$ | no | strongly one-signed | p. 71 |
| Truncation into HUB | free — drop bits | $\le\tfrac12\;\mathrm{ulp}$; HUB$\to$HUB gives exactly $2^{\ell_2-1}-2^{\ell_1-1}$ | yes from a general real; no HUB$\to$HUB | none — representable set is symmetric | pp. 72–73 |
| **Faithful** operator ($\delta<u$) | $u/2$ of budget to spend on $g$ and $\theta$ | $<1\;\mathrm{ulp}$ | no | implementation-dependent; usually inherits the internal truncations' bias | p. 76 |
| **Correctly rounded** operator | needs a hardest-to-round bound; may be undecidable (TMD) | $\le\tfrac12\;\mathrm{ulp}$ | yes | as round-to-nearest | pp. 75–77 |

No area numbers appear because the source gives none in this chapter.

**Pick truncation** when the error is one term among many in a budget that already closes, or when the extra bit costs less than the adder. **Pick round-to-nearest** whenever the half-ulp can be injected for free upstream — then it is strictly better than truncation. **Pick HUB internally** when you want half-ulp accuracy at truncation cost and can tolerate no exact zero. **Specify faithful** by default for a whole operator; **specify correctly rounded** only for $+,-,\times,\div,\sqrt{\;}$, where $\delta_{\mathrm{approx}}=0$, or when a standard forces your hand.

## Worked Example

Build a faithful multiplier by $\pi$: $X\in\mathrm{ufix}(-1,-8)$ (8 fractional bits, $0\le X<1$), output $R\in\mathrm{ufix}(1,-8)$, so $u=2^{-8}$. Architecture: a truncated multiplier producing $R_{\mathrm{ext}}$ at $\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}=-8-g$, then one round-to-nearest to $\ell_R=-8$.

**Budget.** Two datapath terms, from the derivation above:

$$
|\delta_{\mathrm{approx}}| = |\widetilde\pi-\pi|\cdot|X| \le 2^{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}-1},\qquad
|\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}}| < 2^{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}}
$$

$$
|\delta_{\mathrm{datapath}}| < \tfrac{3}{2}\cdot 2^{\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}} = \tfrac{3}{2}\cdot 2^{-8-g}
\;\overset{!}{<}\; \frac{u}{2}=2^{-9}
\;\Longleftrightarrow\; \tfrac{3}{2}\cdot2^{1-g}<1
\;\Longleftrightarrow\; g>\log_2 3 \approx 1.585
$$

so $g=2$ guard bits, $\ell_{\mathrm{ext}}=-10$. Total bound $\delta<\tfrac{3}{2}2^{-10}+2^{-9}=0.875\,u<u$. **Faithful.**

**Walk, $g=2$, $X=83/128$.**

$$
\begin{aligned}
X &= \texttt{0.10100110}_2 = 0.6484375\\
\widetilde\pi=\lfloor\pi\rceil_{-10} &= \texttt{11.0010010001}_2 = 3.1416015625 &&\delta_\pi=+8.909\cdot10^{-6}\\
\widetilde\pi\cdot X &= \texttt{10.000010011000000110}_2 &&\text{(exact, 18 fractional bits)}\\
R_{\mathrm{ext}}=\lfloor\widetilde\pi X\rfloor_{-10} &= \texttt{10.0000100110}_2 = 2.037109375\\
R=\lfloor R_{\mathrm{ext}}\rceil_{-8} &= \texttt{10.00001010}_2 = 2.0390625 &&\text{(discarded }\texttt{10}\text{: a tie, rounded up)}\\
\pi X &= \texttt{10.000010011000000100}_2 = 2.0371264\ldots
\end{aligned}
$$

Error accounting, in ulps of $2^{-8}$:

| term | value | bound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\delta_{\mathrm{approx}}$ | $+0.0015$ ulp | $\le 2^{-11}=0.125$ ulp |
| $\delta_{\mathrm{trunc}}$ | $-0.0059$ ulp | $<2^{-10}=0.25$ ulp |
| $\delta_{\mathrm{final}}$ | $+0.5000$ ulp | $\le 0.5$ ulp |
| **total** | $\mathbf{+0.4956}$ **ulp** | $<0.875$ ulp |

This input is the worst case over all 256 inputs, and the two datapath terms together contribute only $-0.0044$ ulp: the error is *entirely* the incompressible final rounding. The datapath's whole share of the budget went unused, which is what "computing just right" looks like when the guard-bit count is right.

**Why $g$ had to be 2.** Exhaustive evaluation over all 256 inputs:

| $g$ | proven bound | measured worst $\lvert\delta\rvert$ | faithful? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0$ | $1.5\,u$ | $1.177\,u$ | **no** — 28 of 256 inputs violate it |
| $1$ | $1.25\,u$ | $0.708\,u$ | not provable, though it happens to hold here |
| $2$ | $0.875\,u$ | $0.496\,u$ | **yes** |

$g=0$ is not a near miss — it genuinely returns non-adjacent values on 11% of inputs. $g=1$ passes by luck, which is exactly the situation an error budget exists to forbid: you ship the bound, not the experiment. (For $g=2$ the operator is in fact correctly rounded on all 256 inputs, but *proving* that required enumerating them — the [tabulate-everything argument](#rounding-to-the-nearest-when-an-approximation-is-involved), not an error bound.)
