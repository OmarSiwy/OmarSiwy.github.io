**Inputs.** One floating-point $X=(-1)^{s_X}2^{E_X}(1+F_X)$ in $\mathrm{float}\langle w_E,w_F\rangle$ ([[1.1) Number Formats]]); the other operand is a compile-time constant $C\in\mathbb{R}$, or absent entirely.
**Outputs.** $R$ in the *same* $\mathrm{float}\langle w_E,w_F\rangle$ — most of the tricks below break if $w_E$ or $w_F$ differ between input and output (p. 453).
**Accuracy.** Selectable, and this is the design knob: $1$ ulp (faithful) for the cheap variants, $0.5$ ulp (correctly rounded, bit-for-bit IEEE-754) when the operator must be substitutable for a software run.
**Optimized for.** Area first, then latency. Both come from *deleting* hardware: exponent-difference logic, normalizers, rounding adders, whole datapath paths.

Two independent levers act here and it is worth keeping them apart.

**Specialization** — one operand is known at synthesis time, so the parts of the operator that existed only to handle its variability evaporate. The exponent adder loses its bias correction, the significand multiplier becomes a shift-and-add graph ([[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]]), the normalizer's shift range collapses to $|E_C|$ instead of $w_F$. Specialization does not change the mathematical result — it just stops paying for cases that cannot occur.

**Fusion** — two operators are merged so that the intermediate between them is *never rounded*, and one normalize-and-round step serves both. Fusion costs area (a wider internal datapath) and buys accuracy. These are opposite trades, and the chapter is really two chapters.

## Floating-point constant multiplication

Start from the generic multiplier of [[2.7) Floating-Point Operators]]. Substituting a constant $C=(-1)^{s_C}2^{E_C}(1+F_C)$ for the second operand rewrites the three datapaths as $s_R=s_X\oplus s_C$, one $w_E$-bit add $(E_X+E_0)+E_C$, and $(1+F_X)\times(1+F_C)$.

The exponent line is the sharpest saving and it comes from an algebraic identity, not a circuit trick. Generic multiplication computes $E_X+E_Y$ on *unbiased* exponents and must subtract the bias once, $(E_X+E_0)+(E_Y+E_0)-E_0$. With a constant, $E_C$ is available unbiased at compile time, so $(E_R+E_0)=(E_X+E_0)+E_C$ lands directly in biased form (p. 454). The bias subtractor is gone, not shrunk. The sign XOR degenerates to a wire or an inverter. The significand multiplier becomes a constant multiplier — shift-and-add graph or KCM, [[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]] — which is *sublinear* in the constant's size ([DIZ07], cited p. 460), a fact that will matter in the correctly-rounded case.

Exception handling also simplifies, purely from range: if $C<1$ strictly the product can never overflow; if $C>1$ it can never flush to zero (p. 454). One of the two exceptional branches always dies.

### Multiplication by a power of two

$F_C=0$ collapses the operator to exponent-and-exception management (p. 454). Nothing touches the significand. The tentative result exponent $E_R+E_0$ is carried on $w_E+1$ bits, read as an `sfix`: the extra bit is a sign bit detecting underflow when $E_C<0$ and an overflow bit when $E_C>0$, and **both cases are detected by the same test** — $E_R+E_0$ going negative. That single spare bit is what lets `exception handling & pack` implement Table 11.2, p. 343 with no extra comparator. ($E_C=0$ means $C=1$ and the compiler has already deleted the multiplication.)

Worth building even though it looks trivial: `x * 2.0` and `x * 0.5` are everywhere in real code, and an HLS compiler that does not recognise them instantiates a full multiplier for a rewiring.

### The baseline faithful multiplier by a constant

Restrict to $1<1.F_C<2$ and normal inputs. Since $1\le 1.F_X\le 2-2^{-w_F}$, the exact product lies in $[1,\;2(2-2^{-w_F}))$ — strictly less than $4$ — so **at most one bit of normalization** is ever needed (p. 455). That single fact is the whole architecture.

The optimization over the generic multiplier is that the normalization decision can be taken *from the input fraction alone*, in parallel with the multiplication. Define the reciprocal-derived fraction

$$1+F_{C^{-1}}=\frac{2}{1+F_C}\ \in(1,2)$$

and compare $F_X$ against $\lfloor F_{C^{-1}}\rfloor_{-w_F}$, the value $F_{C^{-1}}$ truncated to the input's format (p. 456):

- **$F_X>\lfloor F_{C^{-1}}\rfloor_{-w_F}$.** Then $F_X\ge\lfloor F_{C^{-1}}\rfloor_{-w_F}+2^{-w_F}>F_{C^{-1}}$, so by monotonicity of $t\mapsto(1+F_C)(1+t)$, the product exceeds $2$: shift right one bit.
- **$F_X<\lfloor F_{C^{-1}}\rfloor_{-w_F}$**, i.e. $F_X\le\lfloor F_{C^{-1}}\rfloor_{-w_F}-2^{-w_F}$. Then

$$(1+F_C)(1+F_X)\ \le\ (1+F_C)\!\left(1+F_{C^{-1}}-2^{-w_F}\right)\ =\ 2-2^{-w_F}(1+F_C)\ \le\ 2-2^{-w_F}$$

  using $1+F_{C^{-1}}=2/(1+F_C)$ in the middle equality and $1+F_C>1$ at the end. No normalization.

- **$F_X=\lfloor F_{C^{-1}}\rfloor_{-w_F}$**, the limit case, may round up to $2$ or down to $2-2^{-w_F}$ depending on both the constant and the construction of the faithful multiplier. But a *built* multiplier always takes the same decision for this one fraction value, so the comparison is resolved at compile time: build `product normal?` as $F_X\ge\lfloor F_{C^{-1}}\rfloor_{-w_F}$ if the multiplier returns 2 on that input, and as $F_X>\lfloor F_{C^{-1}}\rfloor_{-w_F}$ otherwise (p. 457).

Two consequences. The exponent update runs concurrently with the significand product instead of after it. And the *rounding bit position* is known early — $-w_F$ when the test is false, $-w_F+1$ when true — so the rounding addition can be absorbed into the constant multiplier's bit heap ([[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits#Bit Heaps]]) rather than sitting as a separate post-normalization adder. The book's own accounting: a post-normalization adder is at least as large as the constant comparator that replaces it (Sect. 6.3.3, [[2.2) Comparators]]), so this is **delay improvement at no area cost** (p. 457).

### Correctly rounded multiplication by a floating-point constant

When $C$ is itself a float, its fraction $F_C\in\mathrm{ufix}(-1,-k)$ is finite with $k\le w_F$. The architecture is unchanged except that the faithful constant multiplier becomes an *exact* one, producing $2+w_F+k$ bits before rounding (p. 458). This is the variant an HLS compiler needs: replacing a generic multiplier by a constant one is only a legal optimization if the result is bit-for-bit what a software run produces.

Round-to-nearest-ties-to-away is then immediate. Ties-to-even needs a sticky bit, so the real question is *when can a tie occur at all*. Reason on integer significands: $I_X=2^{w_F}(1+F_X)\in[2^{w_F},2^{w_F+1}-1]$, and with $k$ minimal, $I_C=2^k(1+F_C)$ is an **odd** integer in $[2^k,2^{k+1}-1]$. Their product fits on $w_F+k+2$ bits, and a tie means $k-1$ trailing zeroes, i.e. $2^{k-1}\mid I_CI_X$. Since $I_C$ is odd, that forces $2^{k-1}\mid I_X$, hence $I_X\ge 2^{k-1}$. Therefore:

| condition | tie situation | hardware consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $k-1>w_F$ | impossible ($I_X<2^{w_F+1}$) | no sticky path at all |
| $k-1=w_F$ | only $I_X=2^{w_F}$, i.e. significand exactly $1$ | anticipate with a pseudo-sticky on $F_X$, off the multiplier's critical path |
| $k=w_F$, $I_C>\tfrac23 2^{w_F}$ | impossible: $I_{C^{-1}}<3\cdot 2^{w_F-1}$, and no odd multiple of $2^{w_F-1}$ in $I_X$'s range $[2^{w_F},2^{w_F+1})$ is smaller | none |
| $k=w_F$, $I_C<\tfrac23 2^{w_F}$ | a single case | pseudo-sticky on $F_X$ |
| $k<w_F$ | $I_X<I_{C^{-1}}$ an odd multiple of $2^{k-1}$, or $I_X\ge I_{C^{-1}}$ an odd multiple of $2^k$ | anticipation still possible but filters fewer cases; else full sticky on the low product bits (Sect. 11.2.2) |

*(pp. 458–459, with $I_{C^{-1}}=2^k(1+F_{C^{-1}})$.)* All of this is compile-time arithmetic on the constant. The point of the table is that the sticky-computation hardware — the expensive part of correct rounding — is often provably unnecessary, and the proof is two lines of parity.

### Correctly rounded multiplication by a real constant

Now $C$ is irrational ($\pi$, $\log 2$) and has no finite representation. The construction [BDM08] finds the smallest $k$ such that rounding $1.F_X\times\lfloor 1.F_C\rfloor_{-k}$ gives the correctly rounded $1.F_X\times 1.F_C$, then reuses the previous architecture with $\widetilde C_k=\lfloor 1+F_C\rfloor_{-k}$.

Such a $k$ exists by a counting argument (p. 459). Midpoints are the points equidistant from two consecutive floats. For each $X$ let $\delta(X)$ be the distance from the exact real product $CX$ to the nearest midpoint. There are *finitely many* $X$, so the set of nonzero $\delta(X)$ has a minimum $\bar\delta>0$: no product comes closer to a midpoint than $\bar\delta$ unless it is exactly one. If we compute any $\widetilde P$ with $|\widetilde P-P|<\bar\delta$, then $\widetilde P$ and $P$ lie on the same side of every midpoint, so rounding $\widetilde P$ to $\mathrm{float}\langle w_E,w_F\rangle$ rounds $P$ correctly.

Truncating the constant gives exactly that handle:

$$|\widetilde P_k-P|=\big|\lfloor C\rfloor_{-k}X-CX\big|=\big|\lfloor C\rfloor_{-k}-C\big|\times(1+F_X)<2^{-k-1}\times 2=2^{-k}$$

— the constant's own half-ulp $2^{-k-1}$ amplified by the significand bound $1+F_X<2$. So $k=\lceil-\log_2\bar\delta\rceil$ suffices (p. 460).

> [!note] Where $\bar\delta$ comes from, and why $k\approx 2w_F$
> If the $\delta(X)$ were uniform in $[0,2^{-w_F-1}]$, the minimum of $2^{w_F}$ samples would sit around $1/2^{w_F}$ of that interval, giving $\bar\delta\approx 2^{-2w_F-1}$ and hence $k\approx 2w_F+1$. The uniformity assumption is unfounded but holds in practice for irrational constants (p. 459). For $w_F\le 24$, $\bar\delta$ is obtained by exhaustive enumeration of all $F_X$ with each $CX$ evaluated to, say, 512 bits — the enumeration cost is the honest price, and the $2^{-512}$ evaluation margin is irrelevant. Beyond that, continued fractions compute $k$ directly [BM08].

The cost verdict is unusually crisp. Faithful rounding needs $k=1+w_F$ constant bits, correct rounding needs $k\approx 2w_F+1$ — twice the constant. But constant multiplication is *sublinear* in constant size [DIZ07], so the area penalty is **less than a factor 2**, and the delay penalty is much smaller still since delay grows logarithmically with precision (p. 460). "Less than $2\times$ the area for one more bit of accuracy" is still called a large price by the authors.

### Correct rounding of multiplication by a rational constant

Rational $A/B$ with $A,B$ small has efficient fixed-point architectures (Sect. 12.4, [[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]]). The floating-point version is cheap because of an exclusion-zone theorem — a generalization of the "exclusion lemma" behind correctly rounded division.

> **Theorem 15.1 (p. 461).** If $C$ is $A/B$ truncated to at least $w_F+1+\lceil\log_2 B\rceil$ bits, then rounding $CX$ to $w_F$ fractional bits is equivalent to rounding the exact $\tfrac{A}{B}X$.

*Proof.* Scale by powers of two so $X\in[1/2,1)$ and $A,B$ are odd. Write $X=I/2^{w_F}$ with $I$ integral, and a midpoint as $M=(2J+1)/2^{w_F+1}$. Then

$$\left|\frac{A}{B}X-M\right|=\left|\frac{A}{B}\frac{I}{2^{w_F}}-\frac{2J+1}{2^{w_F+1}}\right|=\left|\frac{2AI-(2J+1)B}{2^{w_F+1}B}\right|$$

after putting both terms over the common denominator $2^{w_F+1}B$. Now the parity argument: $2AI$ is even, while $(2J+1)B$ is a product of two odd integers and is therefore odd. Their difference is a nonzero integer, hence at least $1$ in absolute value, so

$$\left|\frac{A}{B}X-M\right|\ \ge\ \frac{1}{2^{w_F+1}B}$$

An exclusion zone of that radius surrounds every midpoint. Any $R$ with $|R-\tfrac{A}{B}X|<2^{-w_F-1}/B$ rounds like $\tfrac{A}{B}X$, and truncating $A/B$ to precision $2^{-w_F-1-\lceil\log_2 B\rceil}$ delivers that accuracy. $\square$

A floating-point fraction is the special case $B=2^k$, where the theorem says only "compute the significand product exactly" — true but useless (p. 462). The theorem earns its keep for genuine rationals like $1/3$ or $3/10$.

### Subnormal handling

This is where the operator gets expensive, and the two cases are structurally different.

**$C>1$ (so $E_C\ge 0$).** No normal input can produce a subnormal output, but subnormal *inputs* exist and some become normal. Let $n_X$ be the "is normal" bit and $\lambda$ the leading-zero count of $n_X+F_X$ (so $\lambda=0$ for normals).

- $\lambda>E_C$: multiplying $0+F_X$ by $2^{E_C}$ is a left shift by $E_C$ that still does not normalize, so feed $0+2^{E_C}F_X$ straight to the significand multiplier. By construction the product is below $2$ — no post-normalization. The one wrinkle: at $\lambda=E_C+1$ the product *does* become normal and the exponent field must be incremented.
- $0\le\lambda\le E_C$: the output is normal. A normalizer rebuilds $0+F_X=2^{-\lambda}(1+F'_X)$ as a genuine normal float that the previous architecture accepts, and the exponent adder adds $E_C-\lambda$ instead of $E_C$.

Both are one block: a **saturated normalizer** (LZC + shifter, [[2.6) Shifters#Normalizers]]) shifting left by at most $E_C$ bits and emitting $\lambda'$, saturated at $E_C$. It is a no-op for normal inputs. *(Book prints $\lambda'=\max(\lambda,E_C)$ on p. 462; the accompanying prose — "saturates to $\lambda'=E_C$" for subnormal-in-subnormal-out — reads as the saturating minimum.)*

> [!note] The saving is in the shift range, not the block count
> A generic FP multiplier's subnormal normalizer must shift by up to $w_F$. Here it shifts by at most $E_C$, and for most practical constants $E_C\ll w_F$ (p. 463). Same block, a $\log$ factor shallower — that is the whole subnormal optimization, and it is entirely a consequence of knowing $C$.

**$C<1$ (so $E_C<0$).** The mirror image: subnormal inputs are never renormalized, but normal inputs can produce subnormals. The fix is post-processing — if the tentative exponent $E_R<e_{\min}$, right-shift by $\lambda=e_{\min}-E_R$. Crucially, this shift **cannot be moved before the multiplier**, because we are not allowed to discard any shifted-out bit. So the architecture must revert to rounding *after* normalization, giving up the merged-rounding trick of the baseline. The same shifter then serves input subnormals, always shifting right by $-E_C$.

> [!warning] Not covered in the source — needs outside research
> The book explicitly declines to give the $C<1$ subnormal architecture: "We do not detail the architecture here" (p. 464). Only the two savings are stated — the constant significand multiplier and the shift distance limited to $|E_C|$ when $|E_C|<w_F$.

## Floating-point division by a small integer constant

Target: bit-for-bit compatibility with an IEEE-754 divider, built on the Euclidean-divide-by-a-constant machinery of [[3.2) Division by Constants]], so that an FPGA compiler can drop it in for a generic divider at a fraction of the cost (p. 464). Written for odd $D$; $D'=2^kD$ follows immediately, so this covers dividing by 12 or by 0.75.

### The baseline operator for normalized inputs

Write $D=2^{E_D}(1+F_D)$. Since $D$ is odd it is an integer of $E_D+1$ bits and $F_D$ fits on $E_D$ bits — e.g. $5=2^2(1.01_2)$, $F_D=.01_2$, $E_D=2$.

**Normalization.** By the quotient rule for exponents,

$$\frac{X}{D}=\frac{2^{E_X}(1+F_X)}{2^{E_D}(1+F_D)}=2^{E_X-E_D}\cdot\frac{1+F_X}{1+F_D},\qquad \frac{1+F_X}{1+F_D}\in[0.5,2)$$

so the quotient significand is *almost* normalized already, and a single comparison decides:

$$
Y=\begin{cases}
\left\lfloor \dfrac{2^{E_D}(1+F_X)}{D}\right\rceil_{-w_F}2^{E_X-E_D} & F_X\ge F_D\quad(\text{ratio}\in[1,2))\\[10pt]
\left\lfloor \dfrac{2^{E_D+1}(1+F_X)}{D}\right\rceil_{-w_F}2^{E_X-E_D-1} & F_X<F_D\quad(\text{ratio}\in[0.5,1))
\end{cases}
$$

The comparison is nearly free: $F_D$ occupies $E_D$ nonzero bits, so it reduces to comparing $E_D$ bits against the leading $E_D$ bits of $F_X$ (p. 465). Everything else in Fig. 15.7 is one small exponent adder and one significand-wide multiplexer wrapped around the integer divider.

**Rounding, and the trick that deletes the rounding adder.** Ignoring the remainder gives round-toward-zero. The obvious repair — compare the final remainder to $D/2$ and increment — is followed by a significand-wide addition, which is exactly what we want to avoid. Instead use the round-to-nearest identity $\lfloor z\rceil=\lfloor z+\tfrac12\rfloor$:

$$
\left\lfloor \frac{2^{E_D+\varepsilon}(1+F_X)}{D}\right\rceil
=\left\lfloor \frac{2^{E_D+\varepsilon}(1+F_X)+D/2}{D}\right\rfloor,\qquad \varepsilon=[F_X<F_D]
$$

$D$ is odd, so write $D=2H+1$ with $H=(D-1)/2$ an integer, and split $D/2=H+\tfrac12$:

$$
=\left\lfloor \frac{2^{E_D+\varepsilon}(1+F_X)+H}{D}+\frac{1}{2D}\right\rfloor
=\left\lfloor \frac{2^{E_D+\varepsilon}(1+F_X)+H}{D}\right\rfloor
$$

The last step is the one that pays: the added $\tfrac{1}{2D}<1$ cannot change the floor, because the quantity to its left is a rational with denominator $D$ and the gap to the next integer is at least $\tfrac1D>\tfrac{1}{2D}$. So the round-to-nearest correction moves from the *result* to the *dividend*.

> [!important] Adding $H$ is free — it is wiring, not arithmetic
> $H$ is an $E_D$-bit number, and the dividend is $1+F_X$ shifted **left** by $E_D+\varepsilon\ge E_D$ bits, whose bottom $E_D+\varepsilon$ bits are therefore zero. Adding $H$ into them is a mere concatenation at the LSB: no carry can ever propagate. Both the area *and* the latency of the rounding adder vanish (p. 466). Since $D$ is odd, no tie can occur when input and output formats are identical, so no sticky logic either.

### Subnormal handling

Division by a constant is, from the subnormal point of view, exactly multiplication by the inverse — the entire $C>1$ / $C<1$ analysis above applies verbatim (p. 467), with the case split determined by whether $D<1$ or $D>1$.

## Floating-point squares, cubes and $X^p$

Exponentiation distributes over the sign/exponent/significand decomposition:

$$X^2=2^{2E_X}(1+F_X)^2,\qquad X^3=(-1)^{s_X}2^{3E_X}(1+F_X)^3,\qquad X^p=(-1)^{ps_X}2^{pE_X}(1+F_X)^p$$

so the sign is positive for even $p$ and $s_X$ for odd $p$, and the exponent path is a *multiplication by the small constant $p$* — a couple of shifts and adds ([[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]]). Input subnormals are trivial: $X^p$ rounds to zero for any $p\ge 2$. Supporting subnormal *outputs* is not trivial — it needs specific hardware to saturate the exponent at $e_{\min}$ and denormalize the significand (p. 468).

The significand is the real work. $(1+F_X)^p\in[1,2^p)$, so up to a **$p$-bit normalization** is needed — a full normalizer (Sect. 10.3, [[2.6) Shifters]]) whose leading-zero count updates the exponent. If latency matters, predict it instead: the boundary between an $i$-bit and an $(i{+}1)$-bit shift is at $(1+F_X)^p=2^i$, i.e.

$$F_X = \sqrt[p]{2^i}-1,\qquad i\in\{1,\dots,p-1\}$$

Concretely: $X^2$ needs a 1-bit normalization iff $F_X>\sqrt2-1$; $X^3$ needs 1 bit iff $\sqrt[3]2-1<F_X<\sqrt[3]4-1$ and 2 bits iff $F_X>\sqrt[3]4-1$. All $p-1$ comparisons run in parallel and are priority-encoded concurrently with the significand path, so **exponent processing leaves the critical path entirely** (p. 468). This is the same idea as `product normal?`, generalized from $p=2$ operands to $p$ powers.

For the significand itself, the exact $(1+F_X)^p$ is $p(1+w_F)$ bits, expensive but much cheaper than $p-1$ generic multipliers thanks to square-and-multiply (Sect. 14.3.2): $M^5=(M^2)^2\times M$, two squarers of increasing size and one multiplier instead of four multiplications. If only *faithful* rounding is required, $f(x)=(1+x)^p$ on $[0,1)$ is smooth and singularity-free (Fig. 15.8, p. 469), so generic polynomial approximation ([[4.3) Polynomial Approximation]]) is in principle cheaper — the exact power is only needed for correct rounding. See also [[3.3) Squarers and Integer Powers]] for the fixed-point substrate.

## Floating-point addition of positive terms

The cleanest specialization in the chapter, because it deletes a *path*, not a block.

Take $E_X\ge E_Y$, $d=E_X-E_Y\ge 0$, significands $m_X,m_Y\in[1,2)$. The aligned significand result before normalization is $S=m_X\pm 2^{-d}m_Y$, and the three regimes are:

| case | range of $S$ | normalization required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| effective addition, any $d$ | $S\in[1,4)$ | at most a **1-bit right** shift |
| effective subtraction, $d\ge 2$ | $S>1-2^{-2}\cdot 2=\tfrac12$ | at most a **1-bit left** shift (*far path*) |
| effective subtraction, $d\in\{0,1\}$ | $S$ can be as small as $2^{-w_F}$ | up to $w_F+1$ bits of **left** shift (*close path*) |

Only the third row needs a leading-zero counter and a full barrel shifter, and only the third row can produce catastrophic cancellation. If the application guarantees all terms have the same sign — probabilities being summed, or multiplied in the log domain (p. 468) — the third row is unreachable.

> [!important] This deletes the critical path, it does not shrink it
> Removing the close path removes the LZC ($\lceil\log_2(w_F{+}1)\rceil$ levels) *and* the $(w_F{+}1)$-bit left normalizer of [[2.6) Shifters]], *and* the path-select multiplexer, *and* the close-path exponent-decrement network. What remains is alignment shift → adder → 1-bit right shift → round, which is precisely the far path of Fig. 11.6, p. 341. The book measures the saving at **about 1/3 of the hardware of a generic floating-point adder** [Som+20], p. 468.
> And unlike the faithful constant multiplier, this costs **nothing in accuracy**: the deleted path is not an approximation, it is an unreachable case. Error stays at $0.5$ ulp. Specializations that are free are rare; recognize them.

## Combined floating-point sum and difference

When both $X+Y$ and $X-Y$ are needed — the FFT butterfly — two full adders are wasteful [TDX12], p. 469:

- Operand decoding and the initial alignment shift (Fig. 11.5, p. 337) are computed once and **shared**.
- At most one of the two results can be an effective subtraction, so at most one close path is needed. But *both* results may need a far path.

So the dual-path adder of Fig. 11.6, p. 341 becomes a **3-path architecture**: one close path plus two far paths sharing a single alignment shifter — three paths and one shifter, against four paths and two shifters for two independent adders.

## Fused multiply-add and other sums of products

The FMA computes $A\times B+C$ with a **single rounding of the exact result**. The book is careful to note it is the opposite of application-specific: it is a *universal* operator that subsumes addition ($A\times1+C$) and multiplication ($A\times B+0$) and enables flexible division and square root [Mar00; CHT02; Mul+18], p. 469.

> [!important] Fusion buys an error bound relative to the *result*, not to an intermediate
> Let $u=2^{-w_F-1}$ be the unit roundoff and $\circ(\cdot)$ round-to-nearest ([[1.2) Error Accuracy]]). Unfused, with $|\delta_1|,|\delta_2|\le u$:
> $$\hat r=\circ\big(\circ(ab)+c\big)=\big(ab(1+\delta_1)+c\big)(1+\delta_2)$$
> Expanding and dropping the $O(u^2)$ term,
> $$\hat r-(ab+c)=\underbrace{\delta_2\,(ab+c)}_{\le\,u\,|ab+c|}\;+\;\underbrace{\delta_1\,ab}_{\le\,u\,|ab|}$$
> The second term is a half-ulp **of the product**, not of the sum. Fused:
> $$\hat r_{\mathrm{FMA}}=\circ(ab+c)=(ab+c)(1+\delta)\ \Longrightarrow\ |\hat r_{\mathrm{FMA}}-(ab+c)|\le u\,|ab+c|$$
> Two readings follow. **Without cancellation** ($|ab|\lesssim|ab+c|$) the chain is bounded by $2u$ — one ulp — and the FMA by $u$ — half an ulp; with two *faithful* roundings instead of two correct ones the same argument gives 2 ulp against 1. Rounding once instead of twice halves the bound. **With cancellation** the $\delta_1\,ab$ term is bounded by nothing at all relative to $ab+c$: the chain's relative error is unbounded while the FMA's is still $u$. That second reading is the one that matters, and it is why $\circ(c-ab)$ from an FMA is *exact* whenever $c-ab$ is representable — the exact-residual property that makes Newton–Raphson refinement converge to correct rounding ([[2.5) Dividers#Division by Reciprocal]]).

The price is stated bluntly: an FMA is **more expensive than an adder and a multiplier combined**, because a single rounding mandates a wider internal datapath (p. 469). The algebra forces it — the exact product occupies $2w_F+2$ bits and $c$ may align anywhere above or below it, so the accumulator must span roughly $3w_F$ bits before the one rounding, where a separate multiplier and adder each only ever carry $\approx w_F$ significant bits past their own rounding.

Fusion beyond the FMA has been studied for the sum of two products [SS08; Tao+13], the sum of squares [DKP09; MS13; Tao+13], and sums of three [Tao+12] or four [Tao+13] floats. The design rule the book extracts (p. 470):

> Faithful rounding gives significantly cheaper fused operators **only for sums of same-sign terms**. Otherwise large cancellation is possible, and handling it properly requires computing most of the bits of the exact result anyway — so there is little left to save by relaxing from correct to faithful rounding.

Which closes the loop with the previous section: sum-of-squares is cheap to fuse for exactly the reason positive-term addition is cheap to specialize. The faithful floating-point sum of three squares is the book's opening example of fusion (Fig. 1.5, p. 12) and it saves **both area and latency** against a combination of adders and multipliers.

## Floating-point optimizations in an HLS context

An HLS compiler could in principle apply every specialization above, and mostly does not — partly a software inheritance problem [Ugu+20], p. 470. The optimizations split in two:

- **Unsafe** — those that do not preserve C/C++ semantics. GCC's `-funsafe-math-optimizations` permits reassociating $+$ and $\times$ as if they were associative, swapping signed zeros, and degrading accuracy (faithful instead of correct rounding).
- **Safe** — those that preserve IEEE-754 semantics exactly. Specialized adders, multipliers, dividers and comparators must implement IEEE semantics to qualify.

Several innocent-looking rewrites are *not* safe, per the C standard [StdC18], p. 471:

| rewrite | fails when | because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $x/x\to 1.0$ | $x$ is $0$, $\infty$, or NaN | $x/x$ is NaN there |
| $x-y\to-(y-x)$ | always, at zero | $1.0-1.0=+0$ but $-(1.0-1.0)=-0$ |
| $x-x\to 0$ | $x$ is NaN or $\infty$ | result is NaN |
| $0\times x\to 0$ | $x$ is NaN, $\infty$, or $-0$ | NaN, NaN, $-0$ |
| $x+0\to x$ | $x$ is $-0$ | $(-0)+(+0)=+0$ in round-to-nearest |
| $0-x\to-x$ | $x$ is $+0$ | $-(+0)=-0$ but $0-(+0)=+0$ |

Nobody writes `x/x`; it appears as a residue of code hoisting, procedure specialization and cloning [Muc97]. And the hardware answer differs from the software one: guarding `x/x` with `is_zero(x)||is_infty(x)||is_nan(x) ? NaN : 1.0` is not worth it on a superscalar, where the test may cost more than the division — but in hardware the test is *all-zero or all-one exponent bits*, 8 bits for single precision and 11 for double. Cheap.

That flips the economics: on FPGA, replace the divider by a mux driven by an 11-bit test (Fig. 15.9b). Better still, the two possible outputs propagate well — $1.0$ is absorbed by multiplication and NaN is contagious — so the multiplexer can be **pushed down the computation** (Fig. 15.9c), enabling further specializations downstream.

## FPGA mapping

Nothing here is FPGA-only, but two things change the arithmetic on an FPGA specifically (a third, the near-free `x/x` exponent test, is in the section above):

- **Constant significand multipliers escape the DSP blocks.** Shift-and-add graphs and KCM tables ([[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]], [[1.3) FPGAs]]) build the $\times(1+F_C)$ core out of LUTs and the fast carry chain, freeing DSPs for the parts of the design that need genuine two-operand products. That is what makes the "less than $2\times$ area for correct rounding" figure of p. 460 tolerable.
- **Constant dividers are drop-in.** `FPConstDiv` is meant to be substituted for a generic FP divider by a compiler targeting FPGA, "at a fraction of the cost" (p. 464) — see [[7.1) FloPoCo]]:

  ```sh
  flopoco FPConstDiv we=8 wf=23 d=3            # divide by 3 (1-D stencils)
  flopoco FPConstDiv we=8 wf=23 d=5 dExp=1     # divide by 10 = 5 * 2^1
  flopoco FPConstDiv we=8 wf=23 d=3:3          # divide by 9 as two /3 stages (Sect. 13.5)
  ```

  The `d=5 dExp=1` interface is the book's own admission of an awkward API: the user must factor the constant into an odd integer and an exponent (p. 467).

## Trade-offs

Specialization and fusion are *different axes*: specialization removes hardware at constant or degraded accuracy, fusion adds hardware to improve accuracy. The table crosses both.

| Operation | Variant | Latency | Area | Worst-case error | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $X\times C$ | generic FP multiplier | exp add, $(w_F{+}1)^2$ mult, norm, round adder in series | full 2-operand multiplier + bias subtractor + sign XOR | $0.5$ ulp | 344 |

> [!warning] The source gives no synthesis numbers for this chapter
> Chapter 15 quantifies cost only as bit widths, structural block counts, and two ratios — "about 1/3 of the hardware of a generic FP adder" (p. 468) and "less than a factor 2 in area" (p. 460). There are no LUT/slice/DSP counts, no delays, no cycle counts anywhere in pp. 453–472. Every figure above is either a bit width, a block count, or one of those two ratios; nothing has been invented to fill the columns.

**Picking.** Take the *specialized* variant whenever an operand is a synthesis-time constant — it is never worse, and for powers of two it is free. Take *faithful* over *correctly rounded* unless the operator must reproduce a software run bit-for-bit (the HLS substitution case), because correct rounding roughly doubles the constant width for one bit of accuracy. Take the *positive-terms* adder whenever the application guarantees sign — it is the only free lunch here. Take an *FMA* only when cancellation is real; without cancellation it buys half an ulp for more area than a multiplier and an adder combined, and if the terms are same-sign you are better served by a cheap faithful fused sum.

## Worked example: correctly rounded division by 3

Format $w_E=4$, $w_F=4$. Input $X=1.0110_2\times2^0=1.375$, constant $D=3=2^1(1.1_2)$, so $E_D=1$, $F_D=.1_2=0.5$, $H=(D-1)/2=1=1_2$ on $E_D=1$ bit.

**Normalization decision.** $F_X=.0110_2=0.375<F_D=0.5$, so $\varepsilon=1$: the ratio $(1+F_X)/(1+F_D)=1.375/1.5=0.9167\in[0.5,1)$ and the significand must be pre-shifted one extra bit. Result exponent $=E_X-E_D-\varepsilon=0-1-1=-2$.

**Dividend assembly, with the rounding bit as wiring.** The integer significand is $I_X=2^{w_F}(1+F_X)=10110_2=22$; shift left by $E_D+\varepsilon=2$ to get $1011000_2=88$. Its bottom two bits are zero by construction and $H=1_2$ is one bit, so $H$ drops straight into them:

$$1011000_2 \;\Vert\; 1_2 \;=\; 1011001_2 = 89$$

No adder, no carry. Compare the naive route: divide $88$ by $3$, get remainder $88-87=1$, compare $1$ against $D/2=1.5$, then conditionally increment a 5-bit significand — a comparator and an incrementer for the same answer.

**Divide.** $89 = 3\times29 + 2$, so $Q = 29 = 11101_2$, which is $1+w_F=5$ bits, already normalized as $1.1101_2$.

**Check against exact rounding.** $88/3=29.333\ldots$; the neighbours are $29$ and $30$, distances $0.333$ and $0.667$, so round-to-nearest gives $29$ — the concatenated $H$ reproduced it exactly.

**Assemble and measure.**

$$
R = 1.1101_2\times2^{-2} = \tfrac{29}{16}\cdot\tfrac14 = 0.453125,\qquad
\tfrac{X}{D}=\tfrac{1.375}{3}=0.4583\overline{3}
$$

$$
\varepsilon_{\text{abs}} = 0.00520833,\qquad
\mathrm{ulp}(R)=2^{-w_F}\cdot2^{-2}=2^{-6}=0.015625
$$

$$\boxed{\ \varepsilon = 0.333\ \text{ulp} \;\le\; 0.5\ \text{ulp}\ }$$

Correctly rounded, with zero rounding hardware on the critical path. And $D$ odd guarantees no tie ever occurs, so no sticky logic is needed either.

### Counter-example: why the FMA is not just half an ulp better

Keep $w_F=4$ but widen the exponent to $w_E=5$, so that $e_{\min}=-15$ ([[1.1) Number Formats]]) and the tiny exact result below is a normal number — only $w_F$ matters for the rounding argument. Take $A=B=1.0001_2=17/16$ and $C=-1.0010_2=-288/256$.

The exact product is $A\times B=289/256=1.00100001_2$, which needs 8 fractional bits — twice the format. Round it: the tail $0001_2$ at weight $2^{-8}$ is below the half-ulp $2^{-5}$, so $\circ(AB)=1.0010_2=288/256$. The FMA instead rounds $AB+C=289/256-288/256=2^{-8}$ once, and $2^{-8}$ is exactly representable in $\mathrm{float}\langle5,4\rangle$:

$$
\hat r = \circ\big(\circ(AB)+C\big) = \tfrac{288}{256}-\tfrac{288}{256} = \mathbf{0},\qquad
\hat r_{\mathrm{FMA}} = 2^{-8}\ \ (\text{error }0\ \text{ulp})
$$

The unfused chain's error is $2^{-8}$ absolute. Measured in ulp *of the product* that is $2^{-8}/2^{-4}=0.0625$ ulp — well inside its bound. Measured in ulp *of the result*, $\mathrm{ulp}(2^{-8})=2^{-12}$, it is $\mathbf{16}$ **ulp**, and the relative error is $100\%$. That gap between "small relative to the intermediate" and "catastrophic relative to the result" is precisely the $\delta_1\,ab$ term of the callout above, and it is the entire case for fusion.
