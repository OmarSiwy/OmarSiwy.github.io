**Inputs.** Two floating-point numbers in a $(w_E,w_F)$ format — sign, biased exponent field $E\in[0,2^{w_E})$, fraction $F$ with an implicit leading bit (see [[1.1) Number Formats]] and [[Floating-Point Formats]]). Either the IEEE 754 encoding, or FloPoCo's `Nfloat`, which spends two extra bits on an explicit exception field instead of stealing exponent codes.
**Output.** One number in the same format, plus exception flags.
**Accuracy.** Round to nearest, $\le\tfrac12$ ulp — or **faithful**, $<1$ ulp, which is the interesting option here. Both are defined in [[1.2) Error Accuracy]] / [[Rounding]].
**Optimized for.** Latency first (these blocks sit on the critical path of every FP datapath), then area. The book's thesis for this chapter: for a *given application-level accuracy* it is usually cheaper to use faithful operators than correctly rounded ones, and subnormal support usually costs more than simply widening the exponent by one bit (p. 330).

## Why addition is the hard one

Write both operands with the biased exponent stripped, $X=(-1)^{s_X}2^{E_X-E_0}M_X$ with $M_X\in[1,2)$.

**Multiplication.** By the exponent law $2^a2^b=2^{a+b}$ and commutativity of multiplication, regroup the four factors as

$$X\times Y=(-1)^{s_X\oplus s_Y}\cdot 2^{(E_X-E_0)+(E_Y-E_0)}\cdot M_XM_Y \tag{11.1}$$

The exponent and significand parts **separate exactly**. The exponent needs one $(w_E{+}1)$-bit add; the significand needs one $(w_F{+}1)^2$ multiplier ([[2.4) Multipliers]]). Nothing in either depends on the other.

**Addition.** The same factoring is impossible, because $2^a+2^b$ does not simplify. The best you can do is factor out the larger scale by distributivity,

$$X+Y=2^{E_X-E_0}\Big(M_X\pm M_Y\cdot 2^{-d}\Big),\qquad d=E_X-E_Y\ge0$$

and that leftover $2^{-d}$ is a **shift by a data-dependent amount** — a barrel shifter ([[2.6) Shifters]], [[Barrel Shifters]]). Worse, the bracket is no longer in $[1,2)$: it can be as small as $2^{-w_F-1}$ under cancellation, so the result must be re-normalized, which is a leading-zero count plus a *second* variable shift ([[Leading Zero Counters]], [[Normalizers]]).

> [!important] The whole chapter is one structural asymmetry
> **Multiplication:** one exponent adder, one significand multiplier, and a **1-bit** normalization that is a multiplexer — because $M_X,M_Y\in[1,2)\Rightarrow P=M_XM_Y\in[1,4)$, a *statically known* two-binade range.
> **Addition:** a variable alignment shifter, a significand adder, an LZC, a variable normalization shifter, and a rounding adder — **five** blocks of $\log_2 w_F$ depth in series (p. 340).
> Multiplication is *costlier in area* (a quadratic multiplier) but *structurally trivial*; addition is cheap in area and structurally awful. Every trick below — dual paths, leading-zero anticipation, speculation — exists to attack that chain of five. The multiplier gets one trick (injection rounding) because it only has two.

Two bounds fall straight out of the same algebra, and they size the hardware. **The shifter saturates:** if $d>w_F+1$ then $|Y|<2^{E_Y-E_0+1}\le 2^{E_X-E_0-w_F-1}=\tfrac12\,\mathrm{ulp}(X)$, so under round to nearest $X+Y$ returns $X$ (Fig. 11.2, p. 333) — the exponent difference is saturated at $w_F+2$ and the shifter is $\lceil\log_2(w_F+2)\rceil$ stages deep, not $w_E$. **The adder is narrower than it looks:** in the far cases the rightmost $w_F-1$ bits of the shifted $M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}$ can never influence any bit above the rounding position, so they collapse into a single **sticky** bit ($0$ iff all of them are $0$) and the real addition consumes two $(w_F{+}3)$-bit numbers to produce $w_F+4$ bits (p. 335). Computing just right, applied to a subtraction.

## Floating-point addition and subtraction

### General considerations and terminology

Subtraction is free: $X-Y=X+(-Y)$ holds for every floating-point value except NaN, which is treated identically by both operations, so an adder becomes an adder/subtractor for the price of **one XOR** on $s_Y$ (p. 331). What remains is the *effective* operation — the $\pm$ signal — an effective subtraction when the signs differ. After exception handling (Table 11.1, p. 331) and a swap ensuring $E_X\ge E_Y$, addition is four steps (pp. 333–335):

1. **Alignment shift** — shift $M_Y$ right by $d=E_X-E_Y\le w_F+1$; tentatively set the result exponent to $E_X$.
2. **Exact addition** — $M_X\pm M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}$. Exact, but not necessarily normalized.
3. **Normalization shift** — bring the leading non-zero bit back to position $0$ and fix the exponent.
4. **Rounding** — which may itself overflow the significand and bump the exponent, e.g. $1.1111110\times10^0\to1.000\times10^1$.

The two regimes have names. The **far case** is when the exponents differ enough that the result exponent is $E_X$, plus or minus one (Fig. 11.1); the result sign is always the sign of $X$. The **near case** is $d\in\{0,1\}$ *and* an effective subtraction, so massive **cancellation** can occur and a large normalization shift is needed (Fig. 11.3) — but the alignment shift is then $0$ or $1$ bit only. Two facts about the near case earn their own hardware. The sticky bit is **always zero** (nothing was shifted out), and any cancellation makes the result **exact** — this is Sterbenz's lemma. But when $d=0$ the difference can go negative, so the near case needs a compound adder that returns $|M_X-M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}|$ together with a "sign change" flag (Fig. 11.3d, p. 336).

Reading the widths back:

- The **exponent difference is saturated** at $w_F+2$, so an out-of-range $M_Y$ is shifted entirely away and $|M_X\pm M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}|$ simply adds $0$ (p. 337).
- The **LZC is saturated** too: $\lambda=w_F+4$ means total cancellation, and the pack block emits zero (p. 338).
- The exponent update subtracts $\lambda-1$, not $\lambda$ — the $-1$ pays for the possible 1-bit *right* normalization of Fig. 11.1b. Rather than pay for an extra subtraction on the critical path, $E_X$ is **incremented first**, in parallel with the significand path, and $\lambda$ subtracted from $E_X+1$ (p. 338).
- The exponent update output is $w_E+2$ bits — one sign bit and one overflow bit above the $w_E$ exponent bits — so the pack block can tell overflow from underflow (p. 338).
- The **rounding adder adds a single 1 at the LSB of the concatenated $(w_E{+}2)$-bit exponent and $(w_F{+}1)$-bit fraction, then drops that LSB.** Carry propagation out of the fraction lands in the exponent field and updates it *for free*, resetting the fraction to zeros exactly as required. This is why a biased exponent costs nothing: it changes neither the exponent difference nor the exponent update (pp. 335, 338).

Baseline as drawn implements **round to nearest, ties away**, and flushes subnormals to zero. `flopoco FPAdd we=8 wf=23`.

### From baseline adder to IEEE 754 compliance

Two features are missing, and they cost very differently. **Ties to even** needs the OR of every bit shifted right of the round bit — the sticky. Either replace the first shifter (a quasi-barrel shifter) with a full shifter plus a wide OR, or integrate sticky generation into the shifter itself (Sect. 10.1.5). The sticky must then be *updated* after the normalization shift in the Fig. 11.1b case. Detecting a tie is then "round bit $=1$ and sticky $=0$", and the fix is to force the LSB even. **A handful of gates in the rounding adder; the bulk of the overhead is the sticky computation** (p. 339).

> [!note] Why "ties to even" and not something simpler
> The sticky bit is *mandatory* for the directed rounding modes: $N=1.101\,0001$ must round up to $1.110$ while $N=1.101\,0000$ rounds up to $1.101$. IEEE-compliant hardware pays for a sticky whether it wants to or not, so ties-to-even was chosen as a good use of hardware that already exists (p. 336). In an application-specific context with no directed modes, "ties to away" — a lone $1$ injected at position $-w_F-1$, no sticky at all — is the right call.

**Subnormals** are handled almost entirely in the unpack block. Extract the "is normal" bits $n_X,n_Y$ from the exponent fields and use them twice: as the leading significand bit, $M_X=n_X.F_X$ instead of a hardwired $1$; and in the exponent difference, which becomes $E_X-n_X-(E_Y-n_Y)$ (Sect. 2.5.1, p. 48). That is enough for correct alignment. The cost sits elsewhere: the LZC must be **saturated** so that $E_X-\lambda$ never goes negative, which needs a comparison and a multiplexer between the counter and the shifter — and therefore **forbids the fused LZC+shifter** the baseline used. The rounding adder needs no change at all, thanks to the format encoding of Table 2.1. Area overhead is limited and mostly on the exponent datapath, **but it lands on the critical path** (p. 340). `flopoco IEEEFPAdd wE=8 wF=23`.

### Dual-path architectures and other speculative techniques

The observation that buys everything: **a large alignment shift and a large normalization shift are mutually exclusive.** Suppose $d\ge2$ and the operation is an effective subtraction. Then $M_X\in[1,2)$ while $M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}=M_Y2^{-d}\in[2^{-d},2^{1-d}]\subseteq(0,\tfrac12]$, so by monotonicity of subtraction

$$M_X-M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}\;\ge\;1-\tfrac12\;=\;\tfrac12$$

The difference cannot fall below $\tfrac12$, so **at most one bit** of left normalization is ever needed. Cancellation therefore requires $d\in\{0,1\}$ — and in that case the alignment shift is $0$ or $1$ bit, i.e. a multiplexer. The two shift-heavy blocks live in disjoint halves of the input space. Split them, run both, and pick.

The three highlighted blocks of the baseline become two parallel paths (p. 341):

- **far path** — one large alignment shifter, an adder/subtractor, a very small (2-bit) LZC/normalizer.
- **near path** — a 1-bit alignment mux, a compound adder producing $|M_X-M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}|$, a full-width LZC, and a full-width normalization shifter.

**Overhead is one output multiplexer and one duplicated significand adder** — the shifters are *not* duplicated, each path only builds the one it needs. Without subnormal support the two paths are well balanced. *With* subnormal support the near path can no longer fuse its LZC and shifter, so it carries **three** $\log_2w_F$ operations against the far path's two, and becomes the critical path on its own (p. 342).

**Leading-zero anticipation** fixes that. Replace the LZC with a component that takes $M_X$ and $M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}$ directly and runs *in parallel with* the subtraction, predicting $\lambda$ from the operands rather than measuring it on the result. The prediction can be off by one bit, corrected by a final multiplexer (surveyed in [SN01], improved in [Lut17]).

**Full speculation** is the extreme. Lutz reports 1-cycle double-precision addition at 1 GHz by computing, while the exponents are still being subtracted (p. 342):

- all four possible near-path differences $M_X-M_Y$, $M_Y-M_X$, $M_X-(M_Y\gg1)$, $M_Y-(M_X\gg1)$;
- the far-path shift on *both* operands, not knowing yet which is larger;
- both rounding pre-additions to $M_X$ — $M_X+0.5$ and $M_X+1$ for effective addition, $M_X+0.5$ and $M_X+0.25$ for effective subtraction;
- three far-path additions in parallel, speculatively adding to $M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}$ either the unrounded $M_X$ or one of the two rounded versions.

Total: **two 54-bit incrementers and seven 54-bit adders**, plus a large number of multiplexers.

## Floating-point multiplication

### Baseline architecture

From (11.1) the exponent path is a $(w_E{+}1)$-bit sum $E_X+E_Y$, from which the bias must be subtracted once because both fields carry it. The significand path is a $(w_F{+}1)\times(w_F{+}1)$ multiplier producing $P=M_XM_Y$ as a $(2w_F{+}2)$-bit `ufix(1,`$-2w_F$`)` number. Since $M_X,M_Y\in[1,2)$, monotonicity of multiplication gives $P\in[1,4)$: **exactly two binades**, statically. So normalization is a 1-bit right shift controlled by the leading bit of $P$ — a multiplexer, not a shifter. After it, keep the $w_F$ fraction bits and one round bit at position $-w_F-1$, discard the leading one and everything below: `ufix(-1,`$-w_F-1$`)`, then a rounding adder to `ufix(-1,`$-w_F$`)`. That is round to nearest, ties away (pp. 343–344, Fig. 11.7).

The exponent path has one nice trick. Two tentative exponents are computed, $E_X+E_Y-E_0$ and $E_X+E_Y-E_0+1$; the incremented one is used when the product overflows into $[2,4)$ **or** when the rounding addition overflows the fraction to all zeros. These are **exclusive**, because the largest possible product is

$$(2-2^{-w_F})^2=4-4\cdot2^{-w_F}+2^{-2w_F}\;\xrightarrow{\ \div2\ }\;2-2^{-w_F+1}+2^{-2w_F-1}<2-2^{-w_F}$$

so a normalized product can never round up into the next binade. Exclusivity means a **multiplexer instead of a carry propagation** on the exponent bits — strictly cheaper on the critical path than the FP adder's rounding adder, which must propagate across all $w_E+w_F$ bits (p. 345). `flopoco FPMult we=8 wf=23`.

### IEEE 754 compliance

**Ties to even** is nearly free: an OR tree over the low bits of $P$, and since those bits come out of the compressor tree *first*, the sticky never joins the critical path (p. 345). Contrast the adder, where the sticky is the bulk of the compliance cost.

**Subnormals** are the expensive part, in both area and latency. The product of two subnormals always rounds to $0$, so only one subnormal input need be handled. The naive route — normalize the subnormal input (LZC, shifter, 1-bit wider exponent), multiply, then possibly denormalize the result — **more than doubles the latency** of Fig. 11.7. The better route performs **one shift only**: the true result exponent is deducible from $E_X$, $E_Y$ and the LZC $\lambda$ of the subnormal input, and a significand multiplier works perfectly well on an input whose implicit bit is the "is normal" bit. So the LZC, the exponent update and the shift-distance computation all run *in parallel with* the multiplier, and a single shift of $P$ precedes rounding (pp. 345–346).

### Faithful multiplier

Replacing the exact significand multiplier with a faithful one ([[Truncated Multipliers]]) gives "an appreciable reduction in area and delay". The requirement is that $P$ be faithful to `ufix(1,`$-w_F-1$`)`, i.e. error strictly below $2^{-w_F-1}$; the error budget then closes in both binades (p. 346):

$$P\in[1,2):\quad \underbrace{2^{-w_F-1}}_{\text{faithful }P}+\underbrace{2^{-w_F-1}}_{\text{final rounding}}=2^{-w_F}$$
$$P\in[2,4):\quad \underbrace{2^{-w_F-2}}_{\text{halved by the }\gg1}+\underbrace{2^{-w_F-2}}_{\text{dropped bit }2^{-w_F-2}}+\underbrace{2^{-w_F-1}}_{\text{final rounding}}=2^{-w_F}$$

Both bounds are strict, and $2^{-w_F}$ is exactly $1$ ulp of the result: the multiplier is faithful, and the architecture is **unchanged** — Fig. 11.7 with a narrower $P$. One check is required before believing that: does a faithful $P$ still land in $[1,4)$, so that the 1-bit normalization still suffices? The smallest product, $1$, is exactly representable in `ufix(1,`$-w_F-1$`)` so a faithful multiplier returns it exactly; the largest, $4-4\cdot2^{-w_F}+2^{-2w_F}$, plus the error bound $2^{-w_F-1}$, is still comfortably below $4$. Yes.

### Injection rounding as a speedup

The baseline has **two** $\log_2w_F$-depth operations in series: the multiplier and the rounding adder. Fuse them. Round to nearest, ties away, of a positive $p$ to a multiple of $2^{-w_F}$ is *by definition* a truncation of a shifted value. Write $p=m2^{-w_F}+r$ with $m\in\mathbb{N}$ and $0\le r<2^{-w_F}$, and let $\lfloor\cdot\rfloor_{w_F}$ denote truncation to a multiple of $2^{-w_F}$. Then

$$
\big\lfloor p+2^{-w_F-1}\big\rfloor_{w_F}=\big\lfloor m2^{-w_F}+(r+2^{-w_F-1})\big\rfloor_{w_F}=
\begin{cases}m2^{-w_F}, & r<2^{-w_F-1}\\[2pt] (m+1)2^{-w_F}, & r\ge 2^{-w_F-1}\end{cases}
$$

which is exactly "round up iff the discarded part is at least half an ulp, ties up" — and for a positive significand, ties up *is* ties away. So **adding the constant $2^{-w_F-1}$ before truncating implements round to nearest**, and a constant addend costs nothing more than one extra column absorbed into the bit heap of the multiplier ([[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]], [[Bit Heaps]]). Set $P'=P+2^{-w_F-1}$; when $P'\in[1,2)$, dropping the lower half of $P'$ *is* the rounded result (p. 346).

The wrinkle is the binade. If $P'\in[2,4)$ the result ulp is $2^{-w_F+1}$, so the injection should have been $2^{-w_F}=2\times2^{-w_F-1}$ — one more bit of the same weight, a "correction bit". Adding it with a second adder would put the delay straight back. Instead (p. 347): $P'$ leaves the compressor tree in carry-save form $(C,S)$; complete it with a **compound adder** producing $C+S$ and $C+S+1$ simultaneously ([[Carry-Select Adder]]), and let a multiplexer driven by the overflow bit of $P'$ pick $C+S$ for $P'\in[1,2)$ and $C+S+1$ shifted right one bit for $P'\in[2,4)$. Two slow operations become one.

## Floating-point division

### Baseline architecture

Floating-point division is *easier* than fixed-point division, because a normalized divisor $M_Y\in[1,2)$ removes the singularity at zero entirely. (Several fixed-point division and square-root techniques begin by converting their inputs to a floating-point-like normalized form for exactly this reason, p. 347.) The algorithms themselves belong to [[2.5) Dividers]]; what is left here is floating-point management.

$$X/Y=(-1)^{s_X\oplus s_Y}\cdot2^{E_X-E_Y}\cdot M_X/M_Y \tag{11.2}$$

The exponent is a difference — and the difference of two *biased* fields already has the bias cancelled, so $E_0$ must be added back. With $M_X,M_Y\in[1,2)$, $Q=M_X/M_Y\in(\tfrac12,2)$: again exactly two binades, again a 1-bit normalization, but now a *left* shift when the leading bit $q_0$ is $0$, together with $-1$ on the exponent.

That $-1$ is free. Merge it into the bias addition: instead of adding $E_0$, add $E_0-1+q_0$. Since $E_0=2^{w_E-1}-1$ is **odd** its LSB is $1$, so $E_0-1$ has LSB $0$, and $E_0-1+q_0$ is simply *$E_0$ with its LSB replaced by $q_0$* — a wire substitution, no adder (p. 349). Both exponent adders are kept exact on $w_E+2$ bits so the pack block can read overflow and underflow off the two MSBs.

Rounding happens in two places. Inside the divider, the remainder is used to return $Q$ as a truncation of the exact quotient to `ufix(0,`$-w_F-2$`)`; the extra LSB at $-w_F-2$ is a guard bit that *becomes* the round bit at $-w_F-1$ when $Q\in(\tfrac12,1)$ and the left shift happens. Then the rounding adder concatenates $F_Q$ and $E_Q$ and adds the bit of $F_Q$ at $-w_F-1$, carry propagating into the exponent as in the adder. This is round to nearest ties away — but **ties are impossible in binary floating-point division when input and output formats match** ([Mul+18], lemma 4.13), so the tie rule is irrelevant (p. 349). Finally, $Q\in(\tfrac12,1)\iff M_X<M_Y$, so the normalization is predictable from a plain comparison ([[2.2) Comparators]]) and can be moved *before* the divider as a 1-bit left shift of $M_X$ — which looks more expensive but can shorten latency when the divider does preprocessing such as prescaling. `flopoco FPDiv we=8 wf=23`.

### IEEE 754 compliance

Because ties cannot occur, the baseline is already correctly rounded to nearest. **Directed rounding** needs one addition: an exactly-representable quotient (e.g. $1.0/1.0$) must be returned unmodified in every mode, and exactness corresponds to a **null remainder** — the logical OR of the remainder bits inside the divider, exported as a sticky bit. With that bit, directed modes are straightforward (p. 350).

**Subnormals** cost about what they do in multipliers, with one extra difficulty: unlike multiplication, the both-inputs-subnormal case must be handled (for standard formats the quotient is then never subnormal), and most divider algorithms *require* $M_Y$ normalized, whereas a mantissa multiplier does not care. Simplest fix: normalize inputs into an internal format with a 1-bit wider exponent (LZC + one shifter) before the baseline. Subnormal outputs — "tiny results" — are predictable early from the exponent difference with a 1-bit uncertainty; predicting them can *reduce* latency, since a subnormal result needs fewer quotient bits. In [Bru18], **subnormal inputs add two cycles before the main computation and tiny results add one cycle at the end** (p. 350).

### Faithful divider

Fig. 11.8 returns a faithful result as soon as the divider block itself is faithful to its output format, i.e. $|\delta_Q|=|Q-M_X/M_Y|<2^{-w_F-2}$. Propagating that through the two remaining stages (p. 351):

$$\underbrace{2^{-w_F-2}\xrightarrow{\ \times2\ }2^{-w_F-1}}_{\text{possible left normalization}}\;+\;\underbrace{2^{-w_F-1}}_{\text{rounding adder}}\;=\;2^{-w_F}=1\ \text{ulp}$$

and if the rounding adder instead updates the exponent, the significand is halved and so is the attached error, so the bound survives. What matters is the *bound*, not the format: a slightly wider output format `ufix(0,`$l_Q$`)` is fine as long as $|\delta_Q|<2^{-w_F-2}$ holds — Sect. 9.3.1 builds a quotient from a faithful reciprocal and a faithful multiplier on `ufix(0,`$-w_F-3$`)` and still lands inside that bound. And the extremal quotients $1/2$ and $2-2^{-w_F}$ are exactly representable in `ufix(0,`$-w_F-2$`)`, so a faithful divider returns them and the range $[1/2,2)$ is safe.

If the significand division is done by multiplying by an approximate inverse ([[Division by Reciprocal]]), the divider ends in a multiplier — and injection rounding can fuse the rounding adder into *that* multiplier's compression tree.

### Correct rounding out of a faithfully rounded quotient

Start from $M_Q$ faithful to `ufix(0,`$-w_F-1$`)` — the target format plus **one bit**. Writing $M_{X/Y}=\sigma M_X/M_Y$ for the normalized exact quotient ($\sigma=1$ if $M_X/M_Y\ge1$, else $\sigma=2$),

$$\big|M_Q-M_{X/Y}\big|<2^{-w_F-1} \tag{11.3}$$

Two cases (Fig. 11.9, p. 352):

- **$M_Q$ has LSB $0$**, so it is already a `ufix(0,`$-w_F$`)` number. Then (11.3) says $M_{X/Y}$ is within half an ulp of it, so $M_Q$ *is* the correctly rounded result. Done, for free.
- **$M_Q$ has LSB $1$**, so it is the midpoint of two consecutive floating-point numbers, and (11.3) says the answer is one of those two. Decide by the sign of the remainder $\sigma M_X-M_QM_Y$: positive means $M_{X/Y}>M_Q$, round up; negative, round down. Zero cannot happen when input and output formats are identical.

The deciding multiplication is smaller than it looks. From (11.3) and $M_Y<2$, monotonicity gives $|M_QM_Y-\sigma M_X|<2^{-w_F}$, which is exactly $\mathrm{ulp}(\sigma M_X)$. So in the difference, **only bits below $2^{-w_F}$ can come from $M_QM_Y$** — the upper bits of $M_QM_Y$ must already equal $\sigma M_X$. All that is needed is a *left-truncated* multiplier computing $M_QM_Y$ down to the LSB of $\sigma M_X$: if that bit matches, the remainder is $\ge0$; if not, it is $<0$.

> [!note] What the last ulp costs
> A truncated multiplication, a comparison, a second rounding addition and some logic — **to buy one bit**. The book's own conclusion: for the same accuracy it is often cheaper to keep the faithful quotient in a format one bit wider. If the division is buried inside a larger circuit that is already being sized by an error budget ([[1.2) Error Accuracy]], [[Error Analysis]]), take the faithful quotient and spend the bit, not the hardware (p. 352).

## Floating-point square root

### Baseline architecture

The easiest floating-point operator: unary, never overflows, never underflows, and $E_S$ always fits exactly in $w_E$ bits. Its only difficulty is that halving an exponent needs it to be even:

$$\sqrt{X}=\begin{cases}2^{(E_X-E_0)/2}\times\sqrt{1.F_X} & E_X-E_0 \text{ even} \tag{11.4–11.5}\\[3pt] 2^{(E_X-E_0-1)/2}\times\sqrt{2\times1.F_X} & E_X-E_0 \text{ odd}\end{cases}$$

Since $E_0=2^{w_E-1}-1$ is odd, $E_X-E_0$ is even exactly when the exponent **field** $E_X$ is odd, so the low bit $e=\mathrm{LSB}(E_X)$ selects the case directly — no comparison. On the significand side the possible $\times2$ is a 1-bit shift, i.e. a multiplexer on $e$; on the exponent side, write $E_X=2\lfloor E_X/2\rfloor+e$ and substitute. For $e=1$,

$$
E_S=E_0+\tfrac{E_X-E_0}{2}=E_0+\Big\lfloor\tfrac{E_X}{2}\Big\rfloor-\Big\lfloor\tfrac{E_0}{2}\Big\rfloor
=\Big\lfloor\tfrac{E_X}{2}\Big\rfloor+\Big\lfloor\tfrac{E_0}{2}\Big\rfloor+1
$$

using $E_0-\lfloor E_0/2\rfloor=\lfloor E_0/2\rfloor+1$ for odd $E_0$; the $e=0$ case drops the $+1$ identically, so both collapse into (11.6), $E_S=\lfloor E_X/2\rfloor+\lfloor E_0/2\rfloor+e$, where $\lfloor E_X/2\rfloor$ is just the $w_E-1$ upper bits of the field, $\lfloor E_0/2\rfloor=2^{w_E-2}-1$ is a **constant string of $w_E-2$ ones**, and $e$ is a **carry-in**. One addition, total (p. 354).

The pre-normalized $M_X^\mathrm{norm}\in[1,4)$ makes $M_S=\sqrt{M_X^\mathrm{norm}}\in[1,2)$ — already the target format. So the baseline has **no rounding logic and no normalization logic at all** outside the $\sqrt{\ }$ block itself, because a correctly rounded $M_S$ is necessarily already normalized (p. 354). `flopoco FPSqrt we=8 wf=23`.

### Faithful square root

Faithfulness breaks that "already normalized" guarantee, in exactly **one** input. At the largest $M_X^\mathrm{norm}=M_X^\mathrm{max}=2(2-2^{-w_F})=4-2^{1-w_F}$, a first-order Taylor expansion of $\sqrt{4-\varepsilon}=2\sqrt{1-\varepsilon/4}\approx2(1-\varepsilon/8)$ gives

$$\sqrt{M_X^\mathrm{max}}\approx 2-2^{-w_F-1}-2^{-2w_F-4}$$

which sits a hair below the **midpoint** between the two largest representable mantissas. A faithful implementation (error bound $2^{-w_F}$) is therefore entitled to return $M_S=2.0$ — not representable in `ufix(0,`$-w_F$`)`. If it does, the architecture of Fig. 11.10 silently discards the bit of weight $2^1$ and returns $1.0$ for $\sqrt{4-2^{-w_F+1}}$, and similarly wrong values for every $4^iM_X^\mathrm{max}$ (p. 355).

> [!warning] One input value, catastrophically wrong, invisible to random testing
> This is a single point in a $2^{w_F}$-sized input space. Random verification will never find it. Fix it by an ad hoc clamp on $\sqrt{M_X^\mathrm{max}}$, by saturation inside the $\sqrt{\ }$ block, or by widening $M_S$ to `ufix(1,`$-w_F$`)` and letting its MSB drive a multiplexer between $E_S$ and a precomputed $E_S+1$. The last option is genuinely cheap: no multiplexer on the significand side, and — unlike the multiplier and divider — **no extra rounding step after normalization**.

### IEEE 754 compliance

**Ties cannot occur.** Let $S=M+2^{-w_F-1}$ be an exact midpoint with $M$ in `ufix(0,`$-w_F$`)`. Then

$$S^2=M^2+2^{-w_F}M+2^{-2w_F-2}$$

$M^2$ is a `ufix(1,`$-2w_F$`)` number (leading one at position $0$ or $1$) and $2^{-w_F}M$ is `ufix(`$-w_F,-2w_F$`)`, so $S^2$ has a set bit at position $0$ or $1$ **and** a set bit at position $-2w_F-2$: it spans more than $w_F+1$ significant bits and cannot be a floating-point number of the input format. No input squares to a midpoint, so Fig. 11.10 rounds to nearest whatever the tie rule (p. 356).

**Other modes.** Rounding down and rounding to zero change only the inside of the $\sqrt{\ }$ block and the exception logic; rounding *up* needs the small normalization unit above, for the single $M_X^\mathrm{max}$ case. **Subnormals.** Outputs can never be subnormal (the square root of any input is far from zero), which also means ties never occur for subnormal inputs. Subnormal *inputs* need only be converted to normals in an internal format with the same fraction size and a **1-bit wider exponent** — and since the exponent is about to be halved anyway, the baseline architecture is reused **unchanged**. LZC plus one shifter (p. 356).

### Correct rounding out of faithful rounding

The divider technique transposes directly: substitute $\sqrt{X}$ for $X/Y$ in Fig. 11.9, and determine the sign of $S_0-\sqrt{X}$ as the sign of $S_0^2-X$. The deciding hardware is therefore a **left-truncated squarer** ([Din+10], and see [[3.3) Squarers and Integer Powers]]). This generalizes to any algebraic function (Sect. 16.3.1) — and the message is the same as for division: *this last bit of accuracy may be quite expensive compared to faithful rounding* (p. 357).

## Floating-point comparison

### Specification

The biased exponent encoding is chosen so that the order of positive floating-point numbers, from $0$ to $+\infty$, is the order of their bit patterns read as integers (Table 2.1, p. 51). **Subnormals are included** — so subnormal handling in a comparator is free (p. 357).

The standard interface emits **four mutually exclusive predicates**: $X>Y$, $X=Y$, $X<Y$, and $X?Y$ ("unordered", true iff at least one input is NaN). With the additional rules that $+0$ and $-0$ compare equal and two infinities of the same sign compare equal, the first three are total on everything but NaN. A NaN is never greater, less, or equal to anything — including another NaN — so all three return False (Tables 11.5, 11.6, pp. 358–359).

> [!note] $\le$ is not $\lnot(>)$, but $\ne$ is $\lnot(=)$
> $X\le Y$ and $X\ge Y$ must also return False when either input is NaN, so they are *not* the Boolean negations of $X>Y$ and $X<Y$ — they differ exactly on the NaN inputs. But $X\ne Y$ **is** the plain negation of $X=Y$, which is why the idiomatic NaN test in every programming language is `x != x` (p. 358).

### Implementation and specializations

Unpack computes "is zero", "is infinity" and "is NaN" for both operands — this is the *only* difference between an IEEEfloat comparator and an Nfloat one. Concatenate $E_X$ with $F_X$ into an integer $I_X$, likewise $I_Y$, feed both to an integer comparator ([[2.2) Comparators]]) producing $I_X<I_Y$, $I_X=I_Y$, $I_X>I_Y$, and let a flat layer of logic implement Tables 11.5 and 11.6 from those and the exception flags (Fig. 11.12, p. 359). `flopoco FPComparator we=8 wf=23`.

Specialization is mostly about the output layer: needing only $X>Y$ trivially deletes the logic for the others. It does **not** necessarily shrink the integer comparator, because signed numbers still have to be compared — but when the sign of both inputs is known fixed, only one of $I_X<I_Y$ and $I_X>I_Y$ is needed and the integer comparator halves (p. 360).

## On FPGAs

- **Do the swap on the whole number, not the exponent.** FloPoCo takes the swap decision by comparing the *concatenation* of exponent and fraction, which guarantees $M_X>M_Y$ even in the near cases. The sign of the result is then always the sign of $X$, and the significand block collapses from $|M_X\pm M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}|$ to a plain $M_X\pm M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}$ — no absolute value, no sign-change flag. This is a good trade **specifically on FPGAs**, because that early wide comparison is fast and cheap on the fast carry logic (Sect. 6.3, p. 148; note p. 338). The book is explicit that the best $|M_X\pm M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}|$ is technology-dependent: on ASIC the compound adder of Sect. 5.3.7 may be worth it, on FPGA the comparison-and-swap usually wins (p. 338).
- **`Nfloat` versus IEEE.** For an operator buried in an application-specific datapath, non-standard formats and relaxed compliance make perfect sense; as soon as the architecture is programmable and exposes standard formats, users expect full IEEE 754 and the predictability is worth the overhead. The book cites GPUs as the case study: liberties were acceptable while GPU programming was niche, and became unacceptable the moment it went mainstream (p. 330).
- Injection rounding is a natural fit for the FPGA bit-heap flow — the injected constant is one more column in a heap that is going to be compressed anyway ([[Bit Heaps]], [[Compressor Trees]]), and the compound adder maps onto the same carry chain as the plain one.

## Trade-offs

**Adder variants.** "Slow blocks" counts the $\log_2 w_F$-depth components on the critical path.

| Adder variant | Slow blocks on the critical path | Area vs. baseline | Accuracy | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Baseline, RN ties-away, no subnormals | 5: align shifter, significand adder, LZC, normalizer, rounding adder | $1\times$ | $\le\tfrac12$ ulp, ties away | 337, 340 |
| $+$ ties to even | 5 (sticky foldable into the shifter, Sect. 10.1.5) | full shifter $+$ wide OR, *or* sticky-in-shifter; a handful of gates in the rounding adder | $\le\tfrac12$ ulp, ties even | 339 |
| $+$ subnormal support | 5, and the LZC/shifter can no longer be fused | limited, mostly exponent datapath — but on the critical path | unchanged | 340 |
| **Dual path**, no subnormals | far 2, near 2 — "quite well balanced" | $+$ one significand adder, $+$ one output mux; shifters *not* duplicated | unchanged | 341–342 |
| **Dual path** $+$ subnormals | far 2, **near 3** — the near path becomes the critical path | as above | unchanged | 342 |
| $+$ leading-zero anticipation | near path LZC moves off the critical path, $+1$ correction mux | $+$ LZA (bigger than an LZC) | unchanged | 342 |
| Full speculation (Lutz, ARM) | 1 cycle, double precision, 1 GHz | **2$\times$ 54-bit incrementers, 7$\times$ 54-bit adders**, many muxes | unchanged | 342 |

**Faithful versus correctly rounded, per operator.**

| Operator | Cost of the last ulp (correct rounding) | What faithful buys | Page |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Add | sticky over the full shifted-out tail $+$ tie logic; the sticky *is* the compliance cost | nothing worth having — the adder is already narrow | 339 |
| Multiply | exact $(2w_F{+}2)$-bit product $+$ rounding adder; ties-even is an OR tree off the critical path | faithful significand multiplier, "appreciable reduction in area and delay"; **architecture unchanged**, error $<2^{-w_F}=1$ ulp | 344–346 |
| Divide | left-truncated $M_QM_Y$ multiply $+$ comparison $+$ second rounding addition $+$ logic, **for one bit** | divider faithful to $2^{-w_F-2}$; result faithful after normalization and rounding | 348, 351–352 |
| Square root | left-truncated squarer to sign $S_0^2-X$ | faithful $\sqrt{\ }$, but requires the $M_X^\mathrm{max}$ fix: $M_S$ widened to `ufix(1,`$-w_F$`)`, MSB muxing $E_S$ / precomputed $E_S{+}1$ | 355–356 |
| Compare | n/a — exact by construction | n/a | 357 |

Pick the **baseline adder** whenever it is hidden inside an application-specific datapath: five slow blocks in series is fine if the pipeline has the stages, and it is the smallest thing that works. Pick the **dual path** when single-cycle or low-latency addition is the requirement, and pair it with leading-zero anticipation the moment you also need subnormals — otherwise the near path eats the gain you paid for. Pick **faithful** operators for multiply, divide and square root inside a datapath with an error budget; pick **correctly rounded** only when the operator is user-visible or when reproducibility across implementations is a requirement. For the adder, do not bother with faithful: there is nothing to save.

The only hard quantities the text gives are the bit widths in Figs. 11.5–11.10, the block-count arguments above, Lutz's "two 54-bit incrementers and seven 54-bit adders" (p. 342), the "more than doubles the latency" for naive subnormal multiplication (p. 345), and Bruguera's "+2 cycles for subnormal inputs, +1 cycle for tiny results" (p. 350). Anything more precise needs outside research or your own synthesis run.

## Worked example: an 8-bit addition with a worst-case alignment

Format: $w_E=4$, $w_F=3$, bias $E_0=2^{3}-1=7$. Take

$$X=1.101\times2^{3}=\mathtt{0\;1010\;101}\qquad Y=1.011\times2^{-1}=\mathtt{0\;0110\;011}$$

**Exponent difference and swap.** $d=E_X-E_Y=10-6=4=w_F+1$ — the largest difference that is *not* discarded outright, the case of Fig. 11.1d. Both operands positive, so this is an effective addition, and $E_X>E_Y$ means no swap. Result exponent tentatively $3$.

**Alignment shift.** $M_{Y}=1.011$ shifted right 4 places is $0.0001011$. The datapath keeps $w_F+3=6$ bits, positions $0$ down to $-5$; everything below is OR-ed into the sticky:

$$M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}=0.00010\quad\Big|\quad \text{shifted out: } \underbrace{11}_{\text{positions }-6,-7}\;\Rightarrow\;\mathrm{sticky}=1$$

**Exact addition,** on the same 6 bits — the $|M_X\pm M_{Y_\mathrm{a}}|$ block emits $w_F+4=7$ bits, so $M_S=\mathtt{01.10110}$ with the overflow bit $0$:

$$\begin{array}{r} 1.10100\\ +\;0.00010\\ \hline 1.10110 \end{array}$$

**Normalization.** The leading one is already at position $0$: $\lambda=1$ leading zero on $M_S$, so the exponent update subtracts $\lambda-1=0$ and the exponent stays $3$, $N=1.10110$.

**Rounding.** Keep $w_F=3$ fraction bits; the round bit is position $-4$ and the sticky is (bit at $-5$) OR (shifted-out) $=0\lor1=1$, so the discarded tail is strictly above the midpoint and we round up regardless of tie rule, $1.101+0.001=1.110$:

$$\underbrace{1.101}_{\text{keep}}\;\underbrace{1}_{R=1}\;\underbrace{0\,|\,11}_{\text{sticky}=1}\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad R=1.110\times2^{3}=\mathtt{0\;1010\;110}$$

**Error.** Exactly, $X+Y=13+\tfrac{11}{16}=13.6875$ and $R=14$; with $\mathrm{ulp}(R)=2^{E-w_F}=2^{0}=1$,

$$\big|R-(X+Y)\big|=0.3125=\mathbf{0.3125\ \text{ulp}}\;\le\;\tfrac12\ \text{ulp}\quad\checkmark$$

Two instructive comparisons. **Truncating** instead of rounding — dropping the round bit, which is what the baseline would do without its rounding adder — gives $1.101\times2^3=13$, an error of $0.6875$ ulp: still **faithful** ($<1$ ulp), but not correctly rounded. And had $d$ been one larger, $d=5>w_F+1$, the operator would have returned $X=13$ *by design* — the same $0.6875$ ulp error, reached by skipping the entire significand datapath. The rounding adder, the sticky and the tie logic exist purely to close the gap between those two numbers.
