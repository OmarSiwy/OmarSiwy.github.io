Division is the operator where the two arithmetic families finally diverge. Addition and multiplication have one right answer computed one way; division has two, and they optimise opposite things.

## Specification

- **Inputs.** $X$ on $w_X$ bits, $D \ne 0$ on $w_D$ bits, both fixed-point (`ufix`/`sfix` per [[1.1) Number Formats]]). Most hardware dividers restrict $D$ to a *normalized significand* $D \in [1,2)$ — the singularity at $D \to 0$ is what makes general fixed-point division expensive.
- **Outputs.** $Q$ with ulp $u_Q = 2^{\ell_Q}$, and (digit-recurrence only) an **exact** remainder $R$ on the format of $D$.
- **Accuracy.** Digit recurrence: $R$ is exact, so $Q$ is *correctly* roundable at no extra cost. Multiplicative: faithful only, $|\delta_Q| = |Q - X/D| < 2^{-w_F-2}$ (9.46, p. 289) — see [[1.2) Error Accuracy]].
- **Optimised for.** Digit recurrence minimises **area** (one add/sub per cycle, latency $\propto w_Q$). Multiplicative minimises **latency** (iteration count $\propto \log w_Q$) by spending the multipliers of [[2.4) Multipliers]].

Every choice below traces back to one line: *do you already own a multiplier?*

## Formats: what division must produce

Euclidean division fixes $Q, R$ by

$$X = QD + R, \qquad 0 \le R < D \tag{9.1}$$

Dividing through by $D$ and naming $\delta = R/D$ (a rescaling, nothing more) gives the form that generalises to fixed-point:

$$\frac{X}{D} = Q + \delta, \qquad -\frac{u_Q}{2} \le \delta < \frac{u_Q}{2} \tag{9.4}$$

> [!note] The remainder *is* the rounding error
> (9.4) is literally the definition of round-to-nearest applied to $X/D$. So an algorithm that produces an exact $R$ has produced the rounding decision for free, and an algorithm that produces an accurate-enough $Q$ can recover $R = X - QD$ (9.5). This is why digit recurrences round correctly and multiplicative methods need an error budget.

Format consequences (§9.1.1–9.1.3, pp. 262–263):

| Case | $w_Q$ | $w_R$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| unsigned integer | $w_X$ (worst case $D=1$) | $w_D$ |
| signed integer | $w_X + 1$ ($X/{-1}$ overflows two's complement) | $w_D - 1$ unsigned |
| fixed-point | $m_Q = m_X - \ell_D$ (±1) | $\ell_R = \ell_Q + \ell_D$, $m_R = m_D$ |

The last row is the useful one: **$R$ has the size of $D$, whatever $\ell_Q$ you asked for**. Accuracy of $Q$ costs iterations, not remainder width.

**Normalized significands.** With $X, D \in [1,2)$ the quotient lies in $(1/2, 2)$, so $m_Q = 1$ and one bit of normalisation fixes it. The literature's $[0.5,1)$ convention is equivalent — dividing $2X$ by $2D$ changes nothing (p. 263). The real payoff is that $D$ can never approach zero.

## The digit recurrence

Work in radix $\beta$ and write $X$ in $k = \lceil w_X/\alpha \rceil$ digits when $\beta = 2^\alpha$ (9.14). Define

$$R_0 = \beta^{-k} X, \qquad R_{i+1} = \beta R_i - q_{k-i-1} D \tag{9.15–9.16}$$

**Why this computes the quotient.** Unroll by repeated substitution — each step replaces $R_{i-1}$ using the same rule, and $\beta$ distributes over the subtraction:

$$R_i = \beta R_{i-1} - q_{k-i}D = \beta^2 R_{i-2} - (\beta q_{k-i+1} + q_{k-i})D = \dots = \beta^i R_0 - \Big(\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\beta^j q_{k-i+j}\Big) D$$

At $i = k$, with $R_0 = \beta^{-k}X$ so that $\beta^k R_0 = X$ exactly:

$$R_k = X - \underbrace{\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\beta^j q_j\Big)}_{=\,Q} D \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad X = QD + R_k \tag{9.21–9.22}$$

which is (9.1) provided we can also guarantee $0 \le R_k < D$. So the recurrence is correct *by construction* — the entire difficulty is packed into choosing $q$.

**The invariant that makes it work.** Impose $0 \le R_i < D$ at every step (9.24) and prove it is preserved. Take

$$q_{k-i-1} = \left\lfloor \frac{\beta R_i}{D} \right\rfloor \tag{9.25}$$

By the induction hypothesis $0 \le R_i < D$, so $0 \le \beta R_i / D < \beta$ and $q$ is a legal radix-$\beta$ digit. By the defining property of the floor, $0 \le \frac{\beta R_i}{D} - q < 1$; multiplying by $D > 0$ (order-preserving) gives $0 \le \beta R_i - qD = R_{i+1} < D$. Induction closes.

> [!important] The catch
> (9.25) selects the digit by *performing a division*. The whole subject exists to replace that floor with something cheap, and every cheap replacement needs slack in the invariant $0 \le R_i < D$ — slack that (9.24) does not have.

## Restoring Division

Radix 2 collapses the digit product to $q_i D \in \{0, D\}$: assume $q_i = 1$, subtract, and if the result went negative, **restore** the old remainder. Fixing the shift by the change of variable $R_i = \beta^{w_X - i} R'_i$ makes every iteration identical:

$$
R'_0 = 2^{-w_X}X, \qquad
q_{w_X-1-i} = \begin{cases} 1 & 2R'_i - D \ge 0\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}, \qquad
R'_{i+1} = 2R'_i - q_{w_X-1-i}D \tag{9.11–9.13}
$$

Cost, from Fig. 9.4, p. 269: one $w_D+1$-bit subtractor (the $+1$ is the sign bit) and one $w_D$-bit multiplexer, selected by the sign bit of the difference. The subtraction only touches the integer bits of $R'_i$ — the low $w_X$ bits are just $X$ shifting through, which is exactly the "bring down the next digit" of paper-and-pencil (p. 267).

Latency is $w_Q$ cycles, each one full carry propagation of $w_D$ bits. **Unrolling in space does not help delay**: the multiplexer is controlled by the MSB of the subtraction, which arrives last. It only helps throughput, via pipelining (p. 267).

## Non-Restoring Division

The restore step is a second addition in the $q_i = 0$ case, which on a register-starved machine costs a cycle. Non-restoring removes it by *letting the remainder go negative* and fixing it next iteration — which is the maximally redundant radix-2 instance of the machinery below: $\beta = 2$, digit set $\{-1, +1\}$, so $\gamma = 1 = \beta - 1$ and $\rho = 1$.

$$q_{-i} = \operatorname{sign}(R_i) \in \{-1, +1\}, \qquad R_{i+1} = 2R_i - q_{-i}D$$

Exactly one add-or-subtract per iteration, and the operation is chosen from the **sign bit of the previous remainder** — available before the carry chain of the current step finishes. The price is one extra iteration to correct a possibly negative final remainder (footnote 2, p. 267).

The trade is target-dependent: on ASIC there is no benefit over restoring, because both cost two carry propagations' worth of critical path per quotient bit; on FPGAs with small LUTs it does better; high-radix dividers beat both on modern FPGAs (p. 267).

## Redundant Digit Sets and the Selection-Interval Overlap

This is the load-bearing derivation of the whole chapter.

Replace the tight digit set $\{0,\dots,\beta-1\}$ by the signed redundant set of [[1.1.2) Redundant Number Systems]]

$$\mathcal{D} = \{-\gamma, \dots, 0, \dots, \gamma\}, \qquad \gamma \ge \beta/2 \tag{9.27}$$

so $\mathcal{D}$ holds at least $\beta+1$ digits, and relax the invariant to something symmetric around zero:

$$-\rho D \le R_i \le \rho D \tag{9.28}$$

**Fixing $\rho$.** For (9.28) to be preserved, take the worst case: $\beta R_i$ at most $\beta\rho D$, and the largest digit available is $\gamma$. Then $R_{i+1} \le \rho D$ requires $\beta\rho D - \gamma D \le \rho D$, i.e. $\rho(\beta-1) \le \gamma$. The symmetric calculation on the negative side gives the same. The largest usable $\rho$ — and larger is better, because $\rho$ *is* the slack — is the **redundancy factor**

$$\rho = \frac{\gamma}{\beta-1} \tag{9.29}, \qquad \rho > \tfrac12 \text{ since } \gamma \ge \beta/2$$

**The selection interval.** Now impose (9.28) on $R_{i+1} = \beta R_i - q_{-i}D$ directly. Divide by $D > 0$, which preserves the inequalities:

$$
-\rho \le \frac{\beta R_i}{D} - q_{-i} \le \rho \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
-\rho + \frac{\beta R_i}{D} \;\le\; q_{-i} \;\le\; \rho + \frac{\beta R_i}{D} \tag{9.30}
$$

Read it the other way round. For a fixed digit $k$, the set of scaled remainders $y = \beta R_i / D$ for which $k$ is a *legal* choice is

$$S_k = [\,k - \rho,\; k + \rho\,], \qquad |S_k| = 2\rho$$

an interval of width $2\rho$ centred on $k$. Two consecutive selection intervals **overlap** iff the top of $S_k$ reaches past the bottom of $S_{k+1}$:

$$k + \rho \;\ge\; (k+1) - \rho \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 2\rho \ge 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \rho \ge \tfrac12$$

and the overlap has width $2\rho - 1 > 0$, strictly positive exactly because $\gamma \ge \beta/2$ forces $\rho > 1/2$. **This is the entire point of redundancy.** With the non-redundant $\lfloor \cdot \rfloor$ of (9.25) the intervals are half-open and abut with zero overlap ($\rho = 1/2$), so the boundary between $q=k$ and $q=k+1$ is a single exact point and you must know $y$ exactly — i.e. divide.

**Why overlap buys you an approximation.** Suppose you only compute an approximation $\hat{y}$ with $|y - \hat y| \le \varepsilon$. Choosing $q$ by comparing $\hat y$ against fixed thresholds is safe iff every uncertainty band $[\hat y, \hat y + \varepsilon]$ lies wholly inside some $S_k$. Since consecutive $S_k$ overlap by $2\rho - 1$, thresholds can be placed anywhere in the overlap, and such a placement exists as long as

$$\varepsilon \;<\; 2\rho - 1$$

More redundancy ⇒ wider overlap ⇒ coarser $\hat y$ ⇒ smaller selection table. That is the whole design knob.

**Back to hardware coordinates.** Multiplying (9.30) by $D$ turns the boundaries into the straight lines $\beta R_i = (k \pm \rho)D$ of the **P-D diagram** (Fig. 9.5, p. 274) — $\beta R_i$ against $D$, one wedge per digit, wedges overlapping. Truncating $D$ to $a$ bits and $\beta R_i$ to $b$ bits tiles the plane with rectangles of size $2^{-a}\times 2^{-b}$; a digit can be assigned to a rectangle iff the rectangle sits entirely inside one wedge. Note the wedges *narrow* as $D \to 0$ (their vertical extent is $(2\rho-1)D$), which is why the diagram is clipped at $D = 1/2$: quotient digit selection from a fixed-size table is only possible for normalized $D$.

> [!important] The Pentium FDIV bug is a mislabelled rectangle
> Fig. 9.6 (p. 275) shows the radix-4, $\gamma = 2$ case with 3 bits of $D$ and 5 bits of $\beta R_i$. One rectangle, marked `!`, has its top-left corner only in the domain of digit 2 and its bottom-right corner only in the domain of digit 1 — it straddles both wedges and *cannot* be labelled. At this resolution the selection function does not exist; going to 6 bits of $\beta R_i$ (Fig. 9.7, p. 276) makes every rectangle labellable.
>
> Rectangles fully outside the convergence domain are genuine don't-cares and may be given any value. Rectangles containing even one point of the convergence domain must carry a valid digit. **The 1995 Pentium FDIV bug was a handful of these border cells being improperly labelled** (footnote 3, p. 275) — don't-cares that were not, in fact, don't-cares. The failure was not in the algorithm but in the table's boundary rows.

## SRT Division

Named after Sweeney, Robertson [Rob58] and Tocher [Toc58], who arrived at redundant-digit division independently in 1958 (p. 272). SRT is the digit recurrence (9.16) run with digit set (9.27) and the selection function derived above.

**Radix 4, $\gamma = 2$** ($\rho = 2/3$). Digits $\{-2,\dots,2\}$, so $q_{-i}D$ is $0$, $\pm D$ or $\pm 2D$ — a multiplexer and a one-bit shift, no multiplier. Selection needs 6 bits of $\beta R_i$ and 3 bits of $D$ (Fig. 9.7). Encoding each digit in two's complement on three bits, $q_{-i} = -4q^{\ominus}_{-i} + q^{\oplus}_{-i}$ (9.32), lets the multiplexer and add/sub merge into a single row of 6-input LUTs (p. 276).

**Radix 4, $\gamma = 3$** ($\rho = 1$, maximally redundant). Selection intervals now overlap fully; 4 bits of $\beta R_i$ and 1 bit of $D$ suffice (Fig. 9.9, p. 278) — a much smaller table. But $3D$ is now needed, giving the multiplexer a fourth input, which breaks the 6-LUT packing (p. 277). $3D = D + (D \ll 1)$ can be precomputed once outside the loop, trading a row of registers against a row of LUTs; recomputing it locally instead adds to the critical path.

**Radix 8, $\gamma = 7$** exists in FloPoCo but is both larger and slower than either radix-4 variant, consistent with the earlier FPGA survey [SBD04] (p. 277).

### Initialization and termination

Convergence needs (9.28) true from the first iteration. For a maximally redundant system ($\rho = 1$), $R_1 = X/2$ works; otherwise $1/2 < \rho \le 1$ and $R_1 = X/4$ is required, with the quotient scaled back by the same factor (p. 279).

Unrolling the significand recurrence as before,

$$R_{i+1} = \beta^i\Big(R_1 - D\sum_{j=1}^{i}\beta^{-j}q_{-j}\Big) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{R_1}{D} = Q_i + \beta^{-i}\frac{R_{i+1}}{D} \tag{9.33–9.34}$$

and since $|R_{i+1}/D| \le \rho$ by the invariant,

$$\left|\frac{R_1}{D} - Q_i\right| \le \rho\,\beta^{-i} \tag{9.35}$$

Stop when this drops below the target. For a correctly rounded floating-point quotient you need $w_F+3$ bits, so $k = \lceil (w_F+3)/\alpha \rceil$ iterations at radix $2^\alpha$ (p. 271). Crucially (9.34) is an **exact** identity — no rounding happened anywhere — so correct rounding is a decision, not an approximation (p. 280).

### On-the-fly conversion to binary

The quotient arrives as $\sum \beta^j q_j$ with signed digits. One final subtraction converts it: split $q_j = -\beta q^{\ominus}_j + q^{\oplus}_j$ and use distributivity of the sum,

$$Q = -\beta\sum_j \beta^j q^{\ominus}_j \;+\; \sum_j \beta^j q^{\oplus}_j \tag{9.36–9.37}$$

both operands being plain concatenations of the digit bits. Cheap, but it adds a full carry propagation to the latency.

To avoid it, convert **on the fly**, maintaining alongside $Q_i$ a pre-decremented $Q^{\ominus}_i = Q_i - \beta^{-i}$ so that a negative digit can borrow from it instead of propagating a carry:

$$
q_{-i} > 0:\ \begin{cases}Q_i = Q_{i-1} \Vert q^{\oplus}_{-i}\\ Q^{\ominus}_i = Q_{i-1}\Vert(q^{\oplus}_{-i}-1)\end{cases}
\quad
q_{-i} = 0:\ \begin{cases}Q_i = Q_{i-1}\Vert 0\\ Q^{\ominus}_i = Q^{\ominus}_{i-1}\Vert(\beta-1)\end{cases}
\quad
q_{-i} < 0:\ \begin{cases}Q_i = Q^{\ominus}_{i-1}\Vert q^{\oplus}_{-i}\\ Q^{\ominus}_i = Q^{\ominus}_{i-1}\Vert(q^{\oplus}_{-i}-1)\end{cases}
$$

(9.41–9.43, p. 282; $\Vert$ is concatenation at position $\beta^{-i}$). Every update is wiring. The cost is two multiplexers and two registers of quotient width per stage — fast but area-hungry.

### High-radix integer division and prescaling

The P-D tiling fails for $D$ near zero, so high-radix SRT cannot divide two arbitrary integers directly. Two escapes (p. 282). (i) Select the digit by comparing $\beta R_i$ in parallel against precomputed multiples of $D$ lying in the overlap regions ($\pm D/2$, $\pm 3D/2$ for radix 4, $\gamma=2$, Fig. 9.11, p. 283) — but that puts two carry propagations in sequence, so it rarely beats plain restoring; it pays better with a *non-redundant* digit set at radix 8, computing all $8R_i - q_iD$ in parallel and picking the first non-negative (one carry propagation, plus a priority encoder and an 8:1 mux). (ii) Multiply $X$ and $D$ by $2^k$ so the MSB of $D$ is 1 — a leading zero counter (see [[2.6.2) Leading Zero Counters]]) and two shifters, i.e. normalisation.

**Prescaling** goes further: multiply $X$ and $D$ by the same factor to push $D$ towards 1, where the wedges are widest. Multiplying by $3/2$ when $X < 5/8$ costs one addition and removes the problem cell of Fig. 9.6 while creating many don't-cares (p. 283). The stronger scheme of Table 9.2 (p. 285) reads the 3 leading non-trivial bits of $D$ and applies a factor built from two additions ($1+\frac12$, $1+\frac18+\frac14$, …), landing $D_{\text{scaled}}$ in $[\frac{126}{128}, \frac{144}{128}] = [0.984, 1.125]$. Then the selection function stops depending on $D$ entirely: **round $\beta R_i$ to the nearest integer** — a 4-bit addition — and you have the digit (Fig. 9.12b, p. 284). This is what enables very high radices, at the cost of duplicated prescaling hardware, wider iteration datapaths (one more MSB, three more LSBs), and a worse initial scaling: for $X \in [1,2)$ prescaled by Table 9.2, $R_1 = 2^{-3}X$ is needed to keep $0 \le R_1 \le \rho D$, which may add iterations (p. 286).

### Speeding up the partial remainder

Keep $R_i$ in **carry-save** form ([[2.1.2) CSA, Carry-Save Adder]], [[2.3.2) Compressor Trees]]). Since $q_{-i}$ is a few bits, $q_{-i}D$ is a few addends, and $\beta R_i - q_{-i}D$ compresses to height 2 in very few levels — no carry propagation at all in the loop (p. 286).

The bill lands on digit selection. $R_i$ now occupies twice the bits, so the table of Fig. 9.8 would take $6+6+3 = 15$ inputs instead of 9. Fix it with a small fast carry-propagate adder over the leading bits — but truncating a redundant number at position $t$ loses an unknown carry of weight $2^t$:

$$\beta \widetilde R_i = \beta R_i + \delta, \qquad \delta = 0 \ \text{or}\ -2^t \tag{9.44}$$

a one-sided approximation, $\beta\widetilde R_i \le \beta R_i \le \beta \widetilde R_i + 2^t$. Substituting the worst case into (9.30) tightens the selection constraint to

$$-\rho + \frac{2^t}{D} + \frac{\beta\widetilde R_i}{D} \;\le\; q_{-i} \;\le\; \rho + \frac{\beta \widetilde R_i}{D} \tag{9.45}$$

The ignored carry eats $2^t/D$ off the overlap. Redundancy absorbs it — that is precisely the $\varepsilon < 2\rho - 1$ budget above — but not for free: at $t = -4$, 6 bits of $\beta\widetilde R_i$ and 3 bits of $D$ are no longer enough (Fig. 9.14, p. 287).

On ASIC this is the dominant lever. A 1997 survey of radix-2 and radix-4 implementations concluded that *divider performance is only weakly sensitive to reasonable choices of architecture but significantly improved by aggressive circuit techniques* [HOH97] (p. 287) — which is why radix-2 with a redundant remainder is a perfectly sane design point. Bruguera's low-latency double-precision divider [Bru18] stacks everything at once: each cycle is three radix-4 steps ($64 = 2^{2\times3}$), $R_i$ is carry-save, Table 9.2 prescaling shrinks the selection input to 6 bits of $\beta\widetilde R_i$, and all possible $\beta R_i - q_{-i}D$ are speculatively computed in parallel while $q_{-i}$ is being determined, a multiplexer picking the winner (p. 288). Latency bought with area, throughout.

## Division by Reciprocal

Different family: approximate $1/D$, then multiply by $X$ (Fig. 9.15, p. 289). Only usable when $D$ is bounded away from zero, i.e. on significands $D \in [1,2)$.

**Error budget.** Insert the exact intermediate $XR$ and rewrite $\delta_Q$ as a sum of two errors you control separately:

$$\delta_Q = Q - \frac{X}{D} = \underbrace{(Q - XR)}_{\delta_{\text{mult}}} + \underbrace{X\Big(R - \frac1D\Big)}_{X\delta_{\text{recip}}} \tag{9.47–9.49}$$

Triangle inequality, then $|X| < 2$, gives $|\delta_Q| < |\delta_{\text{mult}}| + 2|\delta_{\text{recip}}|$ (9.50–9.51). Against the faithful-divider target $|\delta_Q| < 2^{-w_F-2}$ (9.46), with a faithful truncated multiplier ($|\delta_{\text{mult}}| < 2^{\ell_Q}$) and faithful reciprocal ($|\delta_{\text{recip}}| < 2^{\ell_R}$), the budget splits as $\ell_Q = -w_F - 3$, $\ell_R = -w_F - 4$: three guard bits on the quotient, four on the reciprocal (p. 290). The same accounting in [[1.2) Error Accuracy]]; the truncated multiplier is the one from [[2.4.3) Truncated Multipliers]]. If instead the reciprocal is *tabulated*, it can be correctly rounded and $\ell_R = -w_F-3$ suffices.

**Generic approximation.** $1/x$ on $[1,2)$ is just a function, so the machinery of [[4.3) Polynomial Approximation]] and [[4.2) Table-Based Function Evaluation]] applies. Pasca [Pas12] found a degree-2 piecewise polynomial the most attractive option for single precision on DSP-enabled FPGAs; for double precision, reciprocal-specific iterations win (p. 291).

## Newton-Raphson

Zero-finding on $f(r) = 1/r - D$, whose root is $1/D$. With $f'(r) = -1/r^2$:

$$r_{i+1} = r_i - \frac{f(r_i)}{f'(r_i)} = r_i + r_i^2\Big(\frac{1}{r_i} - D\Big) = 2r_i - D r_i^2 \tag{9.52–9.53}$$

Division-free: one square and one multiply, necessarily in sequence.

**Quadratic convergence.** Define the reciprocal remainder $e_i = 1 - Dr_i$ (9.54). Substitute (9.53) and complete the square (the binomial identity $1 - 2u + u^2 = (1-u)^2$ with $u = Dr_i$):

$$e_{i+1} = 1 - Dr_{i+1} = 1 - 2Dr_i + (Dr_i)^2 = (1 - Dr_i)^2 = e_i^2 \tag{9.55}$$

The relative error is $\varepsilon_i = \frac{r_i - 1/D}{1/D} = Dr_i - 1 = -e_i$, so $\varepsilon_{i+1} = \varepsilon_i^2$ as well: **the number of correct bits doubles per iteration**, provided $|e_0| < 1$. And $e_i \ge 0$ for $i \ge 1$ gives $r_i \le 1/D$, which saves the weight-0 bit of $R$ in Fig. 9.15 (p. 292).

> [!note] Newton-Raphson is self-correcting; this is the reason to prefer it
> (9.55) is stated in terms of the *actual* $r_i$ the hardware holds, not the ideal one. If rounding perturbs $r_i$, $e_i = 1 - Dr_i$ measures the perturbed value, and the next step still squares *that*. Errors from earlier iterations are re-measured against $D$ and annihilated. The book's design rule follows: keep the per-step rounding errors of the order of $e_i^2$ and near-quadratic convergence survives (p. 293).

**Initial approximation.** Truncate $D$ to $\ell_T$ bits, $D_0 = \lfloor D \rfloor_{\ell_T}$, and tabulate

$$R_0 = \left\lfloor \frac{1}{D_0 + 2^{\ell_T}}\right\rfloor_{\ell_0} \tag{9.56–9.57}$$

which forces $R_0 \le 1/D$ and $1/2 \le R_0 < 1$. Splitting $e_0$ into the two truncation contributions and bounding each term,

$$
e_0 = \underbrace{(D_0+2^{\ell_T})}_{\le 2}\underbrace{\Big(\tfrac{1}{D_0+2^{\ell_T}} - R_0\Big)}_{<2^{\ell_0}} + \underbrace{(D_0 - D + 2^{\ell_T})}_{<0}\underbrace{R_0}_{<1}
\;\Longrightarrow\; 0 \le e_0 < 2\cdot 2^{\ell_0} + 2^{\ell_T} \tag{9.61–9.62}
$$

Choosing $\ell_T = \ell_0 + 1$ gives $0 \le e_0 < 4\cdot 2^{\ell_0}$ — actually nearer $3 \cdot 2^{\ell_0}$ once the correlation between $D_0$ and $R_0$ is accounted for (p. 293).

**Sizing the datapath** (p. 294). Because accuracy doubles, aim for $\ell_{i+1} \approx 2\ell_i$ and $\delta_i \approx 2^{\ell_i}$. Use a **squarer** rather than a multiplier for $r_i^2$ ([[3.3) Squarers and Integer Powers]]) — but an *exact* one, since a truncated squarer would stop $R_{i+1}$ from being twice as accurate as $R_i$. Truncate $D$ in early iterations; feed full $D$ in the last. Use a truncated multiplier for the product. Net: only two rounding errors per iteration, and a datapath whose width **doubles every step**.

## Quadratic Series Expansion

Same convergence, shorter critical path. From the geometric series $\sum x^n = 1/(1-x)$ with $e_0 = 1 - R_0D$:

$$\frac1D = \frac{R_0}{1 - e_0} = R_0(1 + e_0 + e_0^2 + e_0^3 + \dots) = R_0(1+e_0)(1+e_0^2)(1+e_0^4)\cdots \tag{9.64–9.66}$$

The factorisation is the binary carry-free regrouping: each new factor doubles the number of correct bits, so it is the same quadratic convergence in product form. Folding $X$ in at the start gives the quotient directly:

$$q_0 = XR_0, \qquad q_{i+1} = q_i(1+e_i), \qquad e_{i+1} = e_i^2 \tag{9.71–9.75}$$

**The architectural win**: $E_{i+1}$ (a squarer) and $Q_{i+1}$ (a multiplier) are computed *in parallel* (Fig. 9.17b, p. 296), whereas Newton-Raphson chains squarer-then-multiplier. Shorter step, and multiplying $X$ by the short $R_0$ hides the $X$ multiplication entirely (p. 295).

In practice write $Q_{i+1} = Q_i + Q_iE_i$, since $1+E_i$ has a long run of known zeros and multiplying by them is waste — unless the target has a fixed-width multiplier such as a DSP block, in which case $Q_i(1+E_i)$ costs the same (p. 297).

Error behaviour (9.85, 9.91, pp. 298–299) splits cleanly:

- **$E$ datapath**: $\overline{\delta}'_{i+1} = 2^{\ell} + 2^{m_i+1}\overline{\delta}'_i$ with $m_i = 2^im_0$. The incoming error is scaled by $2^{m_i+1} \ll 1$, so it dies. Each squarer's input/output width *shrinks* by a doubling number of bits — the opposite of Newton-Raphson, where the datapath grows.
- **$Q$ datapath**: $\overline{\delta}_{i+1} = 2^{\ell+1} + 2\overline{\delta}'_i + (1 + 2^{m_i})\overline{\delta}_i$. The factor $(1+e_i) \approx 1$ means **errors accumulate**, which is why a single LSB $\ell$ is used for all steps.

Worked sizing from p. 298: $\ell = -32$, $m_0 = -7$, so $m_1 = -14$, $m_2 = -28$; first squarer takes `ufix(-7,-27)` (21 bits) to `ufix(-14,-33)` (20 bits), second takes 7 bits to 6 bits; $\overline{\delta}'_2 = 2^{-32} + 2^{-45}$.

## Goldschmidt Division

Multiplicative normalisation. Run two coupled recurrences with the invariant $n_i/d_i = X/D$ and drive $d_i \to 1$:

$$
p_0 \approx \frac1D,\quad d_0 = Dp_0,\quad n_0 = p_0X, \qquad
n_{j+1} = p_jn_j,\quad d_{j+1} = p_jd_j,\quad p_{j+1} = 2 - d_j \tag{9.96–9.101}
$$

$p_{j+1} = 2 - d_j$ is forced by demanding quadratic convergence on $e_j = 1 - d_j$: $e_{j+1} = 1 - p_jd_j = 1 - (2-d_j)d_j = (1-d_j)^2 = e_j^2$, the same completed square as (9.55). Since $n_i$ and $d_i$ scale together the invariant holds, and $d_i \to 1$ makes $n_i \to X/D$.

Multiplying by numbers close to 1 is hardware-wasteful, so in practice rewrite $p_j \times d_j$ as $d_j + d_je_j$ using $e_j = 1-d_j$ — a smaller multiplier that skips the known zeros. Do that to $p_j$ as well and **you have literally recovered the quadratic series of §9.4** (p. 300).

> [!important] Goldschmidt does not self-correct
> $p_{j+1} = 2 - d_j$ depends only on the *denominator* recurrence. A rounding error injected into $n_j$ is multiplied by $\prod p_k \approx 1$ forever and is never observed by anything, because nothing ever recomputes $n_j$ against $X$ or $D$. Newton-Raphson's $e_i = 1 - Dr_i$ re-derives the error from $D$ every step and squares it away; Goldschmidt's numerator error only accumulates. The book's own bound makes this concrete: the $Q$/numerator datapath carries $(1+2^{m_i})\overline{\delta}_i \approx \overline{\delta}_i$ forward, "contrary to the $E_i$ datapath where the error from step $i$ was scaled down" (9.91, p. 299).
>
> Consequence: Goldschmidt needs a full error analysis and guard bits sized for the *accumulated* error, and cannot be truncated as aggressively. Its compensation is structural: $n_{j+1}$ and $d_{j+1}$ depend on nothing of each other, so the two multiplications issue in the same cycle and pipeline cleanly.

## Other Multiplicative Methods

**Higher-order Householder.** Newton is the degree-1 Householder method (tangent approximation). Order $d$ uses derivatives up to the $d$-th and multiplies the number of correct bits by $d+1$ per iteration. Order 2 is Halley's method; applied to the reciprocal it must be adapted [IP17] to avoid degenerating to $x_{i+1} = 1/D$ [Fly70], giving

$$x_{i+1} = x_i(1 + e_i + e_i^2), \qquad e_i = 1 - Dx_i \tag{9.105}$$

— which is (9.65) truncated to three terms, with $e_i$ recomputed each step (hence self-correcting, like Newton). **Cubic convergence: one Halley iteration triples the precision of $R_0$ for one squarer and two multipliers**, filling the gap between one quadratic step (doubling, two multipliers) and two of them (quadrupling, one squarer and three multipliers). Shown relevant on FPGAs up to 32 bits: an 11-input-bit table plus one Halley step gives a 32-bit faithful reciprocal (p. 301).

**Ad hoc series (VFloat).** Split $D = D_H + D_L$ with $D_L < 2^k$, then

$$\frac{X}{D} = \frac{X}{D_H}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + D_L/D_H} \approx X(D_H - D_L)\cdot\frac{1}{D_H^2} \tag{9.106–9.108}$$

using the first two terms of $\frac{1}{1+x} \approx 1-x$. Tabulate $1/D_H^2$; the table read and the first multiplication run in parallel. Approximation error is $(D_L/D_H)^2$, so $2k < \ell$ for a result accurate to $2^\ell$, and the table needs about twice as many output bits as input bits. Overall cost exceeds one-step Newton-Raphson or quadratic series, but it suits small precisions where the multipliers fit in DSP blocks (p. 302). Jaiswal's larger-precision variant [Jai14] with more series terms turns out to be exactly (9.65) again, only with a full-precision $r_0$ tabulation — strictly more expensive (p. 302).

## Square Root

Restate integer square root as *find $S$ with $X = S^2 + R$, $0 \le R < 2S+1$* — the bound coming from $(S+1)^2 = S^2 + 2S + 1$. Set beside $X = QD + R$, $0 \le R < D$, the two problems are the same shape with the divisor replaced by a function of the running result, which is why every algorithm above has a square-root twin (p. 302):

- digit recurrence for $\sqrt{\cdot}$, generalised in [[4.4) Digit Recurrence for Algebraic Functions]];
- Newton-Raphson on $f(x) = 1/x^2 - X$, converging to $1/\sqrt X$: $\ s_{i+1} = \frac12 s_i(3 - s_i^2X)$ (9.111) — again division-free;
- a Goldschmidt variant, which can share most of the divider's hardware [PB02];
- generic function approximation, since $\sqrt{\cdot}$ is unary — useful at least to seed the multiplicative iteration.

## FPGA Mapping

- **Radix-4, $\gamma=2$ packs into one LUT row.** With the two's-complement digit encoding (9.32), each 6-input LUT takes one bit of $R_i$, one of $D$, one of $2D$ and the three bits of $q_{-i}$, merging multiplexer and add/sub, and the row rides the fast carry chain (p. 276). $\gamma = 3$ has a smaller selection table but needs $3D$, a fourth mux input, and loses the packing (p. 277).
- **Carry propagation is cheap up to ~32 bits.** Fast carry logic makes the full carry-propagate partial remainder fast enough that carry-save is not worth it below that size; above it, keep $R_i$ in high-radix carry-save (HRCS), whose overhead is not the extra registers (1/16 for chunk size 16) but the larger selection function (p. 288).
- **Reciprocal-based dividers want DSP blocks.** Degree-2 piecewise polynomial reciprocal is the best single-precision option on DSP-enabled FPGAs [Pas12]; the VFloat ad hoc series is attractive precisely when both multipliers fit in DSP blocks (pp. 291, 302). See [[1.3.2) DSP Blocks]].
- **Generators.** `flopoco FPDiv wE=8 wF=23 srt=42` builds the radix-4 $\gamma=2$ divider unrolled in space; `srt=43` the $\gamma=3$ one (pp. 276–277). FloPoCo does not support don't-cares in the selection table, so out-of-domain cells are all assigned $\pm2$. See [[7.1) FloPoCo]].

## Trade-offs

> Chapter 9 is architectural: it gives iteration counts, selection-function input widths and error budgets, but no synthesis results. The area column below is structural, not measured. Sizing a real divider needs outside data (or a FloPoCo run).

| Method | Latency (cycles) | Area | Multiplier | Self-correcting | Accuracy | p. |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Restoring | $w_Q$, cycle = one $w_D$-bit carry-propagate sub | 1 sub ($w_D{+}1$) + 1 mux ($w_D$) | none | n/a (exact) | exact $R$ ⇒ correctly roundable | 267, 269 |
| Non-restoring | $w_Q + 1$ (final correction) | 1 add/sub, no restore mux | none | n/a (exact) | exact $R$ ⇒ correctly roundable | 267 fn2 |

| Newton-Raphson | $\lceil \log_2(w_F/\vert\ell_0\vert)\rceil$; step = squarer **then** mult | datapath **doubles** per step; exact squarer | 1 squarer + 1 truncated mult / step | **yes** ($e_i$ recomputed from $D$) | faithful, $\vert\delta_Q\vert < 2^{-w_F-2}$ | 291–294 |
| Quadratic series | same count; step = squarer **‖** mult | $E$ datapath **shrinks**; common LSB $\ell$ on $Q$ | 1 squarer ‖ 1 mult / step | $E$ path yes, $Q$ path no | faithful, needs full error analysis | 295–299 |
| Goldschmidt | same count; two independent mults | two full-width mults, no shrinkage | 2 mults / step | **no** (numerator error never re-measured) | faithful, guard bits sized for accumulation | 300 |
| Halley (Householder-2) | $\lceil \log_3(\cdot)\rceil$ | table (11 in) + 1 squarer + 2 mults | 1 squarer + 2 mults / step | **yes** ($e_i$ recomputed) | 32-bit faithful reciprocal from 11-bit table | 301 |

**Pick restoring or non-restoring** when the divider is rare, area is the binding constraint, and $w_Q$ cycles of latency is acceptable — it is a subtractor and a mux.
**Pick SRT** when you need an exact remainder or correct rounding, or when you have no spare multiplier: radix-4 $\gamma=2$ on 6-LUT FPGAs, radix-2 with a carry-save remainder on ASIC, prescaling plus speculation when latency dominates.
**Pick a multiplicative method** only when the multipliers already exist (DSP blocks, a processor's FPU) and faithful rounding suffices: Newton-Raphson if truncation aggressiveness and robustness matter, quadratic series for the shorter critical path, Goldschmidt for a deeply pipelined throughput machine where you can afford the guard bits.

## Worked example: $181 \div 13$ on 8 bits

$X = 10110101_2 = 181$, $D = 1101_2 = 13$. Exact: $Q = 13 = 00001101_2$, $R = 12 = 1100_2$.

### Restoring

Recurrence (9.11)–(9.13) with $w_X = 8$: $R'_0 = 2^{-8}X = 0.10110101_2$, and each step tests $2R'_i - D$.

| $i$ | $2R'_i$ | $2R'_i - D$ | sign | $q_{7-i}$ | $R'_{i+1}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | $1.0110101$ | $-1011.1001011$ | $-$ | 0 | $1.0110101$ |
| 1 | $10.110101$ | $-1010.001011$ | $-$ | 0 | $10.110101$ |
| 2 | $101.10101$ | $-111.01011$ | $-$ | 0 | $101.10101$ |
| 3 | $1011.0101$ | $-1.1011$ | $-$ | 0 | $1011.0101$ |
| 4 | $10110.101$ | $1001.101$ | $+$ | **1** | $1001.101$ |
| 5 | $10011.01$ | $110.01$ | $+$ | **1** | $110.01$ |
| 6 | $1100.1$ | $-0.1$ | $-$ | 0 | $1100.1$ |
| 7 | $11001$ | $1100$ | $+$ | **1** | $1100$ |

$Q = 00001101_2 = 13$, $R = R'_8 = 1100_2 = 12$. Exact — error 0 ulp. Eight cycles, and on the four rejected steps the subtractor result was thrown away and the old $R'$ restored through the multiplexer.

### Non-restoring

Same alignment, digit set $\{-1,+1\}$, $q_{-i} = \operatorname{sign}(R_i)$, $R_{i+1} = 2R_i - q_{-i}D$. Note the operation for step $i$ is decided by the *sign bit only* of $R_i$, i.e. before step $i$'s carry chain would have finished.

| $i$ | $R_i$ | $q$ | op | $R_{i+1} = 2R_i \mp D$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | $+0.10110101$ | $+1$ | $-D$ | $-1011.1001011$ |
| 1 | $-1011.1001011$ | $-1$ | $+D$ | $-1010.001011$ |
| 2 | $-1010.001011$ | $-1$ | $+D$ | $-111.01011$ |
| 3 | $-111.01011$ | $-1$ | $+D$ | $-1.1011$ |
| 4 | $-1.1011$ | $-1$ | $+D$ | $+1001.101$ |
| 5 | $+1001.101$ | $+1$ | $-D$ | $+110.01$ |
| 6 | $+110.01$ | $+1$ | $-D$ | $-0.1$ |
| 7 | $-0.1$ | $-1$ | $+D$ | $+1100$ |

Digits $(+1,-1,-1,-1,-1,+1,+1,-1)$ at weights $2^7 \dots 2^0$:

$$Q = 128 - 64 - 32 - 16 - 8 + 4 + 2 - 1 = 13 \;\checkmark, \qquad R = R_8 = 12 \ge 0 \ \text{(no correction)}$$

Eight add/subs, zero restores — one operation per digit against restoring's worst case of two.

### Radix-2 SRT with digit set $\{-1,0,+1\}$

Now normalize: $\hat X = 0.10110101_2 = 0.70703125$, $\hat D = 0.1101_2 = 0.8125$, both in $[1/2,1)$, with $X/D = 16\hat X/\hat D$. Since $\gamma = 1 = \beta - 1$ the system is maximally redundant, $\rho = 1$, and $R_1 = \hat X$ is a legal start because $\hat X \le \rho\hat D$.

Selection function, from (9.30) with $\rho = 1$ and $\hat D \ge 1/2$: choose $q_{-i} = +1$ if $2R_i \ge \tfrac12$, $-1$ if $2R_i < -\tfrac12$, else $0$. Check the middle case against $-\rho + \frac{2R_i}{\hat D} \le q \le \rho + \frac{2R_i}{\hat D}$: $q=0$ needs $|2R_i| \le \hat D$, and $|2R_i| < \tfrac12 \le \hat D$ holds. **The rule reads three bits of $2R_i$ and no bits of $\hat D$ at all** — that is the overlap being spent.

| $i$ | $2R_i$ | $q_{-i}$ | $R_{i+1} = 2R_i - q_{-i}\hat D$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $1.0110101$ | $+1$ | $0.1001101$ |
| 2 | $1.001101$ | $+1$ | $0.011001$ |
| 3 | $0.110010$ | $+1$ | $-0.0000100$ |
| 4 | $-0.0001000$ | $0$ | $-0.0001000$ |
| 5 | $-0.001$ | $0$ | $-0.001$ |
| 6 | $-0.01$ | $0$ | $-0.01$ |
| 7 | $-0.1$ | $0$ | $-0.1$ |
| 8 | $-1$ | $-1$ | $-0.0011$ |

Digit string $q_{-1..-8} = (1,1,1,0,0,0,0,\bar1)$.

**On-the-fly conversion** by (9.41)–(9.43), maintaining $Q_i$ and $Q^{\ominus}_i = Q_i - 2^{-i}$ with concatenation only:

| $i$ | $q_{-i}$ | $Q_i$ | $Q^{\ominus}_i$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | $+1$ | $.1$ | $.0$ |
| 2 | $+1$ | $.11$ | $.10$ |
| 3 | $+1$ | $.111$ | $.110$ |
| 4 | $0$ | $.1110$ | $.1101$ |
| 5 | $0$ | $.11100$ | $.11011$ |
| 6 | $0$ | $.111000$ | $.110111$ |
| 7 | $0$ | $.1110000$ | $.1101111$ |
| 8 | $-1$ | $.11011111$ | $.11011110$ |

At step 8 the negative digit borrows from $Q^{\ominus}_7$ instead of propagating a carry through $.1110000$ — no adder anywhere.

$Q_8 = 0.11011111_2 = 0.87109375$ against $\hat X/\hat D = 0.870192\ldots$, so

$$\delta = 9.014\times10^{-4} = 0.231\ \text{ulp} \quad (u_Q = 2^{-8}),$$

comfortably inside the guaranteed $|\hat X/\hat D - Q_8| \le \rho\beta^{-8} = 2^{-8} = 1$ ulp of (9.35). And the bound is tight in the useful sense: $R_9/(\hat D\,2^{8}) = -0.1875/(0.8125\cdot256) = -9.014\times10^{-4}$ recovers $\delta$ **exactly**, because (9.34) is an identity — which is how one more bit of $Q$ or a correct rounding decision comes for free.
