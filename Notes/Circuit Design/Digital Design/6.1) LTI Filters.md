**Specification.** Input: one sample per cycle, `sfix(0, ℓ)` — the MSB is at position $0$ because we normalise the input peak to $\overline{u}=1$. Output: `sfix(m', ℓ')`, where $\ell'$ *is* the accuracy requirement and $m'$ comes from the filter's gain. Accuracy promised: **faithful**, $|\widetilde{y}_{\text{out}}(k) - y(k)| < 2^{\ell'}$, i.e. strictly less than one ulp of the output, for every input signal and every $k$. Optimised: adder count — multiple-constant-multiplication adders plus structural adders — under a frequency mask.

Everything below is an attempt to hold that one-ulp promise across a circuit that contains a *feedback loop*, which is what makes filters different from every other operator in this book: a rounding error injected at cycle $k$ is still circulating at cycle $k+1000$.

> [!important] The standard design flow is backwards
> The textbook flow is three separate optimisations: design an ideal filter meeting the frequency mask (**FD**), quantise its coefficients (**Q**), then implement (**I**). Quantising afterwards can break the frequency specification *or the stability* — interesting IIR filters have poles near the unit circle, so a few-ulp coefficient perturbation moves them outside it (p. 685). Worse, every rounding error inside the loop is amplified by a factor that the **worst-case peak gain** of $1/(1+\sum a_iz^{-i})$ quantifies, and that factor can be $10^6$ (p. 694). Coefficient and datapath widths are therefore *consequences* of the frequency specification, not free parameters to pick and then check. The whole chapter is the argument that FD+Q+I should be **one** optimisation problem.

## An introduction to discrete-time signals and filters

Brisk on purpose — this is notation, not content. For the continuous-domain background ($z$ is to difference equations what $s$ is to differential ones) see [[1) The Laplace Domain]] and [[3) Linear Systems]].

### Discrete-time signals, elementary operations, systems, LTI filters

A signal is a sequence $x=\{x(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}$, $x(k)\in\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. Three operations generate everything: element-wise addition $x+y$, scaling $cx$, and the shift $\mathcal{D}^n(x)=\{x(k-n)\}$ (pp. 670–671). Multi-dimensional supports (images, video) change nothing arithmetically, so we stay in 1-D.

A filter $\mathcal{H}$ maps a signal to a signal. Mathematically it eats the whole infinite sequence; a *realisation* eats one sample per cycle and carries **memory** — the only structural difference from the function evaluators of [[4.1) Fixed-Point Function Approximation]] (p. 671). $\mathcal{H}$ is **linear** iff $\mathcal{H}(x+cy)=\mathcal{H}(x)+c\,\mathcal{H}(y)$ and **time-invariant** iff $\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{D}^n(x))=\mathcal{D}^n(\mathcal{H}(x))$ — delaying the input is the same as delaying the output, which is exactly the property a *hardware* block must have (p. 672).

The impulse response is $h=\mathcal{H}(\delta)$. Decompose any $u$ into shifted impulses, $u=\sum_i u(i)\mathcal{D}^i(\delta)$, and push $\mathcal{H}$ through **by linearity then time-invariance**:

$$
y=\mathcal{H}(u)=\sum_{i}u(i)\,\mathcal{D}^i(\mathcal{H}(\delta))=\sum_i u(i)\mathcal{D}^i(h)
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y(k)=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}u(i)h(k-i)
$$

so an LTI filter *is* its impulse response, and the output is a convolution $y=u\circledast h$ (p. 673). Causality is $h(k)=0$ for $k<0$. **FIR** = finitely many nonzero $h(k)$; **IIR** = infinitely many.

Convolution is commutative, distributive over addition and associative (p. 674) — which is why a **cascade** may be reordered and a **parallel combination** merged. These identities are the ones an implementation silently breaks: rounded addition and multiplication are not linear, so the three "mathematically equivalent" cascades of Fig. 23.4 give different numbers in hardware, and a convergent infinite sum stops converging once infinitely many rounding errors are added to it. That is the root cause of **limit-cycle oscillations** (p. 675).

**BIBO stability** (Def. 23.2): every bounded input gives a bounded output. FIR filters are always stable; an IIR filter is BIBO-stable iff $\sum_k|h(k)|$ converges (p. 676) — keep that sum in view, it is about to become the central quantity of the note.

### Specifying LTI filters by constant-coefficient equations

A finite *difference equation*

$$y(k)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}b_i\,u(k-i)-\sum_{i=1}^{m}a_i\,y(k-i) \tag{23.13}$$

translates directly into a **filter structure** (signal-flow graph) built from exactly three abstract components — adder, multiplication by a constant, unit delay $\mathcal{D}$ — each of which is itself an elementary LTI filter (Fig. 23.6, p. 676). Replace them by adders, constant multipliers and clocked registers and you have a *parallel implementation*: one output sample per cycle, as many multipliers as the equation has multiplications (p. 677).

**Non-recursive** ($a_i=0$): $y(k)=\sum_{i=0}^n b_iu(k-i)$ with $b_i=h(i)$, order $n$, length $n+1$, always FIR. Direct form = register chain + a **sum of products by constants (SOPC)**. Transposing the signal-flow graph (reverse every wire, swap forks and adders) gives the transposed form: a **multiple constant multiplication** of the single value $u(k)$ by all the $b_i$, followed by a chain of delays and *structural* adders (p. 678). This is precisely the MCM problem of [[3.1.3) Multiple Constant Multiplication]] — the coefficients share subexpressions, so $n{+}1$ multiplications cost far fewer than $n{+}1$ multipliers.

**Recursive**: direct form I (Fig. 23.9a), transposed direct form I, and — by viewing direct form I as a cascade of two filters and *commuting them*, legal by commutativity of convolution — **direct form II** (Fig. 23.10), whose two delay lines now hold the same values and can be shared, halving the registers when $m=n$ (p. 679–680).

### Abstract filter structures versus finite-precision circuits

Every one of the four structures splits into a **non-recursive forward path** (the $b_i$) and a **recursive loop** (the $a_i$). The forward path yields to the ordinary error analysis of [[1.2) Error Accuracy]]. The loop does not: a rounding error injected once is fed back, combined with the next rounding error, and so on forever — an *infinite accumulation of rounding errors* (p. 681).

That accumulation reprices every structure:

- In **direct form I** the $b_i$ multiply $u(k)$, which is narrow. Cheap multipliers, unshared registers.
- In **direct form II** the $b_i$ multiply $t'(k)$, which lives in the loop and therefore needs extended precision. Registers are shared, multipliers are *bigger*. A stable implementation of DF-II may still win overall, but not for the reason the register count suggests.
- **Transposed direct form II** multiplies the $b_i$ by $u(k)$ *and* shares registers — apparently the best combination — at the cost of a critical path through two multipliers and two adders (p. 681).

Which structure wins is application-specific; the point of §23.3 is to make it *computable* — given the $a_i$ and $b_i$, derive the minimum internal width $\ell_{\text{ext}}$ that guarantees stability and accuracy, then compare structures at equal guaranteed accuracy.

### Filters defined by their coefficients, and filters defined in the frequency domain

Some filters arrive as coefficients already: pulse-shaping filters (half-sine, root-raised cosine) whose $b_i$ are closed-form in the filter order, and physics-based multimodal additive sound synthesis, where a bell is a weighted sum of decaying harmonics and each harmonic is a second-order IIR *biquad* whose $a_i,b_i$ follow from its frequency $f_i$ and 60 dB decay time $T_i^{60}$ (Fig. 23.12, p. 682).

Most arrive as a frequency mask instead: pass band, transition band, stop band (Fig. 23.13, p. 683). The bridge is the $z$-transform $X(z)=\sum_k x(k)z^{-k}$, which is linear and maps $\mathcal{D}^n$ to multiplication by $z^{-n}$ (hence the $\boxed{z^{-1}}$ notation for a delay) and convolution to multiplication. Evaluated on the unit circle $z=e^{j\omega}$ it is the discrete-time Fourier transform, $\omega=2\pi f/f_s$. Applying it to (23.13):

$$
Y(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}b_iz^{-i}U(z)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_iz^{-i}Y(z)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
H(z)=\frac{\sum_{i=0}^{m}b_iz^{-i}}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_iz^{-i}} \tag{23.19}
$$

**An IIR filter is BIBO-stable iff all poles of $H$ lie strictly inside the unit circle** (p. 684). The intuition: for $|z|\ge1$, $|H(z)|\le\sum|h(k)|\,|z^{-k}|\le\sum|h(k)|$, which is finite for a stable filter, so no such $z$ can be a pole. Since $H$ of a causal FIR filter is a polynomial in $z^{-1}$, every factorisation of that polynomial is a legal cascade. Many useful IIR filters put their poles *very close* to the circle — that is what a long memory means — and that is exactly why they are hypersensitive to coefficient rounding.

### Filter design: from a frequency specification to a time-domain architecture

The classical flow (Matlab `filterDesigner`, Fig. 23.15, p. 686) is three separate optimisation steps:

1. **FD** — find $n$, $m$, and real $a_i,b_i$ meeting the mask. IIR: bilinear transform of a Butterworth/Chebyshev prototype, or direct optimisation (least-$p$). FIR: $a_i=0$, so (23.19) is a polynomial and Remez/Parks–McClellan applies. Larger $n$ buys a tighter transition band at higher architectural cost, and **many coefficient sets satisfy a given mask** — a large design space (p. 685).
2. **Q** — round $a_i,b_i$ to fixed point. "Simply quantizing the coefficients obtained in the filter design step is likely to break the frequency response specification or even the stability" (p. 685).
3. **I** — emit hardware from the quantised coefficients. The result is no longer LTI, and not even linear.

Steps 2 and 3 are where this note lives.

## An arithmetic approach to the implementation of LTI filters

Two sets (Fig. 23.16, p. 687):

- $\mathcal{L}$ — BIBO-stable LTI filters, real coefficients, real signals.
- $\mathcal{C}$ — hardware digital filters: quantised coefficients **and** quantised internal signals.

Their intersection is nearly empty. An unrounded multiplier by a nontrivial constant has more output bits than input bits, so in a recursive loop the width would grow without bound; rounding is compulsory, and rounding is nonlinear. $\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{C}$ therefore contains only non-recursive filters evaluated *exactly*, with more output bits than input bits — not what applications ship (p. 687).

The consequence is sharper than "there is some error": a hardware digital filter has **no frequency response, no transfer function and no poles**, because all three notions presuppose linearity, so the question "does this hardware filter respect this frequency specification" is *fundamentally ill-posed* (p. 687). That is also why limit cycles are not a curiosity but the generic behaviour. The chapter's move is to replace the ill-posed question with a well-posed one, using the same faithfulness notion as the rest of the book ([[1.2) Error Accuracy]]):

**Definition 23.3.** A hardware digital filter $\mathcal{C}$ is **faithful** to an LTI filter $\mathcal{H}$ iff for any input signal, the difference between the output of $\mathcal{C}$ and the output of $\mathcal{H}$ is strictly less than one ulp of the output of $\mathcal{C}$ (p. 688).

Three consequences make this the right definition (p. 688–689):

1. If $\mathcal{H}$ is BIBO-stable, any faithful $\mathcal{C}$ is BIBO-stable. **Stability is inherited, not hoped for.**
2. Nonlinearities are not eliminated but *bounded*: a limit cycle around a converged value has amplitude below one ulp. It is last-bit noise, which hardware users already live with.
3. All faithful implementations are functionally equivalent, so choosing among them becomes a pure **optimisation problem** over cost — comparable by automatic tools.

## Hardware digital filters faithful to an LTI filter

### Worst-case peak gain

**Definition 23.4.** The peak value of a bounded signal is $\lVert x\rVert_\infty=\max_k|x(k)|$.

**Definition 23.5.** For a BIBO-stable LTI filter $\mathcal{H}$, the **worst-case peak gain** is

$$\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle\!\rangle=\max_{\lVert u\rVert_\infty=1}\lVert y\rVert_\infty \tag{23.20}$$

*Derivation.* From $y(k)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}h(i)u(k-i)$, maximise the sum over inputs of peak value 1 term by term — legal because each $u(k-i)$ appears in exactly one term, so the maximum of the sum is the sum of the maxima. Take

$$
u(k-i)=\begin{cases}+1 & h(i)>0\\ -1 & h(i)<0\end{cases}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y(k)=\sum_{i=0}^{k}|h(i)|
$$

This grows with $k$, so letting $k\to\infty$,

$$\boxed{\;\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle\!\rangle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|h(k)|\;} \tag{23.22}$$

which is exactly the sum whose convergence *defines* BIBO stability (p. 676). The WCPG is not an abstraction over stability — it is the stability criterion, given a number. And (23.21) is constructive: it builds the adversarial input, which FloPoCo's test-bench generator actually emits.

By linearity, for any bounded input,

$$\overline{y}=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle\!\rangle\,\overline{u} \tag{23.23}$$

This is a guarantee, not a typical case — the exciting signal has vanishing probability in practice, so the WCPG is pessimistic compared to probabilistic error models, but it is a *bound* (p. 689). FloPoCo computes safe (guaranteed-overestimating) WCPG bounds with an arbitrary-precision library; the notion generalises to MIMO filters (p. 690).

### Interface considerations

Normalise $\overline{u}=1$ without loss of generality (the filter is linear), so the input MSB is $0$ and the input format is `sfix(0, ℓ)`. The output LSB $\ell'$ *is* the accuracy specification. The output MSB follows from (23.23), plus one term for rounding errors that propagate to the MSB, bounded by $2^{\ell'}$ in a faithful filter:

$$m'\le\left\lceil\log_2\!\left(\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle\!\rangle+2^{\ell'}\right)\right\rceil \tag{23.24}$$

This can be wildly pessimistic. Some bell-model IIR filters have $\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle\!\rangle\approx2\times10^5$, giving $m'=18$; but the actual excitation is a sequence of distant impulses (a hammer), not an adversarial signal, so the application should set $m'$ from the peak of the impulse response instead (p. 691). Internally the architecture uses a *wider* format `sfix(m', ℓ_ext)` with the same MSB — overflows in the internal adds are harmless because fixed-point computation is modulo $2^{m'}$ and the final result is still correct (p. 691).

The resulting generator interface (Fig. 23.18, p. 691) takes only $(a_i)$, $(b_i)$, $\ell$, $\ell'$, optionally $m'$ — and *not* $\ell_{\text{ext}}$, which is derived below.

### Overall error analysis of a direct-form implementation

Let $y$ be the ideal output, $\widetilde{y}$ the internal (extended-precision) value, $\widetilde{y}_{\text{out}}$ the rounded output. Define

$$
\delta_{\text{t}}(k)=\widetilde{y}(k)-y(k),\qquad
\delta_{\text{final round}}(k)=\widetilde{y}_{\text{out}}(k)-\widetilde{y}(k)
$$

$$\delta_{\text{total}}=\widetilde{y}_{\text{out}}-y=\underbrace{(\widetilde{y}_{\text{out}}-\widetilde{y})}_{\delta_{\text{final round}}}+\underbrace{(\widetilde{y}-y)}_{\delta_{\text{t}}} \tag{23.29}$$

and let $\delta_{\text{r}}$ be the error of the SOPC block alone:

$$\delta_{\text{r}}(k)=\widetilde{y}(k)-\left(\sum_{i=0}^{m}b_iu(k-i)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\widetilde{y}(k-i)\right) \tag{23.30}$$

Read that carefully: $\delta_{\text{r}}$ compares the hardware SOPC against an *ideal* SOPC that uses infinitely accurate $a_i,b_i$ but is fed the *same finite-precision* $u(k-i)$ and $\widetilde{y}(k-i)$. So $\delta_{\text{r}}$ absorbs both the coefficient quantisation error and the datapath rounding, and nothing else. It can be made arbitrarily small by shrinking $\ell_{\text{ext}}$, and can even be **exactly zero** when the coefficients are finitely representable and the internal format is wide enough (p. 692).

> [!important] The filter body is one bit heap
> $\sum b_iu(k-i)-\sum a_i\widetilde{y}(k-i)$ is a sum of products by constants. Every partial product is a shifted copy of an input word, so a direct-form FIR is *literally a single bit heap* — one column-wise accumulation of weighted bits, compressed once and carry-propagated once, exactly as in [[2.3) Sums of Weighted Bits]]. There is no reason to build $n{+}1$ multipliers, round each, and then add them: build one heap, and spend the single rounding at the bottom. That is what makes $\delta_{\text{r}}$ small at almost no cost, and it is why $\ell_{\text{ext}}$ is cheap to buy. The constants themselves are shared via [[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]] (MCM, or a constant matrix–vector product when several outputs are wanted).

### Error amplification in the feedback loop

Substitute $\widetilde{y}(k-i)=y(k-i)+\delta_{\text{t}}(k-i)$ (from the definition of $\delta_{\text{t}}$) into (23.30):

$$\delta_{\text{r}}(k)=\widetilde{y}(k)-\sum_{i=0}^{m}b_iu(k-i)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_iy(k-i)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\delta_{\text{t}}(k-i)$$

The first three terms collapse **by the defining difference equation (23.15)**, $y(k)=\sum b_iu(k-i)-\sum a_iy(k-i)$:

$$
\delta_{\text{r}}(k)=\widetilde{y}(k)-y(k)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\delta_{\text{t}}(k-i)
=\delta_{\text{t}}(k)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\delta_{\text{t}}(k-i) \tag{23.31}
$$

Rearranged,

$$\delta_{\text{t}}(k)=\delta_{\text{r}}(k)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_i\delta_{\text{t}}(k-i) \tag{23.32}$$

which is *itself a difference equation of the form (23.13)*: an IIR filter with input $\delta_{\text{r}}$, output $\delta_{\text{t}}$, and transfer function

$$\mathcal{H}_\delta(z)=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_iz^{-i}} \tag{23.33}$$

**The error filter is the original filter with its numerator deleted.** Its poles are the poles of $\mathcal{H}$ — near the unit circle for the interesting filters — so it can have enormous gain. Applying the WCPG theorem (23.23) to it:

$$\boxed{\;\overline{\delta_{\text{t}}}=\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle\,\overline{\delta_{\text{r}}}\;} \tag{23.34}$$

One rounding error per sample, amplified by a single computable number. For an FIR filter all $a_i=0$, so $\mathcal{H}_\delta(z)=1$ and $\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle=1$: **no amplification at all**, which is the entire quantitative difference between FIR and IIR arithmetic.

### Putting it all together

Substituting (23.34) into (23.29):

$$\overline{\delta_{\text{total}}}=\overline{\delta_{\text{final round}}}+\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle\,\overline{\delta_{\text{r}}} \tag{23.35}$$

Faithfulness demands $\overline{\delta_{\text{total}}}<2^{\ell'}$. A round-to-nearest final rounding costs $\overline{\delta_{\text{final round}}}=2^{\ell'-1}$, leaving exactly half the budget for everything upstream:

$$\overline{\delta_{\text{r}}}<\frac{2^{\ell'-1}}{\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle} \tag{23.36}$$

A faithful SOPC guarantees $\overline{\delta_{\text{r}}}<2^{\ell_{\text{ext}}}$, so take logarithms:

$$\boxed{\;\ell_{\text{ext}}=\ell'-1-\left\lceil\log_2\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle\right\rceil\;} \tag{23.37}$$

In words: **the internal datapath carries $1+\lceil\log_2\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle\rceil$ guard bits below the output LSB.** One for the final rounding, the rest paid to the feedback loop. FloPoCo uses a guaranteed *over*estimate of $\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle$ so that rounding errors in computing the WCPG cannot themselves break the guarantee — at the price of at most one extra bit (p. 694).

**Hands on (p. 694).** The lowest-frequency resonator of the bell model has $a_1^1=-1.99510896$, $a_2^1=0.999985754$ — poles blatantly close to the unit circle.

```sh
flopoco FixIIR lsbin=-8 lsbout=-8 \
  coeffb="1:0:-1" coeffa="-1.99510896:0.999985754"
```

FloPoCo reports $\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle\!\rangle=178\,750$ and $\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle=1.2806\times10^{6}$. So (23.37) demands $1+\lceil\log_2 1.2806\times10^6\rceil=1+21=22$ guard bits — for a **second-order** filter. That is the number the standard flow never computes and the reason "quantise to 16 bits and simulate" is not a design method.

## Hardware digital filters faithful to a frequency specification

### Formal definition

**Definition 23.6.** A hardware filter $\mathcal{C}$ is faithful to a frequency specification iff **there exists** a BIBO-stable LTI filter $\mathcal{H}$ such that $\mathcal{H}$ fulfils the specification and $\mathcal{C}$ is faithful to $\mathcal{H}$ in the sense of Def. 23.3 (p. 695).

The existential quantifier is the whole trick. It sidesteps the ill-posedness of §23.2 — we never claim $\mathcal{C}$ has a frequency response — while inheriting stability and the one-ulp bound on limit cycles. And it *shrinks the interface*: filter order, coefficient values, coefficient quantisation, internal formats are all implementation technicalities the tool should choose, leaving the user with a frequency mask, an input format, an output format, a target technology and a target frequency (Fig. 23.20, p. 696).

### Filter design as an optimisation problem

- **Parameter space**: the orders $n,m$ and the coefficients $a_i,b_i$ — quantised or not.
- **Performance parameters**: (i) the constant-multiplication method, i.e. which of the two families of [[3.1) Multiplication by Constants]] is used, since sharing intermediate results makes the *cost depend on the coefficient values in a nontrivial way*; (ii) the filter structure — direct forms, lattice wave filters, cascades — each an algebraic rewriting of (23.19), and each with its own quantisation behaviour. Filter structure may also be part of the *functional* specification, since some structures guarantee linear phase.
- **Constraints**: frequency-domain constraints from the mask, embedded directly into the optimisation as done for FIR filters by ILP; plus time-domain last-bit-accuracy constraints from Def. 23.6, handled by §23.3. The optimisation result can be re-checked with safe arbitrary-precision arithmetic to guard against rounding errors in the FD+Q *software* itself.
- **Cost function**: adder count at the high level; finer models count unequal adder sizes with gate-level area/delay/power estimates (p. 696–697).

This is [[7.2) ILP for Arithmetic Design]] applied end to end: the same integer program that picks an adder graph now also picks the coefficients that graph multiplies by.

### The state of the art

| Steps solved jointly | Status | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| FD + Q | studied since the 1960s, "solved" for practical fixed-point FIR instances | KO68, Kod12, BFH18 (p. 697) |
| Q + I | §23.3; FloPoCo does a bit-level cost model, but only table-based constant multipliers and direct form I | Vol+19 (p. 697) |
| I alone | rich: word-size reduction in structural adders, FIR factored into cascades, internal word-size optimisation, MCM by shift-and-add or precomputed tables | Che+17, MW15, CCL04, Ch. 12 (p. 697) |
| FD + Q + I, FIR | "almost there" — custom branch and bound, or ILP | AFM15, KVF22 (p. 697) |
| FD + Q + I, IIR | formalised but limited to **second-order** filters, no bit-level cost for the shift-and-add trees | Gar+22 (p. 697) |
| FD + Q + I, everything | **open problem** | p. 702 |

The reason joint optimisation matters and not just tidiness: the result of the I-step optimisations "depends strongly (and in a non-monotonous way) on the coefficient values", which the earlier FD and Q steps have already frozen (p. 697).

### Frequency constraints for linear-phase FIR filters in ILP

A FIR implementation *is* an MCM — except that here the constants are the unknowns. Linear-phase FIR is the tractable case for three reasons: it is what applications want; its coefficients are symmetric, so only about half the multiplications exist; and its zero-phase frequency response is **real-valued**, which is what lets it enter a linear program at all.

$$H_R(\omega)=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}h_ic_i(\omega),\qquad\omega\in[0,\pi] \tag{23.38}$$

with $c_i$ trigonometric and $m$ given by the symmetry type:

| Type | Symmetry | $n$ | $m$ | $c_i(\omega)$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| I | symmetric | even | $\tfrac{n}{2}+1$ | $1$ if $m=0$, else $2\cos(\omega m)$ |
| II | symmetric | odd | $\tfrac{n+1}{2}$ | $2\cos(\omega(m+\tfrac12))$ |
| III | antisymmetric | even | $\tfrac{n}{2}$ | $2\sin(\omega(m-1))$ |
| IV | antisymmetric | odd | $\tfrac{n+1}{2}$ | $2\sin(\omega(m+\tfrac12))$ |

*Table 23.1, p. 699.*

Since $|H(e^{j\omega})|=|H_R(\omega)|$, the mask is the pair of bounds

$$\underline{D}(\omega)\le H_R(\omega)\le\overline{D}(\omega)\qquad\forall\omega\in[0,\pi] \tag{23.40}$$

Two steps make this an ILP. First, $\omega$ is continuous, so evaluate on a discrete $\Omega_d\subset[0,\pi]$; a uniform discretisation of $16m$ points is typically dense enough. Second, replace the real $h_m$ by scaled integers

$$h_m=2^{\ell}C_m \tag{23.41}$$

with $\ell$ the coefficient LSB — fixed in advance, explored outside the ILP — and $C_m$ an **integer variable**. Each constraint in (23.40) is then linear in the $C_m$, and drops straight into the MCM ILP formulation of [[7.2) ILP for Arithmetic Design]]. A variable gain and a count of structural adders (zero coefficients cost nothing) can be added to the objective.

**The example (p. 699–700).** Low-pass, $\omega_p=0.3\pi$, passband ripple $\delta_p=0.0157$ (so $\overline{D},\underline{D}=1\pm0.0157$), stopband from $\omega_s=0.5\pi$ attenuated by at least $-43.6$ dB, i.e. a factor $0.0066$.

| | naive (Remez, then quantise) | ILP (FD+Q+I jointly) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| coefficients | 25 | 24 |
| coefficient width | 12 bits | 10 bits |
| nontrivial multiplications | 11 (of 13; two trivial: $1$ and $4=2^2$) | — |
| MCM adders | 13 | 4 |
| structural adders | 24 | 19 (four saved by zero coefficients) |
| **total adders** | **37** | **23** |

$$h_{\text{naive}}=2^{-12}\cdot(5,30,1,-53,-49,61,141,4,-261,-236,364,1229,1643,1229,364,-236,-261,4,141,61,-49,-53,1,30,5)$$
$$h_{\text{opt}}=2^{-10}\cdot(3,2,-6,-12,0,20,16,-24,-52,0,136,256,136,0,-52,-24,16,20,0,-12,-6,2,3)$$

Both meet the mask (Fig. 23.21). **38 % fewer adders, from asking one question instead of three.**

The honest caveat (p. 702): the resulting filter is *exact* — no rounding on the datapaths — so 8-bit input gives 18-bit output, and it lives in the small set $\mathcal{L}\cap\mathcal{C}$. It does not address the output-format side of the interface. Rounding it faithfully to 8 bits afterwards is known to be implementable, but then the circuit can no longer claim optimality: to be optimal, truncations must be *inside* the MCM problem, which means counting full adders rather than adders. That single global FD+Q+I formulation "remains to be written".

## Trade-offs

Orders: $n$ feed-forward taps, $m$ feedback taps. The chapter argues qualitatively (p. 677–681) and gives no LUT/slice numbers; the one hard cost comparison it does give is the 37-vs-23 adder table above.

| Structure | Multipliers | Registers | Critical path | Multiplier width | Stability under quantisation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| FIR direct form (Fig. 23.7, p. 677) | $n{+}1$, all $\times u(k)$ | $n$, at input width | 1 mult + adder tree | narrow (input) | unconditional — no poles |
| FIR transposed (Fig. 23.8, p. 678) | one MCM of $u(k)$ | $n$, at product width | 1 mult + 1 adder | narrow (input) | unconditional |
| IIR direct form I (Fig. 23.9a, p. 679) | $n{+}m{+}1$; SOPC | $n{+}m$, unshared | 1 mult + adder chain | $b_i$ narrow, $a_i$ wide | poles move under Q; needs (23.37) |
| IIR direct form II (Fig. 23.10/23.11a, p. 680) | $n{+}m{+}1$; two SOPCs | $\max(n,m)$, **shared** | 1 mult + adder chain | **all wide** ($b_i\times t'(k)$) | as DF-I, but wider datapath |
| IIR transposed DF-II (Fig. 23.11b, p. 681) | two MCMs | $\max(n,m)$, **shared** | **2 mults + 2 adders** | $b_i$ narrow | best area at equal accuracy |
| Cascade of biquads (Fig. 23.12, p. 682) | $5$ per section | $2$ per section | 1 mult + adder per section | narrow per section | best-conditioned; each section's poles independent |

Pick **FIR** when you need guaranteed stability, strict linear phase, or you cannot afford the guard bits — the price is a much longer impulse response for the same transition band (p. 685). Pick **IIR** when a long memory must be compact and you are willing to pay $1+\lceil\log_2\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle\rceil$ guard bits for it. Within IIR, pick **transposed direct form II** unless the two-multiplier critical path fails timing, in which case register the $t'(k)$ wire of the plain direct form II and give up register sharing (p. 681).

## Worked example — a 4-tap FIR, from error budget to coefficient width

Target the ideal filter $b=(0.1,\,0.3,\,0.4,\,0.2)$ (DC gain 1). Input `sfix(0,-7)`: 8-bit, $\overline{u}=1$, $\text{ulp}_{\text{in}}=2^{-7}$. Required output: `sfix(m',-7)`, faithful, so $\overline{\delta_{\text{total}}}<2^{-7}$.

**1 — WCPG and output MSB.** All coefficients positive, so by (23.22)

$$
\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}\rangle\!\rangle=\sum|b_i|=1.0
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
m'\le\lceil\log_2(1+2^{-7})\rceil=1
$$

Output format `sfix(1,-7)`: 9 bits. Also $a_i=0\Rightarrow\mathcal{H}_\delta(z)=1\Rightarrow\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle=1$, so (23.37) gives $\ell_{\text{ext}}=\ell'-1=-8$ *if the SOPC is built faithfully*. Below we build it crudely (independent truncated products) to see the budget explicitly.

**2 — Split the budget.** Round-to-nearest at the output eats $2^{\ell'-1}=2^{-8}$, so by (23.36) $\overline{\delta_{\text{r}}}<2^{-8}$. Halve it again ([[1.2) Error Accuracy]]): $2^{-9}$ for coefficient quantisation, $2^{-9}$ for datapath truncation.

**3 — Coefficient width.** Quantising to LSB $\ell_c$ with round-to-nearest gives $|\varepsilon_i|\le2^{\ell_c-1}$ per tap; the perturbed filter differs from the ideal one by an FIR filter with impulse response $\varepsilon$, whose WCPG is $\sum|\varepsilon_i|$, so the output is displaced by at most

$$
\sum_{i=0}^{3}|\varepsilon_i|\cdot\overline{u}\;\le\;4\cdot2^{\ell_c-1}=2^{\ell_c+1}\;<\;2^{-9}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ell_c\le-11
$$

**11 fractional bits — a 12-bit signed coefficient.** Quantising at $2^{-11}$:

| $b_i$ | $\widehat{b}_i\cdot2^{11}$ | $\widehat{b}_i$ in binary | $\varepsilon_i$ (in $2^{-11}$) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0.1$ | $205$ | $0.00011001101$ | $+0.2$ |
| $0.3$ | $614$ | $0.01001100110$ | $-0.4$ |
| $0.4$ | $819$ | $0.01100110011$ | $-0.2$ |
| $0.2$ | $410$ | $0.00110011010$ | $+0.4$ |

$\sum|\varepsilon_i|=1.2\cdot2^{-11}=2.4\cdot2^{-12}$, comfortably under the $2^{-9}=8\cdot2^{-12}$ allowance. Note $205+614+819+410=2048$ exactly: DC gain is still precisely 1, so $\langle\!\langle\widehat{\mathcal{H}}\rangle\!\rangle=1$ survives quantisation.

**4 — Datapath width.** Four products, each truncated to $\ell_{\text{ext}}$ with error $<2^{\ell_{\text{ext}}}$:

$$4\cdot2^{\ell_{\text{ext}}}=2^{\ell_{\text{ext}}+2}<2^{-9}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ell_{\text{ext}}\le-12$$

Internal format `sfix(1,-12)` — **5 guard bits** below the output LSB, because we rounded four times instead of once. Build the whole thing as a single bit heap and three of those five disappear.

**5 — One sample.** Take $u(k\!-\!3\ldots k)$, in `sfix(0,-7)`:

$$u(k)=0.1001101,\quad u(k{-}1)=-0.0101101,\quad u(k{-}2)=0.1100101,\quad u(k{-}3)=0.0010011$$

i.e. $(77,-45,101,19)\cdot2^{-7}$. Exact products, as integers scaled by $2^{-18}$:

$$205\cdot77=15785,\quad 614\cdot(-45)=-27630,\quad 819\cdot101=82719,\quad 410\cdot19=7790$$

Truncate each to $2^{-12}$ (drop 6 LSBs, round toward $-\infty$):

| product | exact $/2^{-12}$ | truncated | error $/2^{-12}$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $b_0u(k)$ | $246.640625$ | $246=\texttt{11110110}$ | $-0.640625$ |
| $b_1u(k{-}1)$ | $-431.71875$ | $-432$ | $-0.28125$ |
| $b_2u(k{-}2)$ | $1292.484375$ | $1292=\texttt{10100001100}$ | $-0.484375$ |
| $b_3u(k{-}3)$ | $121.71875$ | $121=\texttt{1111001}$ | $-0.71875$ |

Sum: $246-432+1292+121=1227$, so $\widetilde{y}=1227\cdot2^{-12}=0.299560546875$. Total truncation error $-2.125\cdot2^{-12}$, under the bound $4\cdot2^{-12}$ ✓; coefficient error contributes $+0.325\cdot2^{-12}$, under $2.4\cdot2^{-12}$ ✓.

The ideal output is $y=(7.7-13.5+40.4+3.8)/128=0.3$ exactly, so $\delta_{\text{r}}=-2^{-11}$ — and since $\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle=1$, that is $\delta_{\text{t}}$ too, with no amplification.

**6 — Final rounding.** $\widetilde{y}\cdot2^{7}=38.34375$, round to nearest: $38$, so $\widetilde{y}_{\text{out}}=38\cdot2^{-7}=0.296875$.

$$\delta_{\text{total}}=0.296875-0.3=-0.003125=\boxed{-0.4\ \text{ulp}}$$

Faithful ✓. Had this been an IIR filter with $\langle\!\langle\mathcal{H}_\delta\rangle\!\rangle=1.28\times10^6$, the same $\delta_{\text{r}}=-2^{-11}$ would have produced an output error of up to $625$ — about $2^{16}$ ulps — and 22 more guard bits would have been the price of the same one-ulp promise.
