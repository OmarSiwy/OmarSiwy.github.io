A counter is the smallest interesting sequential circuit: an autonomous Moore machine whose next-state function is $Q^{+}=Q+1$ and whose output function is the identity (see [[Synchronous Sequential Logic]] and [[Moore]]). There is no input to decode and no output logic to minimise, so everything hard about a counter is timing, not arithmetic.

**Specification.** *Inputs:* clock, control bits `EN`, `LD`, `UP`, and an $n$-bit load value $D$. *Output:* an $n$-bit state $Q\in[0,2^n)$ advancing by one per enabled edge. *Accuracy:* exact — integer arithmetic modulo $2^n$, error $0$ ulp, so none of the rounding machinery of [[Error Accuracy]] applies; the wrap at $2^n$ is specified behaviour, not error. *Optimised for:* maximum $f_{\max}$ at minimum flop count, subject to two side constraints that end up dominating the choice of encoding — whether the count is **decoded** combinationally, and whether it **crosses a clock domain**.

> [!important] The whole note in one line
> The arithmetic is settled after the first section: an increment is a chain of half adders. Every remaining decision — ripple vs. synchronous, binary vs. Gray vs. LFSR — is answered by *is this count decoded?* and *does it cross a clock domain?* Nothing else moves the needle.

The enable enters as the **carry-in** of the incrementer, not as a mux select — that is the first free lunch, and the derivation below shows why.

## The increment is an adder with one operand tied to 1

Start from the full adder of [[Adders]]:

$$s_i=a_i\oplus b_i\oplus c_i,\qquad c_{i+1}=a_ib_i+c_i(a_i\oplus b_i)$$

An increment is $A+B$ with $B=1$, i.e. $b_0=1$ and $b_i=0$ for $i>0$. Rather than special-casing bit $0$, absorb the constant into the carry-in: $A+1=A+0+c_0$ with $c_0=1$. Now **every** $b_i=0$, and by the identities $x\oplus0=x$ and $x\cdot0=0$ the full adder collapses:

$$s_i=a_i\oplus c_i,\qquad c_{i+1}=a_ic_i$$

which is a **half adder**. The $n$-bit incrementer is therefore a chain of $n$ half adders — one XOR2 and one AND2 per bit — instead of $n$ full adders. That is roughly half the gates of a general adder, and it is the whole reason a counter is cheaper than an accumulator.

**Unroll the carry.** The recurrence $c_{i+1}=a_ic_i$ with $c_0=\text{EN}$ has closed form, by induction:

$$c_i=\text{EN}\cdot\prod_{j<i}q_j$$

so with $a_i=q_i$ the state bits,

$$
\boxed{\;q_i^{+}=q_i\oplus\Big(\text{EN}\cdot\!\!\prod_{j<i}q_j\Big)\;}\qquad
\text{TC}=c_n=\text{EN}\cdot\!\!\prod_{j<n}q_j
$$

In words: **bit $i$ toggles when the enable is high and every lower bit is 1.** The terminal count TC is the carry-out, already present in the chain — free.

**Why lookahead degenerates too.** In generate/propagate form ([[CLA, Carry-Lookahead Adder]]) $g_i=a_ib_i$ and $p_i=a_i\oplus b_i$. With $b_i=0$ these become $g_i=0$, $p_i=a_i$. The prefix operator

$$(g,p)\circ(g',p')=(g+pg',\;pp')$$

with all $g=0$ reduces to $(0,pp')$ — the carry prefix problem is a **pure AND-prefix**, with no generate term to propagate. So a lookahead incrementer is exactly a [[Kogge-Stone Tree]] or [[Brent-Kung Tree]] with $\circ$ replaced by AND: depth $\lceil\log_2 n\rceil$, and half the wires of the adder version because the $g$ rail vanishes. Do not re-derive the tree; it is the one from [[Parallel Prefix Adders]] with a simpler operator.

**Down-counting.** Use the two's-complement identity $\overline{X}=2^n-1-X$ from [[Number Formats]]:

$$\overline{\overline{Q}+1}=2^n-1-\big(2^n-1-Q+1\big)=Q-1 \pmod{2^n}$$

A down-counter is an up-counter run on the complemented state, so complementing $q_j$ inside the product gives $\prod_{j<i}\overline{q_j}$: **bit $i$ toggles when every lower bit is 0.** Both directions in one datapath, with $\text{UP}=0$ meaning down:

$$t_i=\text{EN}\cdot\!\!\prod_{j<i}\big(q_j\oplus\overline{\text{UP}}\big),\qquad q_i^{+}=q_i\oplus t_i$$

The XOR is a single extra literal per AND input — on a LUT-based fabric it is absorbed for free, on an ASIC it costs one gate per bit.

**Loading.** $q_i^{+}=\text{LD}\,?\,d_i:(q_i\oplus t_i)$ — one 2:1 mux per bit, on the critical path unless the flop has a native load port.

## Synchronous counters

All flops share one clock, so the entire word changes on the same edge and the state is valid after $t_{cq}+t_{\text{skew}}$, independent of $n$. The cost is that the increment logic sits inside the clock period. From [[Introduction to Flip-Flops & Latches]] the constraint is

$$T_{\text{clk}}\;\ge\;t_{cq}+t_{\text{logic}}+t_{su}+t_{\text{skew}}$$

with, for the ripple-carry increment, $t_{\text{logic}}=(n-1)\,t_{\text{AND}}+t_{\text{XOR}}$, and for the prefix increment $t_{\text{logic}}=\lceil\log_2 n\rceil\,t_{\text{AND}}+t_{\text{XOR}}$.

**Enable chaining.** Two ways to build a wide counter from $k$-bit slices, and they are not equivalent:

- *Ripple-carry enable* — TC of slice $j$ drives EN of slice $j+1$. The enable path is combinational through every slice, so $t_{\text{logic}}$ grows linearly in the number of slices. This is the `ENT` input of the classic 74x161/163 parts.
- *Parallel (synchronous) enable* — every slice sees the same EN, and each slice ANDs its own all-ones condition locally. Constant depth, at the price of a wide AND per slice and higher fan-in on the state bits. This is the `ENP`/`ENT` split: `ENP` gates counting only, `ENT` additionally gates TC so that the chain can ripple.

**Two-stage decomposition** is the standard escape when $n$ is large and $f$ is high. Split into a fast low half of $k$ bits and a slow high half of $n-k$ bits; the high half increments only when the low half reaches its terminal count, so its long carry chain has $2^{k}$ clock periods to settle rather than one. The full count is still $\{Q_{\text{hi}},Q_{\text{lo}}\}$, still exact, and $f_{\max}$ is set by the $k$-bit slice alone. Pick $k$ so the low slice's chain just meets timing.

> [!note] Synchronous does not mean glitch-free
> All flops switch on the same edge, but not at the *same instant* — intra-register clock skew and per-flop $t_{cq}$ spread mean a decoder watching $Q$ still sees transient codes, for a window of $t_{\text{skew}}+\Delta t_{cq}$ rather than the ripple counter's $n\,t_{cq}$. If the decoded output is a strobe, an enable, or anything a downstream flop's asynchronous port sees, **register the decode**: compute "will equal $N$ next cycle" from $Q^{+}$ and clock it, so the strobe leaves a flop rather than a cone of logic.

## Asynchronous (ripple) counters

Drop the common clock. Stage 0 is a T flip-flop with $T=1$ clocked by `clk`; stage $i>0$ is clocked by $Q_{i-1}$. Each stage is an independent divide-by-two, and a falling-edge-triggered chain counts up (stage $i$ toggles exactly when $Q_{i-1}$ falls $1\to0$, which is the carry condition $\prod_{j<i}q_j$ realised in the time domain instead of in gates). No increment logic exists at all.

The price is that the toggle of stage $i$ is *caused* by the toggle of stage $i-1$, so delays accumulate:

$$Q_i \text{ settles at } (i+1)\,t_{cq} \text{ after the clock edge}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{the word } Q \text{ is invalid for } n\cdot t_{cq}$$

For $n=16$ and $t_{cq}=120\,\text{ps}$ that is $1.9\,\text{ns}$ of garbage after every edge — worse than a full clock period at $500\,\text{MHz}$. Note this is a bound on *sampling* the count, not on the counter's own speed: each stage's self-loop only needs $T\ge t_{cq}+t_{su}$ of **one** flop, so a ripple counter's raw toggle rate is the highest of any structure here.

During the settling window the counter walks through states it was never supposed to occupy. Take $011\to100$ on a 3-bit chain:

$$011 \xrightarrow{\;t_{cq}\;} 010 \xrightarrow{\;2t_{cq}\;} 000 \xrightarrow{\;3t_{cq}\;} 100$$

A decoder for $000$ fires a spurious pulse one $t_{cq}$ wide, in the middle of the count-3-to-4 transition. This is not a marginal-timing hazard that goes away at low frequency — it is present at every edge, at every clock rate, and its width is set by silicon, not by $T_{\text{clk}}$.

Ripple counters remain the right answer in exactly two places, both of which avoid decoding the intermediate bits:

- **Low-power clock dividers and prescalers.** Only stage 0 sees the full-rate clock; stage $i$ toggles at $f/2^{i}$, so the geometric series gives total switching activity $\sum_i f/2^i<2f$ regardless of $n$, and — crucially — there is no clock tree distributing $f$ to $n$ flops. A synchronous divider burns the clock-tree power of $n$ flops at $f$; the ripple chain burns one.
- **Non-decoded dividers.** If the only consumer is the final $Q_{n-1}$ (a $\div 2^n$ output), the skew is irrelevant: that one signal is a clean square wave, merely delayed by $n\,t_{cq}$.

The moment anything decodes the full word, or the count crosses into logic clocked by the original `clk`, the structure is wrong. Also note that each $Q_i$ is a *clock*, so it needs clock-quality routing and shows up in static timing analysis as a generated clock domain per stage — see [[Asynchronous Sequential Logic]] for the general hazard framing.

## Modulo-$N$ counters

For $N\ne2^n$ the natural wrap no longer lands where you want it. Three constructions, in increasing order of laziness:

1. **Compare and clear.** Assert a synchronous clear when $Q=N-1$. Costs an equality comparator ([[Comparators]]) — an AND of $n$ literals with the ones matching $N-1$ complemented — and puts it on the critical path in series with the increment.
2. **Preload and overflow.** Load $2^n-N$ on TC and count up. The terminal condition is the incrementer's own carry-out $c_n$, already computed, so the comparator disappears entirely. Costs the load mux you probably already have.
3. **Count down to zero.** Initialise to $N-1$, decrement, detect $Q=0$ with an $n$-input NOR. The zero-detect is *independent of $N$* — no literal-polarity map to regenerate when $N$ changes — and for a down-counter it is the borrow-out, again free.

Option 3 is the default for timers and timeouts: constant hardware across every $N$, terminal condition already in the carry chain, and $N$ becomes a plain register value rather than a synthesis-time constant. Option 1 only wins when you also need $Q$ to run $0\ldots N-1$ upward for addressing.

> [!warning] Unused states must be recoverable
> A mod-$N$ counter with $N<2^n$ has $2^n-N$ unreachable states. Power-up, a single-event upset, or a partial reset can land in one, and nothing in the next-state equations guarantees it walks back into the legal cycle — it may sit in a disjoint parasitic cycle forever. Either prove self-starting behaviour by checking that every unused state's successor chain reaches the main cycle (the state-graph argument of [[Synchronous Sequential Logic]]), or spend the reset.

## Gray counters

The Gray encoding of $b$ is $g=b\oplus(b\gg1)$, i.e. $g_i=b_i\oplus b_{i+1}$ with $b_n=0$; the inverse is a suffix-XOR, $b_i=\bigoplus_{j\ge i}g_j$.

**One bit changes per increment — proof.** Write $b=\ldots x\,0\,1^{k}$ (a run of $k$ ones at the bottom), so $b+1=\ldots x\,1\,0^{k}$. Evaluate $g_i=b_i\oplus b_{i+1}$ on both:

| $i$ | $g_i(b)$ | $g_i(b+1)$ |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $i<k-1$ | $1\oplus1=0$ | $0\oplus0=0$ |
| $i=k-1$ | $1\oplus0=1$ | $0\oplus1=1$ |
| $i=k$ | $0\oplus x=x$ | $1\oplus x=\bar{x}$ |
| $i>k$ | unchanged | unchanged |

Only $g_k$ differs. $\square$

**Why this is mandatory across a clock domain.** From [[Introduction to Flip-Flops & Latches]], a receiving flop whose setup/hold window is violated resolves to *either* value after some metastability-resolution time — and each bit of a bus resolves **independently**. If $m$ bits change at the same source edge, the destination can latch any of the $2^m$ mixtures of old and new. Sampling a binary pointer mid-transition $0111\to1000$ can therefore yield $1111$ or $0000$: values that are not merely late, but were never counts at all. An async FIFO comparing such a pointer will declare *full* when it is empty.

With a Gray pointer exactly one bit is in flight, so the sampled word is either the old count or the new count — both legal, and the error is bounded at one count. That bound is what makes the FIFO flags **conservative**: a stale read pointer makes the write side see fewer free entries (asserts full early), a stale write pointer makes the read side see fewer valid entries (asserts empty early). Never the unsafe direction.

**Implementation.** Keep a binary counter and register the conversion:

$$b^{+}=b+1,\qquad g^{+}=b^{+}\oplus(b^{+}\gg1)$$

The XOR feeds the Gray register in parallel with the binary register, so it adds one XOR level to the *setup* path but nothing to the increment loop. Registering the Gray value (rather than combinationally converting the registered binary value) is what makes the pointer glitch-free at the crossing point — a combinational $b\oplus(b\gg1)$ would emit hazards from the binary register's own skew. This is the [[Block Memory]]-fronted async FIFO structure: the binary count addresses the RAM, the Gray count crosses.

> [!warning] Gray needs a power-of-two modulus
> Each Gray step flips exactly one bit, so the parity of the codeword alternates. A closed cycle of length $N$ therefore requires $N$ even. Odd-$N$ cyclic Gray codes do not exist. For an async FIFO of non-power-of-two depth this is the reason the pointer is sized to the next power of two and the depth handled elsewhere.

## LFSR counters

A linear-feedback shift register updates by $s^{+}=(s\ll1)\;|\;f$ with $f=\bigoplus_{i\in T}s_i$ (Fibonacci form), where the tap set $T$ is read off the characteristic polynomial $p(x)$ over $\mathrm{GF}(2)$. If $p$ is **primitive** of degree $n$, the state sequence has period $2^n-1$: every state except all-zero, exactly once.

The point is the next-state depth. A binary counter's is $O(n)$ or $O(\log n)$; an LFSR's is **one XOR**, independent of $n$ — and in the Galois arrangement (taps XORed into the shift path rather than gathered into one wide XOR) it is one XOR2, so

$$T_{\text{clk}}\ \ge\ t_{cq}+t_{\text{XOR2}}+t_{su}$$

for a counter of any width. That is within a gate of the flop's own toggle limit. For $n$ flops it also costs only $|T|-1$ XOR2 gates of logic, against $n$ AND/XOR pairs plus a carry chain.

What you give up is monotonicity: state $k$ is some scrambled $n$-bit value, not the integer $k$. So an LFSR is the right counter whenever you need to *count to $N$* but never need to *know the count*: timeouts, watchdogs, high-speed prescalers, refresh counters, memory-test address generation, BIST pattern generation. The terminal state is precomputed offline as $x^{N}\bmod p(x)$ applied to the seed and detected with one equality comparator — same cost as any other terminal-count compare.

> [!warning] All-zeros is a fixed point
> $f=\bigoplus_i 0=0$, so the zero state maps to itself and the counter dies silently. Guarantee a non-zero seed on reset, or convert to a **de Bruijn** counter by XNORing an all-zeros detect into the feedback, which splices the missing state into the cycle and gives the full period $2^n$ at the cost of one $(n-1)$-input NOR.

## Ring and Johnson counters

Both are shift registers with a wire for feedback and no arithmetic at all, so both run at the flop's toggle limit.

**Ring (one-hot).** $q^{+}=\text{rot}(q)$, seeded with a single 1. $N$ flops for $N$ states — hopeless density — but the decode of state $k$ *is* the flop output $q_k$: zero decode logic, zero decode delay, and structurally glitch-free. That is why one-hot is the default encoding for high-speed FSM state, round-robin arbiters, and multi-phase clock generation. Not self-starting: any state with a different number of ones rotates forever, so either reset it or make it self-correcting by driving $q_0^{+}=\overline{q_1+q_2+\dots+q_{N-1}}$, which forces the number of ones back to one.

**Johnson (twisted-ring, Möbius).** Shift, and feed back the **complement** of the bit that falls off:

$$q_{n-1}^{+}=\overline{q_0},\qquad q_i^{+}=q_{i+1}$$

Four bits give eight states: $0000,1000,1100,1110,1111,0111,0011,0001$ and back. So $2n$ states from $n$ flops — twice a ring's density — and exactly one bit changes per step, which means a Johnson counter **is** a Gray code and inherits the CDC safety of the previous section, restricted to $2n$ states. Each state is uniquely identified by two adjacent bits, so decoding costs one 2-input gate per state, glitch-free. A 2-bit Johnson counter is $00,01,11,10$ — quadrature clock phases, which is exactly why it shows up in clock generators and in the phase generators of switched-capacitor circuits. Like the ring it is not self-starting: the $2^n-2n$ unused states form disjoint cycles and need correction logic or a reset.

## FPGA mapping

The conclusion of [[Adders]] transfers wholesale: **do not build a lookahead incrementer on an FPGA.** Every modern fabric has a dedicated carry chain (Xilinx `CARRY4`/`CARRY8`, Intel ALM carry) whose per-bit propagation delay is far below one LUT delay and whose routing is hardwired. The half-adder chain of the first section maps one bit per carry stage: the LUT computes the toggle condition, the carry cell ANDs, the flop is the one already sitting in the slice. A prefix tree abandons that hard logic for general LUTs and general routing and loses for any width you would plausibly build.

Specific wins available on fabric ([[FPGAs]], [[Lookup-Tables]]):

- **The enable and reset are free.** Every slice flop has a `CE` port and an `SR` port. `EN` costs nothing, and a *synchronous* clear costs nothing — which makes the compare-and-clear modulo-$N$ counter cheaper on FPGA than the general argument above suggests. Asynchronous clear is also free at the flop but couples the counter to the global reset network; prefer synchronous.
- **The LUT absorbs the control logic.** A 6-LUT computing $t_i$ can take five lower state bits plus the enable, or fold the load mux and the up/down XOR into the same lookup, so `LD`, `UP`, and `EN` frequently cost zero extra levels rather than one mux delay each.
- **Ring and Johnson counters need no flops at all.** An `SRL16`/`SRL32` turns a single LUT into a 16- or 32-deep addressable shift register. A ring or Johnson counter of length $\le32$ fits in one LUT with its feedback, which is the cheapest sequencer on the die.
- **LFSRs likewise.** Shift path in an SRL, feedback in one LUT — an $n$-bit LFSR counter is a couple of LUTs regardless of $n$, running at the fabric's maximum toggle rate.
- **Wide accumulating counters belong in a DSP block.** If the increment is a constant other than 1, or if the counter is wider than ~48 bits, the DSP's hard accumulator beats fabric logic on both speed and area — see [[DSP Blocks]].

| Gray (binary core + registered conversion) | increment path $+\,t_{\text{XOR}}$ on setup only | $2n$ | increment $+1$ XOR | one bit changes — glitch-free | $2n$ flops at $f$ | **yes** |
| LFSR | $t_{cq}+t_{\text{XOR2}}+t_{su}$, independent of $n$ | $n$ | 1 | non-monotone; equality decode only | $n$ flops, ~half toggle | no |
| Ring (one-hot) | $t_{cq}+t_{su}$ | $N$ for $N$ states | 0 | free, glitch-free decode | 2 flops toggle per cycle | no (2 bits change) |
| Johnson | $t_{cq}+t_{\text{INV}}+t_{su}$ | $n$ for $2n$ states | 1 inverter | 2-gate, glitch-free decode | 1 flop toggles per cycle | **yes**, $2n$ states |

Pick the **synchronous carry-chain counter** by default — on FPGA it stays fastest to surprising widths, and on ASIC switch to the prefix or two-stage form only when static timing says the chain is the critical path. Pick a **ripple counter** only for a pure $\div2^n$ output whose intermediate bits nobody looks at, and take the clock-power win. Pick **Gray** the moment a count leaves its clock domain — there is no cheaper way to be correct, and the cost is one XOR level and one extra register. Pick an **LFSR** when the count is a deadline rather than a quantity and $f$ is the binding constraint. Pick **ring or Johnson** when the decoded output matters more than the flop count: a few states, decoded every cycle, glitch-free.

## Worked example

### 4-bit synchronous up-counter

Substituting $n=4$ into $q_i^{+}=q_i\oplus\big(\text{EN}\prod_{j<i}q_j\big)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
q_0^{+}&=q_0\oplus\text{EN} & t_0&=\text{EN}\\
q_1^{+}&=q_1\oplus(\text{EN}\,q_0) & t_1&=t_0\,q_0\\
q_2^{+}&=q_2\oplus(\text{EN}\,q_0q_1) & t_2&=t_1\,q_1\\
q_3^{+}&=q_3\oplus(\text{EN}\,q_0q_1q_2) & t_3&=t_2\,q_2\\
\text{TC}&=\text{EN}\,q_0q_1q_2q_3 & &=t_3\,q_3
\end{aligned}
$$

The right column is the point: each toggle term is the previous one ANDed with one state bit — four AND2 and four XOR2 total, and the chain is literally the carry chain. Tracing with $\text{EN}=1$, writing $Q=q_3q_2q_1q_0$ and the toggle vector $T=t_3t_2t_1t_0$:

| $Q$ | $q_0$ | $q_1$ | $q_2$ | $T$ | $Q^{+}$ | TC |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $0110$ | 0 | 1 | 1 | $0001$ | $0111$ | 0 |
| $0111$ | 1 | 1 | 1 | $1111$ | $1000$ | 0 |
| $1000$ | 0 | 0 | 0 | $0001$ | $1001$ | 0 |
| $1111$ | 1 | 1 | 1 | $1111$ | $0000$ | **1** |

$0111\to1000$ flips all four bits at once — the transition that produces the ripple counter's glitch and the reason a binary pointer cannot cross a clock domain. The last row is the wrap: $Q^{+}=Q+1 \bmod 2^4$, exact, **$0$ ulp** — the carry-out is not lost, it is the terminal count.

### 4-bit LFSR, $p(x)=x^4+x+1$

Primitive of degree 4, taps at $s_3$ and $s_0$, so $f=s_3\oplus s_0$ and $s^{+}=(s_2\,s_1\,s_0\,f)$. Seeded at $1000$:

| step | $s$ | $s_3\oplus s_0$ | step | $s$ | $s_3\oplus s_0$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | $1000$ | $1\oplus0=1$ | 8 | $0101$ | $0\oplus1=1$ |
| 1 | $0001$ | $0\oplus1=1$ | 9 | $1011$ | $1\oplus1=0$ |
| 2 | $0011$ | $0\oplus1=1$ | 10 | $0110$ | $0\oplus0=0$ |
| 3 | $0111$ | $0\oplus1=1$ | 11 | $1100$ | $1\oplus0=1$ |
| 4 | $1111$ | $1\oplus1=0$ | 12 | $1001$ | $1\oplus1=0$ |
| 5 | $1110$ | $1\oplus0=1$ | 13 | $0010$ | $0\oplus0=0$ |
| 6 | $1101$ | $1\oplus1=0$ | 14 | $0100$ | $0\oplus0=0$ |
| 7 | $1010$ | $1\oplus0=1$ | 15 | $1000$ | — wraps |

Fifteen distinct states — sorted, they are exactly $0001$ through $1111$, confirming the period $2^4-1=15$ and that $0000$ is excluded. Read the sequence as integers ($8,1,3,7,15,14,13,10,5,11,6,12,9,2,4$) and it is plainly non-monotone: to "count 10 cycles" you compare against $s=6$ from step 10, not against a numeral. The count itself is exact — $0$ ulp — it is simply expressed in a permuted alphabet.
